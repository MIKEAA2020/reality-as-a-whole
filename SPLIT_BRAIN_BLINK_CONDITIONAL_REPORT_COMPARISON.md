# Two split-brain blink studies: what is being conditioned on?

## Bottom line

**Later primary-source update:** the user supplied Ptito's complete published 2009 paper and a retry retrieved both chunks of the full 2004 paper's text. The [complete-results audit](SPLIT_BRAIN_BLINK_2004_2009_COMPLETE_RESULTS_AUDIT.md) reports both studies' actual results, corrects the earlier patient timing, and notes that the 2004 methods require T2 to be at a different location even on same-field trials. The 2004 figure itself and both sets of raw trials remain uninspected. Interpret the original source-access record below as historical.

Giesbrecht and Kingstone's 2004 split-brain study reported a blink for both same-field and cross-field target presentations and a more severe blink for second targets presented to the right hemisphere. An exposed results excerpt adds a consequential detail: its **second-target accuracy is conditional on correctly identifying the first target**, and the patient's first-target accuracy itself differed by visual field. This makes the conditional outcome informative about a specified behavioral subset, but it is not a clean comparison of identical trial populations or a direct assay of experienced unity.

Ptito et al.'s 2009 study tested a different patient and a modified, four-stream digit-**counting** task. Its report of a left-hemisphere advantage is a related behavioral extension, not an exact replication of an identical target-identification estimand. The later follow-up recovered both complete results sections, but no new raw data or patient reports.

## 2. What the earlier authors reported

Giesbrecht and Kingstone, *Brain and Cognition* 55, 303–306, DOI [10.1016/j.bandc.2004.02.026](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.bandc.2004.02.026), tested one right-handed, 47-year-old patient, JW, who had undergone callosotomy for intractable epilepsy, and six right-handed college-age controls. The preview does not establish the detailed extent of JW's section or all remaining anatomical pathways. His prior case history is referenced, not examined here. This patient is not simply a hemisphere or a response mechanism.

The introduction describes two sequential, individually masked target **letters**, appearing in the same or different visual fields. The abstract reports evidence of a blink in both presentation arrangements, more severe when T2 was presented to the right hemisphere (i.e., ordinarily the left visual field under maintained fixation). It says this is consistent with a **critical but not unique** right-hemisphere role in capacity-limited processing, rather than proving that a single right-hemisphere processor exclusively causes all blinks. The [later full-text reading](SPLIT_BRAIN_BLINK_2004_2009_COMPLETE_RESULTS_AUDIT.md) adds lag values, response mode and reported marginal results; detailed individual gaze controls and a visual reading of the figure remain unavailable. A reported difference is not automatically an estimated duration or equivalence.

The patient-data excerpt says JW completed two sessions on separate days, with analyses based on means of **12 experimental blocks across those sessions**. Collapsed across lag, mean T1 identification was **76.1% in the left visual field versus 93.9% in the right visual field**, a **17.8 percentage-point** difference; the paper reports `t(11)=5.83, p<.002`. These are the authors' first-target means and block-based test, not independent patient observations, not T2 blink estimates, and not a population-level estimate. Estimates of T2 accuracy were based on trials with a **correct T1 response**. The excerpt originally cut off before those results; their reported lag and T2-field means and interaction now appear in the [complete-results audit](SPLIT_BRAIN_BLINK_2004_2009_COMPLETE_RESULTS_AUDIT.md), but the full cell-wise contingency table remains unavailable.

## 3. Why conditioning matters, without erasing the observation

Let `A` denote correct identification of T1, `B` correct identification of T2, `h` a target-field arrangement and `l` a lag. The exposed excerpt identifies the relevant kind of T2 measure as `P(B | A, h, l)`. That can legitimately answer: *among trials on which the first target was correctly reported in this condition, how often was the second correctly reported?* A lag-dependent reduction in this conditional quantity is evidence about performance on that task. It is not the same estimand as `P(A and B | h,l)` or `P(B | h,l)`, nor automatically a measure of conscious access.

A mathematical identity shows the missing information: `P(A and B | h,l) = P(A | h,l) × P(B | A,h,l)`. The 2004 full text reports lag-collapsed first-target means and marginal conditional T2 values, but not all arrangement-by-lag quantities needed to compute joint accuracy by cell; the figure itself was not visually inspected. The reported field difference in collapsed T1 accuracy makes selection worth examining: conditioning can retain different fractions and kinds of trials across fields. Differences in the selected trials' difficulty, attention, masking, guessing, order errors, and response access are possible explanations or contributors; **none has been demonstrated as the cause** of the published T2 asymmetry. Even equal selection rates would not by themselves ensure comparable selected trials.

Consequently, a contrast in conditional accuracies does not by itself establish what would happen if the very same trials or underlying first-target encoding states were held fixed across fields. Conversely, refusing to interpret any conditioned outcome would be too strong: it is an explicitly defined, often useful report-performance outcome. The additional analysis needed is the full factorial table of T1 correctness, T2 correctness and their joint and marginal rates at each actual lag and field configuration, with false alarms, response categories, trial counts and uncertainty. Repeated blocks should be analyzed as repeated observations from one person, not as 12 independent patients; temporal dependence and sampling choices require checking in the full methods.

## 4. Comparison to Ptito: a targeted extension, not the same measurement

| Feature | Giesbrecht & Kingstone, 2004 | Ptito et al., 2009 |
|---|---|---|
| Patient and controls | One patient; six controls, per accessible preview. | One **different** patient and 22 controls, per published introduction, abstract and accessible thesis manuscript. Patient difference is explicitly stated by Ptito et al.; no inference from initials is needed. |
| Targets and response | Two masked target letters; T2 identification conditional on correct T1. JW pointed to listed letters and the examiner entered them; controls typed. | Two digits embedded among letters in four simultaneous streams; participants reported hemifield counts. The main within/across contrast uses correct paired counts, with lag 8 minus lag 2 as plotted blink magnitude. |
| Spatial contrast | Same versus different visual fields, with four possible locations; the full methods state T2 occurred at a different location from T1 even for same-field trials. | Different-stream targets within one field are compared with targets across fields; same-stream trials are distinct. Ptito's attribution of a same/different-stream confound to the 2004 study conflicts with its own different-location method and requires caution. |
| Published broad findings | Blink reported in both arrangements; more severe for T2 directed to right hemisphere. | Comparable blink reported within and across fields, with no significant variation; a left-hemisphere processing advantage reported only for the patient. These authors also report different overall accuracy across arrangements. |
| What cannot yet be equated | Field-conditional target identification, timing, response access and blink contrast in JW. | Joint count response, different patient timing (233 ms SOA), lag contrast, target asymmetry and patient-specific uncertainty in M.L. |

The direction of asymmetry is related, and Ptito et al. explicitly framed their study as asking whether a different patient showed a related left-hemisphere advantage. But matching the verbal phrase “left-hemisphere advantage” does not show identical effects, mechanisms or independent within/across equivalence. Ptito's matched-stream comparison addresses a spatial-layout issue in their own task; it does not establish that the 2004 same-field targets were at one location or repair the conditional-selection issue in the letter task. Patient diversity, different operations, target types, output affordances and designs remain possible sources of variation.

## 5. From selective report to phenomenology

Both results are relevant to the causal story about temporal selection: cross-field lag effects constrain the idea that entirely independent hemispheric processes cannot interact under these task conditions. But a shared output, alerting, memory, attentional control or other residual pathway can in principle generate some cross-field dependence without proving a unique shared capacity store. The excerpts do not establish which alternative is correct. The time sequence of presenting and reporting targets also does not establish that both particular contents are phenomenally conjoint in one moment.

This is **not** a reduction of Lopez's position to a resource pool. Her argument connects an *experienced* ordering of focus and periphery to phenomenal unity; that premise, if established and accepted as sufficient, is more than common processing interference. These tasks do not establish that antecedent for the relevant contents or decide whether it yields one subject, overlapping fields, or other arrangements. Ordinary first-person reports of unity remain meaningful independent evidence even though they do not verify a particular same-trial conjunction or enumerate inaccessible fields.

The existing four-spin `Jlocal` and `Jglobal` targets remain unclassified: there is no justified neural-to-spin map or calibrated same-window local/global conjunction report. Phenomenal **quality** is a further issue: digit identification, count accuracy and attentional centrality do not calibrate the candidate interaction mismatch `q` or show that it constitutes unpleasantness. No experiential absence follows from a wrong or missing response. C3, B4–B5/F2, B9/F3 and B10 remain open.

## 6. Next evidence gate

The complete final text of both studies is now available and analyzed in the [follow-up](SPLIT_BRAIN_BLINK_2004_2009_COMPLETE_RESULTS_AUDIT.md). Remaining useful materials include any ethically shareable de-identified condition tables and coding: T1-only, T2-only and joint correctness by target locations and lag, false positives and serial-order errors, fixation monitoring, per-cell counts, exclusions and uncertainty. A visual copy of the 2004 figure could further clarify the plotted cells. No author or librarian request has been sent.

A prospective noninvasive follow-up, if participants consent and the design is ethically approved, could counterbalance field and lag, manipulate T1 demand and provide carefully matched T2-only and masking controls. Record both outcomes and confidence independently where possible; compare conditional, marginal and joint accuracies rather than optimizing one after seeing the result. Prespecify uncertainty and any equivalence margin, retain participant-specific findings and monitor fixation, fatigue and response access. For the further unity question, separately validate time-anchored questions about experiential centrality and same-window conjunction, including memory and nonresponse controls; neither conditioning nor behavioral interference substitutes for that validation. Even successful reporting would still require a separately justified physical mapping before testing the toy selector.
