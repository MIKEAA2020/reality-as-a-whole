# First-personal givenness and the individuation of fields

## Decision

First-person experience gives the project an indispensable positive starting point: a qualitative occurrence can be lived, not merely described from outside. But “first-personal,” “self-aware,” “experienced as mine,” and “one numerically individuated subject” are not interchangeable claims. Reading Zahavi's reply to Guillot and Barbieri's challenge exposes a specific debt in our own **field-first** proposal: identifying a thin subject with a first-personal field is a substantive identity proposal, not a derivation of the existence or count of fields, and it does not automatically explain a persisting person.

There is a concrete consequence for the field-first identity. If every distinct local-window field token is itself a subject, then two distinct nested-window tokens cannot also be **the same numerical thin subject**. Treating every analysis window as a new field risks multiplying subjects by changing the description. A disciplined alternative distinguishes actual subject-field occurrences from their window-relative descriptions, but it must still justify the actual fields and their scope. This is a correction to how the candidate must be formulated, not an empirical discovery of subjects or a proof of temporal ontology.

## 2. What the terms do—and do not—say

Guillot distinguishes three notions, using her own technical vocabulary:

| Question | Guillot's term | Do not silently equate it with |
|---|---|---|
| Is the experience itself present to its subject in the relevant special experiential way? | **for-me-ness** | An explicit thought about a self, or an additional inner observer. |
| Is the subject experientially aware of herself? | **me-ness** | Mere first-person access to an experience, without an argument connecting them. |
| Is the experience phenomenally presented as belonging to that subject? | **mineness** | The bare fact that the experience occurs, or mere awareness of it. |

Guillot argues that these notions do not imply one another simply by conceptual analysis; in particular, for-me-ness alone does not establish the other two. She acknowledges that certain identity-based minimalist self theories might support an implication through additional premises. Her clinical interpretations are explicitly tentative, and she thinks all three may normally co-occur. They do not show that experience is an illusion.

Zahavi replies that his own use of *mineness* often means first-personal givenness, **not** Guillot's more demanding phenomenal awareness of a possessive relation. He agrees that simple awareness of an experience need not entail objectifying oneself or thematically experiencing the relation “this is mine.” His thin self is not a second object or an added self-quale; it is associated with the first-personal mode of experience. He resists Guillot's criticism that the connection is achieved by an unargued semantic slide and points to earlier theoretical arguments.

This matters for fair comparison. It would misread Zahavi to refute his claim simply by finding an experience without a **reflective judgment** “this is mine,” or by finding no extra feeling of ownership. Conversely, it would misread Guillot to say she denies real first-person experience. Nor does the claim that an episode is perspectivally given establish, by terminology alone, a richer claim that the subject explicitly or phenomenally apprehends itself as owner.

Zahavi's own footnote 3 is especially important for this project: his experiential self is **not identical to any one specific experience**, and can be shared across changing experiences. Our proposed identity of a local thin subject with a **field-occurrence** is therefore a distinct, more explicitly token-identifying hypothesis; it should not be attributed to him. His account has a diachronic ambition that our local identity has not discharged.

## 3. A real argument, and the remaining inference

Zahavi's reply identifies a theoretical argument, not just a report that reflection finds self-awareness. In outline, he treats reflective self-reference as unable to identify an object as oneself without a prior non-objectifying self-acquaintance: otherwise identifying a candidate as “me” apparently requires knowing something about oneself first, which threatens a regress or circularity. He also invokes the asymmetry between living one's joy and observing another's joy, as well as the need for reflective self-ascription to be answerable to experience. These are substantive motivations for pre-reflective first-personal givenness.

They are not, without further defense, a complete proof of the proposed ontology. The argument relies on contested premises about recognition, non-objectifying acquaintance, the nature of reflection and the explanatory significance of alternatives; a representationalist or other rival can contest them without denying experience. In particular, even if some prior acquaintance is needed for an occurrence of self-reflection, the stronger universal conclusion that **every** conscious occurrence must exhibit the proposed structure needs a separate argument covering experiences that are not being reflected on. This is a logical scope distinction, not an attribution to Zahavi of a simple invalid inference: his earlier, unexamined arguments may attempt to supply that general step.

First-person evidence remains genuine even if a universal thesis is disputed. A lived contrast is not reducible to a person's sentence about it; a report may be sincere and informative without being infallible about its causes, ownership or boundaries. Experience's occurring can be warranted even where a richer explicit self-concept, felt familiarity, or a claim about a persistent owner is uncertain.

## 4. The explanatory-priority challenge to field-first

Barbieri distinguishes consciousness predicated of a **state** from consciousness predicated of a **creature/subject**. His PSC says, roughly, that the subject is phenomenally conscious *in virtue of* its state being conscious; SUBJ says that a state is conscious in virtue of being phenomenally given to the subject. His further, contested reading of givenness makes it a subject's phenomenal awareness of the state. If the latter itself presupposes the relevant conscious subject, the two purported explanatory directions can form a circle. This is an objection to a package of explanatory commitments, not to the mere fact that state and subject descriptions are interdependent.

Barbieri considers two responses. A phenomenal state-self-awareness theorist can identify a thin subject with the experience, but he argues this makes PSC uninteresting rather than explaining a distinct creature property. A self-representationalist can invoke nonphenomenal self-representation, but he argues that it does not supply state consciousness in the specific sense PSC requires, and does not avoid his intermediate explanatory dependence. These are his arguments, not established refutations of all field-first, higher-order or representational identity theories.

For our candidate, distinguish three possible moves:

1. **State-priority:** an independently individuated conscious state explains a distinct subject's consciousness. This owes an ownership/subject relation and faces Barbieri's concern if the state's consciousness was already explained by that subject's phenomenal awareness.
2. **Subject-priority:** a conscious subject's awareness explains the state's first-personal character. This avoids claiming to derive the subject from that state, but must explain why this subject exists and is conscious without relying on the same state-fact in a vicious explanatory circle.
3. **Field identity:** posit an actual, intrinsically first-personal **field-occurrence** `f` and identify the thin subject with `f`; do not represent subject and field as two independently existing items in a two-step grounding chain. This rejects the specific claim that an already independent state first grounds an independently characterized creature. It can avoid that particular cycle, but starts with a subject-like phenomenal occurrence and does not explain why it exists or why it is the relevant occurrence.

Identity of two *bearers* is not by itself identity of every property or an explanatory priority between their descriptions. Thus `subject=f` alone does not settle whether the field's qualitative character, its phenomenal unity and its subject-character are the same fact, distinct mutually dependent facts, or grounded asymmetrically. Nor can the one-level theorist dismiss Barbieri's category objection by simply calling a mental state a subject; the relevant ontology and use of “conscious of” must be defended. A theorist might regard self-manifestation as a **mode of occurrence**, not an additional relation between two conscious objects, but then must explain its phenomenal content and why it amounts to a subject rather than merely assert a reflexive arrow.

The constructive research decision is modest: retain field identity as a candidate **identity**, not as a proof from subject-free ingredients, and do not count the absence of a second observer as full explanation. It preserves the lived datum while exposing the price. Creature-first and neutral/physical identity proposals remain serious competitors; no outcome here decides which gives the best complete account.

## 5. A precise window-individuation fork

Suppose a theory makes all three claims about a case:

1. A field-occurrence `f_W` spans a local window `W`, and a distinct field-occurrence `f_V` is assigned to a proper subwindow `V⊂W`: `f_V ≠ f_W`.
2. The thin subject of **every** such field occurrence is that very occurrence: `subject(f)=f`.
3. The subject assigned to `V` is numerically identical to the subject assigned to `W`: `subject(f_V)=subject(f_W)`.

Substitution of identity in (2) and (3) gives `f_V=f_W`, contradicting (1). So these three commitments cannot all hold. This is elementary identity logic. It does not show that actual experiences have nested field-tokens, that subjects persist, or that a different account of temporal experience is incoherent. Nor does it rule out a persistent person associated with two distinct thin subjects: that would be a **different identity relation**, not the one in premise 3.

In particular, it is not contradictory to call the subject of a long field and the subject of a subepisode “the same person” while treating the thin field-subjects as distinct. The error would be to state both that these are distinct fields and that they are literally the *same thin field-subject*, without further qualification. Likewise, saying that the subject “continues” across episodes may express causal or experiential lineage rather than strict numerical identity; those notions must not be silently interchanged.

### A window-covariant candidate, not yet a solution

A promising refinement is to let the theory posit actual field-occurrences `f` with an intrinsically fixed scope, and treat `view(f,V)` as a **description** of that occurrence relative to an admissible local window `V`, rather than automatically creating another field and subject for every window. Then, for the same actual occurrence, changing `V` need not change the subject count. Where restriction operations are meaningful, their consistency must be specified; for example a nested-view operation might satisfy `view(view(f,W),V)=view(f,V)` for `V⊂W`.

That equation is a proposed representation rule, not a phenomenal law. Holistic character can depend on context: restricting a description must not pretend that the character of a part is unchanged when its surrounding field changes. A context-preserving view could carry the larger field as contextual information, and some theories may deny that such restrictions are well-defined at all. The point is to distinguish **redescribing one field** from positing a numerically distinct field, not to impose an atomistic decomposition.

The proposal still owes (i) an independently motivated criterion of actual field-occurrence and its scope; (ii) reasons to count one actual field rather than overlapping, nested or duplicated ones; (iii) a separately specified account of lineage or persisting subjects where intended; and (iv) a physical and qualitative realization law. Simply declaring one window “canonical” does not justify it. Nor can unrestricted maximality under content inclusion recover local subjects in an encompassing field, as [the articulated self-presence analysis](ARTICULATED_SELF_PRESENCE.md) already shows under its stated assumptions. A local window is a relation within the modeled process, not an external universal clock.

## 6. Applying the fork to the current framework

At `t=1/4`, the L and C proposals select different physical scopes on the same four-spin sector. Even if we add the field-first identity, L's two pair selections and C's whole selection become fields **only by additional physical-to-phenomenal premises**. A label attached to a pair does not establish first-personal givenness. Conversely, direct evidence of a lived contrast remains evidence even before the physical constitution rule is known.

The window refinement clarifies how to handle descriptions *after* fields and their scopes have been established. It does not choose L over C, derive the `q`-to-unpleasantness law, settle whether experience tokens can be shared, or identify the observed human brain with four spins. It also does not show that an experiential ground necessarily exists or that a self-sufficient whole is itself a universal experiencer. The [joint-action comparison](ACTION_UNITY_JOINT_CONTROL_VERSUS_LIVED_PERSPECTIVE.md) independently explains why even two-input control of a single response is not by itself an assay of the disputed conjoint field.

## 7. Next substantive obligation

A successful candidate should present, in one place, (1) a defended account of the locally present first-personal character, without requiring an explicit self-judgment; (2) an independently motivated individuation and scope law that makes a physical realization **this** field rather than another field or mere redescription; (3) a quality law anchored in warranted first-person contrasts; and (4) explicit persistence, fission and representation rules. It must compare its primitives and explanatory costs against subject-first, field-first and neutral-ground accounts, including Barbieri's priority argument and Zahavi's theoretical motivation.

The argument here is an identity constraint on a particular field-first package, not a claim that that package is true or false. C3 remains **full structural and experiential intelligibility**, jointly with C1–C7. B4–B5/F2, B9/F3 and B10 remain open; no experiential bridge, external replay or substantive battery pass is claimed.
