# Split-brain research: pooled timing, visual rivalry, and what integration tests show

Research update, 7 November 2026. This extends the [public-data and network-evidence update](SPLIT_BRAIN_PUBLIC_DATA_AND_NETWORK_EVIDENCE.md). It is a literature search and bounded model check, not a proof that consciousness is one or many.

## Executive conclusion

The evidence is richer than a handful of modern cases: a published meta-analysis pooled measurements from **38 distinct patients** in one timing paradigm, while separately collating a partially overlapping set of 22 in a second. Visual-rivalry studies also show both locally dissociable and partially coordinated reports. These results constrain simplistic claims that all interhemispheric processing vanishes after surgery, but they equally fail to show that a single subject survives or that two subjects necessarily emerge.

A useful methodological distinction is **detection → response speed → content combination → experiential unity**. Evidence at one level need not establish the next. Existing reaction-time literature already provides a race-model inequality and discusses its crucial assumptions; the small models below adapt that prior work rather than claiming to discover it.

## 1. Pooled callosotomy evidence and patient overlap

[Westerhausen, *Neuropsychology Review* (online 2022, issue 2023)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10769931/) systematically collected case-level results from literature through 7 March 2022. It reports:

| Paradigm | Studies | Distinct patient cases | Published observations |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Poffenberger crossed–uncrossed difference (CUD) | 22 | 38 | 116 |
| Bilateral redundant-target gain | 10 | 22 | 103 |

These observations are **not** 116 or 103 independent patients: cases reappear across tasks and publications. The two patient sets may overlap and should not be added. The meta-analysis used mixed models with individual and experiment random effects; it was not preregistered and authors note limitations from heterogeneous protocols and gain definitions.

In the Poffenberger task, a brief target appears in one visual field and a hand responds. **CUD = mean crossed response time minus mean uncrossed response time.** Estimated means were 60.6 ms (95% CI 45.3–75.9) after commissurotomy, 43.5 ms (26.7–60.2) after complete callosotomy, and 8.8 ms (1.1–16.6) after partial anterior–medial callosotomy. These are group-model estimates of a behavioral contrast, not precise measurements of anatomical conduction or conscious integration. Anatomy categories, age, clinical history, experimental conditions and repeated-case selection matter.

In the redundant-target paradigm, participants respond to one target versus targets on both sides. The study's minimum-unilateral-reference estimates of bilateral gain were 42.8 ms (27.1–58.4) in the complete group and 30.8 ms (16.8–44.7) in the partial group. Different references give different gains; these numbers are not mutual information, bandwidth or subject count. The paper reports that the paradigms likely probe distinct facets of callosal function.

### Public-data provenance and an illustrative repeated row

The paper points to an [OSF project](https://osf.io/y46bv/) with annotated extracted tables and R scripts. I inspected the public OSF API directory listing and the beginning of the rendered CSVs, **not** the original papers' raw reaction-time trials or an executed R analysis. The [CUD case CSV](https://osf.io/download/e3fmv/) and [redundancy-gain case CSV](https://osf.io/download/bzxgw/) are aggregate case/experiment extractions. OSF's metadata reports SHA-256 `ef786fec3cbb2cd558cb46bde1cb3df5c510f3630156829abc398ad6715a4c0b` and `e44a251756c6666d176f76b27c9139094ad952a36f7c6eae318053f9f37c78c9`, respectively; **I did not obtain and hash the original byte files**, so these are repository metadata, not independently verified hashes. The project lists no license. I have not redistributed its tables.

For example, the CSV contains three rows for patient JW in Corballis 1998 experiment 1, with the same reported CUD 71 ms but redundancy gain 56.8 ms using the average-unilateral reference and 21.5 ms using each of the minimum and ipsilateral references. These are three calculated summaries of the same case/experiment, not three subjects or three replications. The average-reference row reports 417.25 ms unilateral and 360.5 ms bilateral: their difference is 56.75 ms, consistent with the rounded 56.8 ms. This arithmetic illustrates the dependence on the chosen estimand; it does not independently validate the pooled mixed model or individual studies.

[Wolski and Asanowicz (2023)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S002839322300115X) provide an important counterweight: in **12 neurologically intact participants**, over 100,000 reaction times in total showed that spatial attention and location uncertainty modulate CUD. The authors found insufficient reliability for treating it as an individual-difference measure in their setting. Those observations do not disprove the case meta-analysis: the designs and populations differ. They do show why one should not translate a CUD directly into a fixed callosal or subcortical transit time.

## 2. Rivalry: dissociation and coordination can coexist

[O’Shea and Corballis (2003)](https://jov.arvojournals.org/article.aspx?articleid=2192518) investigated rivalry in JW and VP using separate lateralized trials and corresponding hand reports, with six intact-brain observers. JW was tested across two sessions; for VP the first experiment had only six observations, too few for the authors' statistical analysis. Rivalry-like alternation was reported with stimuli in either visual field, challenging particular models that require rivalry to alternate *between* hemispheres. This does not, by itself, show simultaneous independent experiential subjects: much of the measurement tested one hemifield at a time.

[O’Shea and Corballis (2005)](https://researchportal.scu.edu.au/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/Visual-grouping-on-binocular-rivalry-in-a-split-brain-observer/991012822045802368), accessible here as an abstract, reports that JW's two nearby rivalry patches synchronized when in the same hemifield but not when in opposite hemifields. That is evidence for condition-specific dissociation in **one** patient, not statistical proof of complete independence under every stimulus or definition of consciousness.

[Ritchie et al. (2013)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0042698913002137) tested VP and eight age-matched controls using central rival face/house stimuli and simultaneous left/right hand reports. For traditional whole-image rivalry, VP showed more reported coherent than non-coherent percepts, though less coherence than controls: reported synchrony ratio 0.507 versus control mean 0.824. Their split-image Diaz-Caneja condition instead favored non-synchronous percepts in both VP and controls. VP had five runs of 24 one-minute trials, while controls had one 24-trial set each. The task excluded mixed percepts, used different screens/software for VP and controls, and clear fixation videos were available for only 41% of VP's trials. Crucially, the paper says **VP had spared fibers in the genu and anterior commissure**, so she is not a clean example of complete anatomical commissural removal. Central stimuli may also engage bilateral overlap near the vertical meridian. These observations do not establish the route of remaining synchrony.

The 2003 VP and JW are the same named patients used in the respective 2013 and 2005 studies, so the three papers must not be treated as four independent cases. The experiments differ in stimuli, anatomy and measurements; their results are informative constraints, not a clean contradiction. Shared eye-based stimulus structure, sensory input, motor/report coupling and spared connections all deserve consideration. Synchrony is not by itself co-consciousness; disagreement between two reports is not by itself proof of two co-consciousness-excluding subjects.

## 3. What redundant-target speed can and cannot tell us

[Miller's race-model inequality](https://doi.org/10.1016/0010-0285(82)90010-X) and [Gondan and Minakata's tutorial](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/s13414-015-1018-y) are established prior art. Let `F_L(t)`, `F_R(t)` and `F_LR(t)` be observable response-time cumulative distributions. Under a first-finisher race with the required **context invariance** and suitable assumptions connecting decision and motor times:

`F_LR(t) ≤ min(1, F_L(t) + F_R(t))`.

Statistical independence of the two latent channels is **not** required for this upper bound. If the empirical inequality is reliably violated, at least one assumption of that constrained model fails. It does not uniquely establish a particular pooling mechanism, anatomical route or experience. A context-dependent race can violate the bound too; see also [Otto et al. 2019](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-019-39924-6). Nonviolation does not prove the race model. The literature reports large gains and race-bound violations in split-brain cases, but a mean-gain meta-analysis alone does not replay or establish distributional violations for every included case; those require original trial distributions and design-specific analysis.

### Exact, fictional diagnostic examples

[The independent verifier](verification/race_context_and_joint_content_gate.py) and its [receipt](verification/race_context_and_joint_content_gate.json) use arbitrary times 1 and 2; these numbers are **not patient data**.

1. **Gain without information pooling.** Let each single-target channel finish at time 1 with probability 1/4 and at 2 otherwise. On a bilateral trial suppose the latent pair is `(1,2)`, `(2,1)`, `(2,2)` with probabilities 1/4, 1/4, 1/2. Both channel marginals stay unchanged. The first-finisher response is fast with probability 1/2, versus 1/4 in a single-target trial; mean RT improves by 1/4 time unit. This is an ordinary race with negatively dependent finishing times. Its fast probability exceeds the **independent-race** prediction 7/16 but exactly meets the general Miller bound 1/2. Calling 7/16 the general bound would falsely reject this race.
2. **Violation without uniquely identified pooling.** Keep the single-target distributions. Now let the bilateral latent pairs be `(1,2)`, `(2,1)`, `(2,2)` with probabilities 2/5, 2/5, 1/5. The bilateral fast probability is 4/5, exceeding the single-trial bound 1/2 by 3/10. However each bilateral channel's fast probability is 2/5 rather than the single-trial 1/4: context invariance is false. A separately stipulated pooled-response model can give exactly the same three observable RT distributions: in bilateral trials, binary channel activations are jointly `(1,1)` with probability 4/5 and `(0,0)` otherwise, and their sum reaches the fast-response threshold only in the former case. These toy mechanisms are not asserted to be physiologically realized; the example demonstrates the **logical ambiguity** already discussed in the literature.
3. **Speed does not imply joint content.** Give bilateral targets independent uniform binary attributes X and Y, but let the detection response and response time in either toy model ignore those attributes. The observable `condition, button, time` has identical distributions in the two models and contains no information about `X XOR Y`: the best parity prediction from it is 1/2. This is a stipulated diagnostic counterexample, not an estimate of patient ability. A real experiment must measure the attribute-dependent transcript and the appropriate one-field strategy, not just faster detection.

These examples do not imply there is no neural interaction in real redundant-target experiments. In the context-dependent example, the change of latent distributions itself needs explanation and could reflect common drive, a physical interaction or some other change. The point is that the observed speed distribution alone does not locate it or settle phenomenal unity.

## 4. A recent EEG lead, with lower evidential weight

[Skinner et al., 2026](https://jocnf.pubpub.org/pub/yxy7973d/release/1) is a **conference poster abstract**, not a full independent trial-level validation. It describes clinical EEG from LJ (one pre- and three post-complete recordings), EM (one pre- and one post-partial), and BT (one post-partial), with eyes-open and eyes-closed conditions. The authors compare intra- and interhemispheric squared electrode correlations and report that the first post-complete LJ recording did not show a significant difference between those classes. They suggest dependence on completeness and time. These are the same named patients involved in the 2025 studies; different modality and some before/after observations add context, not independent patient replication. The abstract alone is insufficient to assess preprocessing, autocorrelation, common sources/volume conduction, dependence among electrode pairs sharing sensors, multiplicity or the reliability of longitudinal effects. A nonsignificant contrast does not establish equality. No EEG data or code were reanalyzed here.

## 5. Practical design consequence

For a new ethically approved study, retain the [previous proposal's](SPLIT_BRAIN_PUBLIC_DATA_AND_NETWORK_EVIDENCE.md) randomized, cue-controlled joint-content task, but run it **alongside** a carefully preregistered redundant-target control. Interleave single-left, single-right and bilateral detection trials, use calibrated timing, report complete distributions and censoring, choose a prespecified race-inequality inference with familywise error control, and explicitly test sensitivity to context and motor assumptions. Interleaving helps but does not establish context invariance. Separately use independently randomized stimulus identities and a response whose correct value depends nontrivially on both; preregister information-leak and one-field shortcut bounds. Keep individual outcomes and repeated visits clustered by patient. Poffenberger, redundancy gain and XOR-like content tasks are **different estimands**.

For rivalry, build on—not rediscover—the 2003–2013 paradigms: calibrate gaze and stimulus overlap, preserve and independently verify time-stamped reports, include mixed-percept and response-dependence controls, and compare within-hemifield and cross-hemifield stimulus geometry in the same cases. If feasible, include anatomically characterized partial and complete cases and fixed-sample equivalence margins. Even with all these improvements, functional content sharing, reportable experience and the metaphysical identity of an experiencer remain separate claims requiring independently justified bridges.

## 6. Constraint on the whole-and-subject framework

Any candidate subject-constitution rule should be assessed against the **joint pattern**, not a single favorite result: postoperative dissociations, residual simple response coordination, task- and patient-dependent content integration, and rivalry synchrony that changes with geometry and anatomy. A rule that declares every post-callosotomy connection absent conflicts with this functional evidence; a rule that identifies one subject merely because some coordination persists adds an unvalidated bridge. A simple anatomy-only or fixed-timing threshold must specify how it handles partial section and distinct task scales before it can be tested.

These findings do not choose between one agentive universal consciousness, a non-agentive experiential whole containing plural subjects, or a different account. In particular, the latter two targets must not be treated as equivalent. They also supply no derivation of necessary existence, physical laws or emergent spacetime. The concrete gain is a more demanding empirical benchmark for competing subject-boundary rules, not completion of the wider framework.
