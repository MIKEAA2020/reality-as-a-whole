# Shelat and Giesbrecht 2026: reward, lapses and attentional capture

## What this study contributes

Shelat and Giesbrecht, [*Value-driven attentional capture prevents commission errors in real time*](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13423-026-02930-7), *Psychonomic Bulletin & Review* 33, article 163 (2026), is an interesting **sustained-attention study in two student samples**, not the 2004 split-brain blink experiment. It bears on how learned salience can modify performance during likely lapses; it does not establish a cross-hemispheric common resource, a subject count, or the contents of a unified experience.

## Design and findings

Two independent groups of **50** university participants completed reward training and a visual-search sustained-attention test; the pilot of 12 was discarded. In training, one target color yielded five cents on 75% of correct trials, another was unrewarded, and a third color was absent. In the subsequent test participants repeatedly identified an orientation inside a shape singleton; one orientation was common, the other rare. The trained reward, trained unrewarded, novel, or neutral color appeared as a distractor on triggered rare trials. A trial had to be answered, rather than withheld; a frequent-response key on a rare trial counted as a commission error. Experiment 1 randomly assigned participants to same-day or one-week-separated phases; Experiment 2 was same-day.

A rare trial was triggered after a three-trial response-time window lay more than one participant-specific standard deviation above or below the cumulative mean, provided no rare trial or error occurred in the previous three trials. Fast-triggered trials were markedly less accurate than slow-triggered trials in both samples (Fig. 5; preregistered regressions). Faster recent responses are a **behavioral proxy** for a lapse, not direct confirmation of the participant's momentary thoughts, awareness or phenomenology. End-of-task trait questionnaires are not a same-trial mind-wandering report.

The authors considered capture-suppression and perceptual-recoupling accounts, which they explicitly regard as potentially compatible stages. Their key results are more nuanced than a simple replication claim:

- Experiment 1's recoupling analyses were **exploratory/unregistered**: reward and novelty distractors were associated with higher rare-trial accuracy; the modeled reward effect declined across blocks (p. 8).
- Experiment 2 preregistered the recoupling analysis. Its reward main-effect coefficient was `b=0.41, SE=0.24, p=.089`; novelty `p=.73`; reward-by-trigger interaction `b=-0.87, SE=0.43, p=.045`, indicating a stronger reward effect in fast-triggered than slow-triggered trials in the stated model. Reward-by-trigger-by-block was **not significant** (`p=.069`). Thus it is not a clean replication of every Experiment 1 effect.
- The **unregistered pooled** three-way logistic model reports reward `b=0.57, 95% CI [0.23, 0.91], p=.0011`, reward-by-block `b=-0.15, p=.020`, reward-by-slow-trigger `b=-0.65, p=.030`, and reward-by-slow-trigger-by-block `p=.053` (Table 2). Coefficients are conditional **log-odds** in an interaction model, not percentage-point gains or simple overall effects. A separate model without trigger interactions reported pooled reward `p=.083` and reward-by-block `p=.32`; model specification changes the estimand.
- The first-two-block moderation analysis by individual reward learning showed effects in Experiment 1 but not Experiment 2. Its pooled model **failed to converge**; the authors themselves call its coefficients suggestive. Do not present a stronger early-block/high-learner mechanism as a reliable independent replication.
- In an unregistered reviewer-suggested pooled RT analysis, reward-associated distractors slowed rare-trial responses by `b=12.29 ms, SE=5.78, p=.033`; trigger interaction was not significant. Slowing and accuracy improvement can coexist, so they do not separately prove perceptual recoupling. The planned RT-by-accuracy capture-suppression tests lacked consistent support; their failure is not proof that suppression never occurs.

Appendix A's exploratory delay interactions were nonsignificant: not evidence of no delay effect. Appendix B reports exploratory trigger-specific analyses; Appendix C reports that a non-singular alternative random-slope specification preserved direction and significance for the main pooled accuracy model, while another model's slope selection simplified to the original structure. These are authors' reported checks, not an independent reanalysis. Appendix D's fitted-model simulations (250 simulations per tested effect) illustrate sensitivity, not prospective proof of adequate power or replication. Multiple models and contrasts, modest per-condition trial counts, varying preregistration status and convergence issues matter when weighing the findings.

## What follows, and what remains open

The data support a **task-specific association** between reward history, distractor condition and rare-response performance under the fitted models, with an experimentally manipulated training/distractor arrangement. They are consistent with distractors helping reengage task processing, but neither a mental-state transition nor its neural mechanism was directly measured. In particular, differences in speed–accuracy policy, response preparation, salience, and learning that particular colors predict rare trials are relevant alternatives. The authors' analysis found no increasing accuracy over blocks and argues against learned anticipation; absence of an increasing trend does not by itself rule out rapidly acquired or stable color-based anticipation. Authors acknowledge speed–accuracy concerns and suggest additional physiological measures and tasks.

Compared with the split-brain studies, this adds a warning against treating RT slowing or a distractor as necessarily harmful, or treating a fast RT window as direct experiential evidence. It does not test the same patient, bilateral cue competition, blink or simultaneous phenomenal conjunction. A reasonable research direction is to combine independently validated state probes, gaze and response controls with calibrated, time-anchored first-person reports; learned cueing and experience sampling should be assessed for probe and selection effects. This remains a design proposal, not an established subject assay.

The findings do not choose between L-exclusive, C-full, C-layered and P, identify subject incidence or q-quality, or equate L with IIT. P retains numerically distinct private experiential tokens even if their types correspond. C3, B4–B5/F2, B9/F3 and B10 remain open; PAINgen's 21 mean discrepancies still need actual conversion/export code. No claim is made that Klinge's book was read in full.
