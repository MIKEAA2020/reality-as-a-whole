Two corrections materially change the earlier audit:

1. **Ptito's patient timing:** the control RSVP stimulus-onset asynchrony (SOA) was 150 ms, but M.L.'s was **233 ms**. Using the paper's lag convention, lag 2 and lag 8 correspond to 300 and 1,200 ms for controls, versus approximately 466 and 1,864 ms for M.L. The earlier partial thesis extraction incorrectly treated 150 ms as if it applied to the patient too. In addition, M.L. had ten practice blocks versus one for controls. The patient–control patterns are therefore not an identical-timing comparison.
2. **The earlier study's layout:** Ptito et al. say the 2004 design has a same-stream/different-stream confound. But Giesbrecht and Kingstone's own method says **four possible target locations**, and T2 appeared at one of the **remaining three locations**, including on same-field trials. Their task consisted of individually masked letters, not the four sustained RSVP streams of Ptito's design. Thus our earlier repetition of the purported same-location/within-field confound as an established fact was too strong. The papers' descriptions are in tension; we do not resolve the source of Ptito's characterization or pretend their stimulus sequences are identical. Ptito's own different-stream comparison is valid as a design distinction in that study, not proof that the 2004 targets shared a location.

The new figures provide actual effect estimates. They strengthen the **task-specific** behavioral evidence but do not establish an equivalence bound, an experiential centrality structure, or a numerical subject count.

## 2. Giesbrecht and Kingstone 2004: actual methods and results

One patient, JW, and six right-handed controls were tested. JW was 47 and had a callosotomy for epilepsy. The paper refers elsewhere for detailed case history; its report here does not prove that every other possible pathway was severed. It describes **four marked locations**, two in each visual field, each approximately 2° from the horizontal and vertical meridians. Each target was a letter, followed by a digit mask. T1 appeared at one location; T2 was presented at **one of the other three**. The design factorially varied T1 field, T2 field and three reported inter-target intervals: 114, 298 and 696 ms. Trials were randomly intermixed. JW's stimuli lasted 114 ms, versus 86 ms for controls after pilot performance differed. A response display later listed the possible letters; JW pointed and an experimenter typed his responses, while controls typed their own. The extract does not report quantitative fixation-monitoring results. Different presentation and response procedures complicate patient–control comparisons.

The patient was tested across two days, with 12 blocks used as repeated observations. Mean T1 accuracy collapsed across lag was **76.1% LVF versus 93.9% RVF**, `t(11)=5.83, p<.002`. T2 was evaluated **conditional on correct T1**. Collapsed across other factors the reported patient T2|T1 means were **55.3%, 57.4%, 72.9%** at lags 1, 2, 3, respectively, and **36.7% LVF versus 87.0% RVF** for T2 location. They report a lag effect and a lag-by-T2-field interaction, with a larger lag effect for LVF T2. The reported F tests use block means, not 12 patients. Figure 1B displays the four field combinations by lag: visually the LVF-T2 curves rise markedly with lag while the RVF-T2 curves remain high and comparatively flat. Exact cell values and separate cell tests cannot be inferred from visual inspection alone.

Controls' lag-collapsed T1 means were 95.4% LVF and 95.2% RVF; their conditional T2 means were 81.2%, 90.3%, and 97.7% across the three lags. The authors report a lag effect and an interaction between T1 field and T2 field, with better performance when targets were in different fields. Their conclusion that a blink appears within and across fields does not establish equivalence of the field-specific lag functions. The authors propose more than one explanation for cross-field coupling, including residual routes and attentional shifting; neither is directly identified.

**Conditioning remains important.** `P(T2 correct | T1 correct, condition)` is an interpretable selected-trial outcome, but field-dependent T1 performance makes the retained trial populations potentially different. The article's complete prose still does not supply all lag-by-field joint and marginal counts or errors, or establish that selection caused the asymmetry. The expanded results strengthen what we can say about the reported effect, not a causal or phenomenological interpretation of it.

## 3. Ptito 2009: what the reported blink actually is

M.L., a 28-year-old left-handed man reported to have had a complete callosotomy for epilepsy, and 22 neurologically intact controls were tested. Four simultaneous RSVP streams contained letters and two sequential digit targets. Each participant reported **how many digits** (0, 1 or 2) appeared in each visual hemifield, rather than their identities. M.L. used fingers and the experimenter entered his responses; controls used a keyboard. For M.L. a camera monitored one eye; the experimenter rejected fewer than 2% of trials with detected movement. Controls' eye movements were measured in a separate pilot sample of five, not described as trial-level monitoring for the 22 main controls. These controls are relevant but not proof of zero leak or observer error.

The main within/across comparison used targets in **different** streams in both conditions; same-stream trials were separately considered. The source says accuracy for that contrast was calculated as **correct report of both targets**, because a single count in a same-hemifield trial cannot identify whether T1 or T2 was seen. In cross-field trials the paper also analyzes T2 conditional on T1 for the control hemisphere comparison; its individual patient results report separate T1 and T2 accuracies in inter-hemifield trials. The joint count score must not be relabeled as a universal T2|T1 measure. The authors' nominal 1/9 subjective-chance claim assumes the three paired count choices and no informed prior; it is not a measured individual guessing rate.

The reported **joint count accuracy** for different-stream trials is:

| Participants | Target fields | Lag 2 | Lag 8 | Lag 8 − lag 2 |
|---|---|---:|---:|---:|
| M.L. | within one field | 12% | 29% | +17 percentage points |
| M.L. | across fields | 27% | 39% | +12 pp |
| Controls | within one field | 33% | 42% | +9 pp |
| Controls | across fields | 42% | 57% | +15 pp |

Values are authors' rounded means; the last column is simple arithmetic on them, not a reanalysis of individual trials. Lag 8 minus lag 2 is the paper's plotted blink-magnitude convention. The different-stream interaction in controls is reported `F(1,21)=2.34, p>.14`: failure to reject that interaction is **not** proof that the effects are equal. M.L.'s reported within/across contrasts are descriptive and the figure's confidence intervals are for controls, not a patient-specific equivalence interval. It is inaccurate to say the numerical blink is literally identical: in the rounded values above, M.L.'s across-field lag contrast is 5 pp smaller. Low performance, particularly in some left-field conditions, may limit the possible size of a difference; no floor-effect correction or causal claim is established here.

In same-stream trials, M.L. showed 20% versus 29% at lags 2 and 8; controls 51% versus 47%. The authors report a significant stream-by-lag interaction for the controls but the within-same-stream reverse lag comparison was not itself significant. For M.L.'s different-stream within-field trials, the paper reports 8%/12% for targets assigned to the right hemisphere and 16%/46% for targets assigned to the left hemisphere at lags 2/8. The paper describes a left-hemisphere advantage; this descriptive asymmetry is not the same estimand as Giesbrecht and Kingstone's T2-field-by-lag interaction. Their own discussion also offers guided search and alphanumeric/language specialization as alternative contributors.

The timing matters: controls' 150-ms SOA and M.L.'s 233-ms SOA mean the two nominal lags do not denote the same elapsed time across participants. Equal or similar lag-2/lag-8 differences, even if estimated precisely, would not establish the same time course, mechanism, or underlying capacity. No confidence interval or clinical population generalization follows for one patient merely from similarity to a control mean.

## 4. Layout and task comparison without the previous conflation

Ptito et al. specifically attribute a within-field same-stream versus cross-field different-stream confound to Giesbrecht and Kingstone; the latter's recovered procedure states T2 always appeared in a different **location** from T1. Ptito et al.'s clear description of that confound for the separate two-stream Holländer et al. 2005 design should not be applied uncritically to the 2004 paper. The visually inspected 2004 Figure 1A illustrates a cross-field masked-target example, not all configurations; a conceptual difference between an RSVP *stream* and an isolated masked target location also limits direct terminology. The reliable correction is narrow: **do not say the 2004 methods placed within-field T1 and T2 at the same spatial location**. We do not assert why the later article described it otherwise.

The two investigations use different patients, numbers and types of controls, stimulus sequences, response procedures, time scales, and main accuracy estimands. Ptito et al. explicitly sought a related replication of hemispheric asymmetry in a different patient, so the work is a targeted extension, but not an exact replication of the original conditional letter-identification design. These cross-field effects constrain overly simple independence accounts; alternative motor, sensory, memory, shared alerting, attention or residual pathway explanations cannot be ranked from these sources alone.

## 5. Consequences and missing inputs

Neither lagged behavioral task directly reports the two particular contents as jointly experienced **in the same window**. A common behavioral bottleneck need not be an experienced centrality relation or a single subject; it also does not prove multiple subjects. Lopez's philosophical argument about *experienced* centrality remains distinct from the functional evidence. Independently warranted `G=1` could challenge the stipulated exclusive-L package, while distinct-owner `N=1` is needed to challenge the closed C-layered package; neither study supplies that subject-incidence bridge or a human-to-four-spin physical map. P's local and global subjects have numerically distinct private tokens, not a single shared token. Wrong or absent count responses are not proof of absent experience. L is not identified with IIT and no IIT value is computed.

The full texts and inspected 2004 figure provide substantial results but not trial-level data, independently verified channel/lesion details, complete error distributions, patient-specific equivalence bounds or a calibrated first-person conjunction measure. No public repository for the raw trials was established in this pass; obtaining them would require an author or library inquiry, none sent.
