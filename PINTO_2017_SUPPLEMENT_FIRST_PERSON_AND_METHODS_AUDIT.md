# Pinto 2017 supplement: first-person reports and selection limits

## Result

The user supplied the previously inaccessible supplement to Pinto et al., [*Split brain: divided perception but undivided consciousness*](https://doi.org/10.1093/brain/aww358), *Brain* 140, 1231–1237 (2017). The document's reported questions and answers **qualify our earlier summary** of the main paper: both patients give important indications of ordinary visual and personal continuity, but their individual answers are not interchangeable and neither said every part of the earlier compressed summary in the same way. In particular, DDC described reduced left-hand control and a sometimes involuntary right-arm movement; DDV reported altered peripheral vision and sometimes less clear left-sided bodily perception. Both accounts deserve their specific details, not a blanket claim of no change.

The supplement also records high eye-data exclusion rates in the two-patient detection experiment: 22.4%–68.75%, depending on patient and response block. The authors report that the **pattern** remained the same when trials with erroneous eye movements/fixations were included, which matters, but the supplement does not provide a complete unfiltered cell-level analysis. The reported exclusions warrant caution about selection; they do not negate the observed performance or establish selection bias by themselves.

These are genuinely informative reports of lived experience and test behavior. Neither a broad feeling of being one person nor a brief-trial response is a validated complete census of possible private subjects. Nothing here supplies a clinical-to-four-spin mapping, a same-window local/global subject count, an IIT calculation, or an empirical q-quality law. P and C-layered remain unresolved.

## 2. The questions and the participants' actual answers

Pages 21–22 present four questions to **each** patient. These ask about sight to both sides of fixation; whole versus half-body sensation; whole versus half-body control; and feeling normal/one person versus sensing another presence. Each also asks about change after surgery. They are **structured, contrastive questions**, not an independently verified free-form account of every possible experience. The fourth question ties “normal” to “one person” and “another presence”; these are not equivalent to a neutral question about every kind of distinct conscious subject. That framing may shape interpretation, without making the answers unimportant or insincere. The supplement does not establish that these questions were asked during specific 120-ms visual trials.

| Topic | DDV, as recorded | DDC, as recorded |
|---|---|---|
| Vision | Says he sees well but the peripheral angles are blurred; says the disturbance was not present before the operation. | Says he sees well on both sides and this is the same before and after the operation. |
| Body sensation | Says he feels all his body, while left side—particularly arm and leg—is sometimes less clear, a change from before. | Answers in terms of right-arm sensitivity before and after surgery, not an explicit yes/no statement that he feels every part of his body. |
| Control | Says he can control his whole body as before. | Says the left side, specifically the hand, has less control and that this had always been so. This is not an unqualified “whole-body control unchanged” answer. |
| Other presence | Answers that he feels absolutely normal, as before; the question offers one person versus another presence, but the answer does not separately enumerate all possible subjects. | Denies another presence, as the supplement's bracketed clarification specifies, and adds that the right arm sometimes makes an involuntary/reflexive movement after surgery. |

The main article summarizes the reports more broadly. Our earlier audit repeated that summary without marking these qualifications. The correction is **not** that the participants' reports of ordinary unity should be discarded: DDV explicitly reports feeling normal and whole-body control, and DDC explicitly reports bilateral sight and denies another presence. It is that those claims must be paired with their actual qualifications and not imputed identically to both people. The transcript's bracketed clarification is an editor/translator aid; it is not independently checked against an audio recording.

## 3. The extensive interview is a distinct source item

Pages 23–36 are headed **“Extensive interview of DDC”**, dated 26-09-2016, and credited as translated by D.A. Neville. It is a translated, selectively annotated record with unintelligible segments, digressions and bracketed interpretation; it should not be presented as a verbatim original-language audio transcript. Its relationship in time and procedure to the separate four-question account is not specified in the file, so we do not assume that those four questions appear in this interview or occurred in the same session.

In its first part the interviewer asks DDC to imagine navigating familiar locations and describe what lies to each side. The transcript records him describing locations and using both hands to indicate positions. This is meaningful evidence of his communicated spatial knowledge and imagined navigation; prompts, gesture and memory are intertwined, and it is not a controlled simultaneous bilateral visual-stimulus or subject-count test. We have not independently verified the locations or his imagery.

In its second part the interviewer asks about changes in sight, bodily control, sensation, hearing and imagination. DDC describes left-hand tactile recognition and fine actions as more difficult, and comments on balance and memory. The report also contains hesitations, clarifications, interviewer suggestions and shifts of topic. Some exchanges about limb strength are difficult to reconcile without further context; the interview alone is not a precise dated clinical change score or a proof of the surgery's causal effects. It does show that his own account was more nuanced than a simple “everything felt exactly unchanged.” The fact that an interviewer invites him not to answer and asks what he feels is relevant; it does not eliminate leading options or make the account an exhaustive phenomenological measurement.

## 4. Experimental procedures and selective retention

The supplement says the two patients were 35 and 45 at first testing, 39 and 49 at last testing, respectively, and describes their surgeries (p. 1). Its general procedure states that, except in Experiment 4A, an experimenter used an eye-tracker display to start trials once fixation appeared adequate; the experimenter could observe the patient but not the test screen. The authors report precautions against cross-cueing, including preventing hands from touching each other or the other half of the body (pp. 1–2). These are important controls, not a proof that every internal or external route was blocked.

Experiment 1 presented a circle or nothing, with responses in separate left-hand, right-hand and verbal blocks. Each block had 24 trials. The supplement reports these **eye-data exclusions** (p. 3):

| Patient | Left hand | Right hand | Verbal |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| DDV | 68.75% | 45.8% | 50% |
| DDC | 64.6% | 22.4% | 55% |

These figures concern the stated response-block trial groups, not estimated probabilities that whole experimental sessions were invalid. They include exclusion criteria based on recorded saccades, missing eye positions and fixation position (p. 2); the supplement does not supply a reason-specific or stimulus-specific breakdown for these cells. Selection could change the retained trial mix, but its direction and magnitude cannot be inferred from rates alone. The authors explicitly say the result pattern was the same when all trials with erroneous eye movements/fixations were included; the wording does not supply per-cell unfiltered estimates or demonstrate equivalence. Without trial-level records we cannot independently check this robustness statement or reconstruct uncertainty and possible exclusion dependencies.

Page 15 gives response-mode results rather than a single undifferentiated number. For example, DDC's false-alarm rates are 0% left-hand, 7.7% right-hand and 15.5% verbal; DDV's are 0% in each. The main text reports an overall DDC hit/false-alarm summary of 97.5%/7.7%; the table's blocked results supply context, not a replacement estimate with an invented denominator or weighting. DDV performed two 24-trial blocks per mode; DDC had differing numbers of blocks by mode (p. 3). A participant-specific nonsignificant field-by-response interaction is not a confidence-bounded equivalence result.

For DDC's two-item comparisons, the supplement gives task-specific randomization, response options and eye exclusions: for instance, the rectangle-comparison runs' exclusions are 38.3% and 28.5% (pp. 3–5); the picture-comparison right-hand condition lost an entire eye-tracking block, besides stated trial exclusions (pp. 6–7). The recorded match results remain evidence about performance on those particular tasks; not rejecting chance across fields is not proof of zero information or absent experience. We do not pool these different procedures, assume retained counts from rounded percentages, or reproduce the authors' permutation tests from summaries.

The supplement has internal description inconsistencies that prevent treating every timing/scale as settled. For Experiment 4A its general section describes automatically triggered trials, while its task-specific timing says the experimenter manually initiated them; its task text also mentions a verbal mode but its results list manual modes. The main paper lists 100 ms in 4A, while the supplement's timing says 120 ms (main text and supplement pp. 1, 9–10). For 4B the main paper states a 1–4 confidence scale, while the supplement describes 1–5 (pp. 10–12). These discrepancies do not demonstrate which description is right or invalidate the whole study; raw task code or a clarification would be needed. They do not change the distinctly specified 120-ms Experiment 1 summary above.

## 5. What changes for subject constitution?

The supplementary reports are evidence that these two particular patients communicated a sense of ordinary continuity and bilateral visual experience, while reporting patient-specific alterations. They count against an overly simple prediction of *felt complete half-body blindness, half-body control, or obligatory experienced second presence* for every patient. They do not establish absence of such experiences in other patients, or absence of all private or non-reporting experiencers in these patients. Question framing, access and timing limit the inference; they do not license discounting first-person testimony.

For the project's stipulated episode, P assigns two local subjects plus a numerically distinct global subject, with private local/global experiential tokens that can correspond in type. C-layered assigns a single designated subject with a coarse bilateral conjunction but no detailed cross-side conjunction. Both predict `(D,G)=(0,1)` and differ in `N`, the occurrence of distinct local-detail and global-conjunction owners. Neither a broad personal-unity response nor unilateral trial detection establishes that the target local and global contents were co-present, nor that their owners were numerically identical or distinct. Nor is the question's “another presence” a direct test of P's non-agentive local subjects. A defensible positive `N` needs evidence for both experiences, timing, mapping and independent distinct-owner criterion; `N=0` needs adequate coverage of the specified population. The supplement does not supply either.

Accordingly, the update **revises evidential detail rather than selecting a winner**. C3, B4–B5/F2, B9/F3 and B10 remain open. The separate PAINgen 21 mean discrepancies still require the actual conversion/export code. No new clinical study, raw-data analysis, validated subject-count assay, or full review of the source authors' other papers was performed.
