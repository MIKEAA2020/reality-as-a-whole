# Self-Existent Experiential Plenitude

## Abstract

This framework proposes that reality is a single self-sufficient, necessarily actual whole whose intrinsic nature is experiential. The whole is fundamentally timeless but internally differentiated into multiple subjects and their experiences. Spacetime, mass, physical laws and local temporal succession are to be understood as features of its internal organization, not as a container or machinery external to it. Genuine possibility and actuality coincide: what is genuinely possible is actual, and what is not actual is not genuinely possible. No agent or temporal process is required to turn possibilities into actuality.

The proposal is intended to explain reality without unexplained external ingredients while taking experience, suffering and the non-optimality of our universe seriously. It is **not yet a completed explanation**. The necessity and detailed constitution of the whole, the individuation of subjects, the qualities of experience, the recovery of physics and the appropriate probability measure remain to be established. The framework distinguishes what it assumes, what follows conditionally, what its models illustrate and what must still be explained.

## 1. What the framework represents

### 1.1 One whole, multiple subjects

The **whole** is all of reality: nothing that exists stands outside it. It need not be a person, an agent, or a universal subject. Its unity is ontological and explanatory—it is one encompassing reality—not a claim that every experience belongs to one mind.

A **subject** is a genuine experiencer, not merely a viewpoint, an information-processing system or a record. The framework proposes that there are multiple distinct finite subjects within the whole. Each has experiences with particular contents and qualities; subjects may also interact and make records accessible to one another. Exactly what constitutes a subject, whether subjects can overlap, and how they persist are open questions.

The whole is **intrinsically experiential**: experience belongs to reality's nature rather than being an inexplicable product added to a wholly non-experiential base. This does not mean that every mathematical description, physical component or relation is itself a conscious subject. The relationship between the experiential nature of the whole and the organization of particular subjects requires a constitution rule that has not yet been supplied.

### 1.2 Self-existence rather than self-production

The framework seeks an actuality whose existence is intelligible from its own nature, without an external creator or sustaining mechanism. The intended identity of **essence and existence** expresses this goal: the whole would not first have a merely possible essence and then receive existence from something else.

“Self-actualization” must therefore not be taken to mean that the whole first did not exist, then caused itself to exist. Such a sequence would require a time in which the whole was absent and would make the whole act before it existed. The proposal is that actuality is intrinsic to what the whole is. Processes in which local systems form, change or cease can still occur *within* reality if an account of local time is recovered.

If an external creator were proposed as the explanation of a universe, that creator would itself belong to reality as a whole and its own existence would still require explanation. This framework instead seeks an account of actuality that has no external source.

Calling the whole necessary or self-existent does not prove that it is so. A successful explanation must show why its existence and determinations are intelligible without merely repeating that they exist.

### 1.3 Fundamental timelessness and local time

The whole is not located in an external space and does not evolve against an external master clock. “Timeless” does not mean that observers see an unchanging universe. Local temporal order, change, memory and clocks are to be accounted for as relations among events or subsystems within the whole. A satisfactory theory must recover the functioning of physical clocks and the experienced succession of events; neither follows from the word “timeless.”

## 2. Assumptions and explanatory targets

The framework rests on the following **working assumptions**. They are not presented as established conclusions.

1. **Experiential actuality:** experience occurs, and the whole has an intrinsic experiential nature.
2. **Self-sufficiency and necessity:** the whole is actual through its own nature and could not fail to exist. The intended explanatory target is essence = existence.
3. **Strong possibility–actuality identity:** for a description `d` at a specified scope, genuine possibility `P(d)` and actuality `A(d)` coincide: `P(d) ↔ A(d)`.
4. **Plural subjects:** the whole contains genuinely distinct subjects, not merely multiple descriptions of one universal experiencer.
5. **Fundamental timelessness:** no external time or spacetime is assumed for the whole; local time and spacetime require explanation.
6. **Non-optimality:** our universe is not the best possible universe, and the whole would be perfect only if our universe were best possible.
7. **Intelligibility and parsimony:** the desired account should explain existential, structural and experiential facts without brute external selections, while avoiding unnecessary independent assumptions.

The first part of assumption 1—there is experience—is a starting datum. The claim that experience is intrinsic to the whole is a further metaphysical hypothesis. Similarly, the logical consequences of assumption 6 can be derived once it is granted; neither its value comparison nor its bridge from whole to universe is established merely by stating it.

The framework must eventually specify a single constitution connecting these assumptions to experienced subjects and to observable physics. At present it supplies an **architecture and constraints**, not that completed constitution.

## 3. Possibility, actuality and consistency

### 3.1 The meaning of plenitude

Under the strong identity `P(d) ↔ A(d)`, there are no genuinely possible but wholly unrealized alternatives awaiting actualization. “Possible” here means genuinely possible, not merely imaginable or consistent with a selected formal system. A description can be mathematically consistent without having been shown to describe a genuine possibility or a concrete occurrence.

This does not say that every describable world exists. It says that the distinction between the genuinely possible and the actual must be understood without an extra act of selection. The key task is to explain what makes a description genuinely possible and how the whole's actual content is determined without making that criterion an unexplained external law.

### 3.2 Global necessity

One precise interpretation of the strong thesis uses classical modal operators for **global propositions about the whole**. Suppose `◇φ → φ` holds for every such proposition, including `¬φ`, and take `□φ` to mean `¬◇¬φ`. Then:

```text
◇¬φ → ¬φ        instance of the assumed principle
φ → ¬◇¬φ        contraposition
φ → □φ          definition of necessity
```

If necessity also implies truth, every true global proposition is necessary: `φ ↔ □φ`. This consequence is called **global modal collapse**. It follows from the stated formalization; it is not an independent proof that the formalization describes reality.

Let `E` be the global proposition “experience occurs.” If `E` is actual and the global principle applies, then `□E`: some experience is necessary. This result does not show why experience has its particular nature, why there are multiple subjects, or why the modal principle is true. Nor does an experience-free formal model, used to test a proposed inference, establish that an experience-free reality is genuinely possible.

### 3.3 Variety without contradiction

Distinct local experiences can coexist. For example, one subject can experience warmth while another experiences cold; their claims refer to different subjects and circumstances. Such local differentiation does not make a global proposition and its negation true in the same respect.

If two exhaustive global descriptions assert `φ` and `¬φ`, classical consistency prevents both from being actual. Under the strong modal principle they cannot both be genuinely possible. An adequate theory must explain which global descriptions are admissible and how local differences fit into one coherent whole; re-indexing local claims does not by itself resolve contradictory global claims.

## 4. Subjects, unity and qualitative experience

### 4.1 The constitution problem

A complete account requires answers to four distinct questions:

- **Incidence:** which organizations, if any, are subjects?
- **Unity:** which experiences belong together to one subject?
- **Persistence:** what makes a subject at one local time the same subject at another?
- **Quality:** why does an experience have its particular felt character, including pleasantness or unpleasantness?

A record or report can provide evidence of an experience, but it is not automatically the experience itself. Likewise, causal interaction can connect systems without establishing that they have a common subject. Any proposed subject rule must specify its physical or experiential domain, how it handles overlapping candidates and symmetry, and how its conclusions behave under equivalent descriptions.

### 4.2 A diagnostic example

Consider a system with four interacting components arranged as two strongly connected pairs with a weak connection between the pairs. Two conceivable constitution rules applied to the same physical dynamics give different answers:

- a **connectivity rule** counts the connected four-component system as one subject;
- a **local-cohesion rule** counts the two more strongly connected pairs as two subjects.

If both rules leave the physical dynamics and report laws unchanged, they make the same predictions for those reports by construction. A shared report combining information from both pairs can show shared information access, but it does not by itself establish that one subject experiences both contents. The example does not prove that all subject theories are empirically indistinguishable; it shows that the missing link must be stated rather than inferred from connectivity alone.

A more demanding rule might use causal influences measured under controlled interventions. But its chosen intervention context, scale, grouping, treatment of ties and assignment of experience must still be justified. Exact symmetry can leave overlapping candidates tied, and a subject-boundary rule alone does not settle who persists when boundaries change.

### 4.3 Qualitative character and discomfort

A proposed connection between **misalignment and discomfort** can be expressed as an identity hypothesis: for a specified subject and context, a particular organization of mismatch might be the unpleasant aspect of an experience. The relevant organization must be identified independently of the reported unpleasantness and distinguished from mere correlation.

Even in a simple network of coupled phases, several quantities differ: total mismatch, unavoidable frustration, excess mismatch above the best attainable configuration, and dissipation. For three equally coupled phases whose pairwise preferences all favor opposite alignment, write

```text
E = k Σ_{i<j} [1 + cos(φ_i − φ_j)]
  = (k/2) [3 + |Σ_i exp(iφ_i)|²],          k > 0.
```

The minimum is `3k/2`, attained when the phases are equally spaced by `2π/3`. The energy gradient vanishes there. Thus, in gradient relaxation, the dissipation proxy based on the squared gradient is zero even though the minimum frustration is positive. Persistent frustration therefore need not imply ongoing dissipation. This calculation is not evidence that a brain, a fundamental string system or a person has that organization, and it does not show that frustration is felt as pain.

The quality problem remains: why would a specified organization be *this* feeling rather than another quality or no experience? An answer needs a principled experiential identity or constitution, together with empirical constraints that do not define the physical measure from the target report.

## 5. Physical reality and quantum phenomena

Physical descriptions characterize relations, states, events and their observable consequences. In this framework they are descriptions of the whole's internal organization; they are not assumed to be a separate, non-experiential substance producing experience from outside. This is an ontological proposal, not yet a derivation of the known laws of physics.

A successful account must recover, in their tested domains:

- quantum states, observables, outcome probabilities and measurement records;
- causal propagation and locality, with appropriate quantum qualifications;
- local spacetime geometry and gravitational dynamics;
- matter, mass, interactions and the empirical particle spectrum;
- physical clocks, records and their resource costs.

For example, if one already assumes the effect structure of finite-dimensional quantum theory and a normalized noncontextual additive probability assignment, the probabilities take the Born form `P(E)=Tr(ρE)` for a density operator `ρ` and effect `E`. This is a conditional reconstruction: the framework has not derived the quantum effect structure, a particular physical state or a subject measure from self-existent actuality.

Quantum measurement also constrains any proposed account of subjects and records. In an idealized reversible measurement, a memory can become correlated with a system and later be coherently reset. Preserving an independent which-outcome record constrains the interference recoverable in the specified experiment. These are physical relations between records and interference; they do not establish that a qubit memory is a subject, that experience requires an indelible record, or that consciousness causes collapse.

## 6. Emergent time, fields and gravity

A finite relational-clock model illustrates how a stationary description can contain changing conditional states. Take a three-level system with `H = diag(0,1,2)`, three orthogonal clock readings `|j⟩`, and `t_j = 2πj/3`. Set `U(t)=exp(−iHt)` and

```text
p_n = exp(−βn)/Z,    |ψ₀⟩ = Σ_n √p_n |n⟩,    β > 0,
|Ψ⟩ = (1/√3) Σ_j |j⟩ ⊗ U(t_j)|ψ₀⟩.
```

For `j=0,1`, define `D_j = ⟨j+1| ⊗ I − ⟨j| ⊗ U(t_{j+1}−t_j)` and the positive constraint `C=Σ_j D_j†D_j`. Then `C|Ψ⟩=0`, while conditioning on clock reading `j` gives `ρ_j=U(t_j)|ψ₀⟩⟨ψ₀|U(t_j)†`. These conditional states change despite the stationary whole-history state.

Tracing out the clock removes the energy-basis coherences in this example and gives the faithful Gibbs marginal `τ=exp(−βH)/Z`. With `σ_s(A)=τ^(is)Aτ^(−is)` and `α_t(A)=exp(iHt)Aexp(−iHt)`, direct substitution yields `σ_s=α_(−βs)`. A non-Gibbs marginal need not give the same correspondence. The clock, state, constraint and calibration are inputs to this example; it is not a derivation of physical time in quantum gravity.

A physical account must go further: identify actual clock systems and records, show how different local clocks compare, include their interactions and gravitational backreaction, and recover appropriate relativistic dynamics without assuming a universal clock. Cold-atom internal-clock experiments are relevant analogues, not demonstrations of an external cosmic time or solutions of quantum gravity.

The distinction between a mathematical approximation and the intended continuum physics is also essential. In a specified finite-mode field model, a spatial cutoff can introduce small nonlocal tails and an artificial dependence of recorded probabilities on the ordering of nominally separated operations. That artifact is not evidence of superluminal signalling. To recover a local continuum theory, the complete instruments—including records, all outcomes and physical resource bounds—must be compared on common input and output domains. A finite calculation or convergence of one probability is not sufficient to establish the whole instrument's continuum limit.

No completed derivation of quantum gravity, the general problem of time, the Standard Model or the emergence of spacetime follows from the framework as currently stated.

## 7. Probability and an observer's location

A finite subject can be uncertain which situation or location is theirs even if the whole is already actual and no possibility is selected for existence. If a justified measure `μ` is defined over actual subject-locations and `E` is the available evidence, one may write

```text
Cr(Q | E) = μ(Q ∩ E) / μ(E),
```

provided the denominator is defined and positive. This expresses **self-location uncertainty**; it is not a probability that reality is created or that one merely possible world is made actual.

The expression does not determine its own measure. One must justify which subjects or subject-moments count, what evidence is shared, how different locations are weighted and how infinite populations are handled. Symmetry can require equal weights among interchangeable locations without fixing weights between non-equivalent groups. For infinite populations, different cutoff procedures can give different limiting proportions.

The useful general lesson from Bostrom's simulation argument is that reasoning from an observer's position requires a reference class and a measure. The literal comparison between simulated and nonsimulated observers, its population formula and a claim that our universe is a simulation are **not assumptions of this framework**.

## 8. Non-optimality and suffering

Let `Perfect(W)` mean that the whole is perfect in the relevant moral or evaluative sense, and `Best(U)` that our universe is the best possible universe. The framework adopts:

```text
Perfect(W) → Best(U)
¬Best(U)
Therefore ¬Perfect(W).
```

The inference is valid. Its conclusion depends on the substantive bridge and comparison; it is not an empirical proof of those premises. A necessary or self-sufficient whole is therefore not assumed to be morally perfect, and reality is not assumed to select the best world.

A better genuinely possible **local universe** would have to be actual under the strong possibility–actuality thesis. Its coherent place within the whole and the relevant value comparison still need to be specified. A better local universe does not automatically imply that a different, better *entire whole* is genuinely possible; global modal collapse places additional constraints on that claim.

Suffering is a real feature to be explained. Calling it privation, mismatch, an inevitable cost or part of a consistent whole does not by itself justify it, show that it is necessary, or erase its felt character. An account of value must identify affected subjects and address their experiences rather than infer goodness from structural consistency.

## 9. Empirical constraints and tests

The framework must engage both first-person evidence and third-person science. Reports communicate a subject's testimony through a physical channel that can be delayed, incomplete or mistaken. Neural activity, behavior and no-report measures provide further evidence, but none is automatically identical to subjective experience.

A discriminating experiment should specify competing hypotheses before data collection, identify the physical intervention and possible alternative pathways, and independently assess whether the experiential measure remains valid under that intervention. For a simple example, let `Y` be a decoder's binary estimate, `R` a report, and `E` the target experience. If independent evidence supports bounds

```text
P(Y ≠ R) ≤ d,    P(R ≠ E) ≤ r,
```

then `P(Y ≠ E) ≤ d+r`, by the union bound. Agreement between decoder and report can inform `d`; it does not by itself establish `r`. Missing and unresolved responses, intervention failures, timing and population selection must be included in a real analysis.

Empirical research can test particular physical–phenomenal proposals and rule out specified predictions. It may leave multiple ultimate ontologies compatible with the same data. Conversely, a metaphysical account that purports to explain our world must not contradict well-established physical or neuroscientific findings.

## 10. Evaluation and outstanding obligations

The framework is judged against seven requirements:

1. **Experience:** recognize phenomenal occurrence and explain subjecthood and quality.
2. **Non-optimality:** account for an imperfect universe and real suffering.
3. **Intelligibility:** explain actuality, necessity, structure and experience without unexplained external selection.
4. **Predictive power:** produce discriminating claims with clear failure conditions.
5. **Physics:** recover the quantum, relativistic, gravitational and material phenomena observed in their domains.
6. **Consciousness science:** engage empirical evidence with valid interventions, reports and uncertainty.
7. **Elegance:** minimize total unexplained assumptions while satisfying the other requirements.

The principal unresolved obligations are:

- derive or justify the self-sufficient actuality of the whole and its essence–existence relation;
- justify the strong possibility–actuality identity and its global consistency;
- explain why the whole is experiential and how distinct subjects, their unity, identity and qualities are constituted;
- derive or recover quantum theory, matter, spacetime, local clocks and gravity from a common underlying account;
- justify a self-location measure and its treatment of infinite cases;
- explain value and suffering without assuming a perfect whole or dismissing local harms;
- obtain discriminating empirical consequences and compare the total explanatory cost with serious alternatives.

Until these obligations are met, the proposal is a **research framework, not a demonstrated complete or zero-residue theory**. Its assumptions are not conclusions, and finite illustrative models are not evidence that the universe or its subjects have the structures stipulated in those models.
