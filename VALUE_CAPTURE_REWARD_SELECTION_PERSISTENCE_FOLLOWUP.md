# Reward, prior selection and the persistence of attentional capture

## Bottom line

The [2011 Anderson et al. experiment](ANDERSON_LAURENT_YANTIS_2011_VALUE_DRIVEN_ATTENTIONAL_CAPTURE_AUDIT.md) is not the end of the evidence. Later direct contrasts provide stronger support that **reward history can affect subsequent search beyond simply having selected a color before**. But the effect varies with the paradigm, and a former-target bias can also occur without monetary reward. Neither conclusion requires a wholly separate neural mechanism or a claim about phenomenal subject unity.

A [subsequent follow-up](SHELAT_2026_REWARD_SELECTION_RT_AND_R_WORKFLOW_AUDIT.md) adds an independent RT-model fit and exploratory accuracy and RT contrasts between reward and selection, and documents an unsuccessful R setup attempt.

A further exploratory contrast in our previously fitted 2026 model compares reward-colored with selected-but-unrewarded distractors at observed blocks. In the **pooled fast-trigger, block-1** contrast, the estimate is **0.186 log odds**, 95% Wald CI **[−0.047, 0.420]**, unadjusted p=.118. The interval is compatible with either direction: this does not establish equality or a lack of reward specificity. These contrasts are retrospective and model-dependent, and the pooled analysis was unregistered.

This is a targeted, non-systematic follow-up, with the main results and some search snippets known before the [plan](VALUE_CAPTURE_REWARD_SELECTION_PERSISTENCE_FOLLOWUP_PLAN.md). We read relevant methods and results in the linked primary sources; no 2013, 2016, 2017, 2018 or 2023 participant-level data or code was independently reproduced. The 2026 calculation is a new contrast from the existing independently fitted model, not an execution of the authors' R scripts.

## 1. What later direct comparisons say about 2011

### Anderson and Halpern (2017): a new sample and a reanalysis

In [*On the value-dependence of value-driven attentional capture*](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13414-017-1289-6), the authors replicated the short 2011 training/test paradigm with **40** participants. The no-trained-color, low-value and high-value mean correct RTs were 688, 689 and 698 ms. Importantly, they report a **direct within-person high-versus-low comparison**: t(39)=2.30, p=.027, d=.36. Another 40-person group had otherwise similar training without monetary feedback: its average former-target-present minus absent RT was −1 ms, t(39)=−0.35, p=.731. They directly compared the high-value capture in the rewarded group against former-target capture in the unrewarded group, reporting t(78)=2.29, p=.025, d=.51, with unequal variances not assumed. This is stronger than claiming reward specificity from one significant result and one nonsignificant result. Different groups, recruitment sites, feedback conditions, reward motivation and the operational contrast still limit causal interpretation and generalization. A further no-reward experiment used a different color-search test, not the same shape-search task.

The same paper **reanalyzed the original 2011 experiments 1 and 3**, together N=50: high vs low, t(49)=2.19, p=.034, d=.31; printed pooled mean RTs were 666, 674 and 682 ms for absent, low and high. This is a previously unreported post-publication comparison in the **original data**, not an independent replication. It corrects the incompleteness of our prior 2011-only review: while the 2011 main text did not report that direct comparison, a later source did.

It also reports a cross-experiment comparison of the original pooled rewarded samples with the original 10-person unrewarded control as t(58)=2.73, p=.016, d=.89, “equal variance not assumed.” **Reporting inconsistency:** a conventional two-sided t distribution with exactly t=2.73 and df=58 gives p≈.00837, not .016; plausible rounding of t cannot explain the gap. Both the [author manuscript](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5463414/) and [publisher text](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/s13414-017-1289-6) print these numbers. Welch degrees of freedom or a different adjustment could in principle differ, but the printed statistics alone do not resolve this; no conclusion about which entry is wrong is warranted without the data or clarification. The two-sided p calculated from the displayed t and df is an arithmetic check, **not** a reanalysis of the raw data. The separately reported 2017 sample's direct comparisons above do not depend on resolving this discrepancy.

### Marchner and Preuschhof (2018): reward and selection in the same subjects

In [*Reward history but not search history explains value-driven attentional capture*](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13414-018-1513-z), 84 analyzed participants each searched during training for three counterbalanced target colors: high-reward, low-reward and selected-but-unrewarded. In the subsequent unrewarded shape-search test, the four conditions had 64 trials each: high, low, unrewarded former-target and no trained-color distractor. This is a useful **within-person direct contrast** without changing the whole reward context between groups.

Their correct-RT means were 729, 722, 717 and 718 ms, respectively. Bonferroni-adjusted comparisons gave high vs selected-unrewarded **p=.008** and high vs absent **p=.024**; the high–low contrast was not statistically reliable. The former is direct evidence that, in this task, the higher-reward former target interfered more than the unrewarded former target. It does not prove that selection-history influences cannot exist, that high and low reward are equal, or that reward and selection require different physical mechanisms. The groups of colors differed in feedback history as well as monetary value. The reported nonsignificant condition × block interaction does not establish that capture cannot decline. The authors reported no RT-capture correlation with their additional working-memory measure; this is not a direct between-study interaction test against the 2011 correlation.

### Sha and Jiang (2016): an important contrasting result

[Components of reward-driven attentional capture](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13414-015-1038-7) reported slowing from former target colors after training **without monetary reward** in experiment 1 (N=24), t(23)=2.10, p<.05. In two reward-training versions (N=24 each), former targets versus absent slowed search, but direct high-versus-low/no-reward comparisons did not reach significance: t(23)=−0.16, p>.85 and t(23)=0.58, p>.55. Their cross-experiment reward-training interaction was also not significant. These null comparisons are not formal demonstrations of equivalence; likewise, a significant selection-history result in one task versus a nonsignificant result elsewhere does not establish that tasks differ. The experiments differed in training length, delay, feedback, deadlines and payment. Anderson and Halpern discuss these as plausible influences, not experimentally established explanations of the discrepancy.

Together these papers support a **conditional**, not absolute, inference: a reward-history contrast is measurable in some tasks; previous selection can also bias performance without monetary reward; and relative contributions depend on design and uncertainty. Avoid using the reward-versus-absent comparison alone as a pure measure of reward history.

## 2. Persistence is possible, not unconditional

[Anderson and Yantis (2013)](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0030860) retested **13** returnees 7–9 months after prior training, without additional reward learning. They report high-vs-low distractor t(12)=2.45, p=.031, and high-vs-absent t(12)=3.79, p=.003. Ten of the 13 had participated in the original 2011 experiments; these are not independent participants from that work. All reported no memory of which color had been high-value when asked after the task. That retrospective question is evidence about **explicit contingency recall**, not proof of unconscious visual experience. The small, selected returnee sample, mixed prior protocols and previous test exposure limit generalization; persistence after a delay does not mean the behavioral effect is permanently resistant to extinction.

[Milner, MacLean and Giesbrecht (2023)](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13414-022-02621-0) studied test context after a one-week delay. Their reward color was p=.8 rewarded on correct training trials; the other previously selected color was unrewarded. The authors use Bayesian support intervals and Bayes factors relative to a specified region of practical equivalence, **not ordinary 95% confidence intervals**. Those results depend on their model, priors and null-region definition.

- In experiment 1 (analyzed N=20), previously rewarded colors remained **targets**, and responses were faster for them; the authors note this is not necessarily involuntary capture. The difference declined across repeated unrewarded trials but remained supported in the last 100.
- With reward-colored features as irrelevant distractors, experiments 2a, 2b, 3 and 4 did not provide convincing overall reward-versus-unrewarded evidence in their stated models; experiment 3 nevertheless showed a reward × trial pattern. For example, experiment 4 (N=19) reported reward vs unrewarded b=−14.96 ms, support interval [−32.55, 1.31], BF=.13 against their specified null region. These are model- and context-specific results, not proof that the effect is identically zero.
- Experiment 5 (N=35) combined a shape-singleton target with absent trials. The direct reward-versus-unrewarded model reported b=−15.92 ms, support interval [−26.38, −5.34], BF=6.03, and a reward × trial coefficient b=19.30, support interval [6.21, 32.15], BF=2.59, indicating a reduction in the difference over the long test. Coding yields a negative coefficient for slower rewarded-distractor trials; it is not a claim that reward made RT faster. Their last-100-trial reward-vs-unrewarded BF=.55 was only anecdotal support for the null, not proof of complete extinction. Test comprised 1,600 trials, with 400 reward-distractor exposures, much more than many earlier tests.

Their comparisons motivate the hypothesis that singleton salience and distractor frequency matter. However, studies differed in more than those features, including number of training trials, and we have not identified a direct factorial interaction isolating each feature. A positive experiment 5 versus weaker experiments is not itself proof that **both features are necessary**. It is evidence against treating strong persistence or extinction resistance as universal.

## 3. What the 2026 data say about reward versus selection

The earlier [independent reproduction](SHELAT_2026_ANALYSIS_CODE_AND_MODEL_REPRODUCTION.md) fits the shared data using a random-intercept logistic Laplace model close to the authors' reported formula, with a numeric, uncentered block, neutral distractor reference, and fast trigger reference. The reward coefficient by itself is the reward-minus-neutral contrast at **block 0**, not a direct reward-versus-selected-unrewarded comparison. The reward and selection colors were both former training targets, making their comparison relevant to reward history; this still does not identify reward alone separately from the associated feedback, strategy and test context.

We extended the [Python implementation](verification/shelat_2026_laplace_accuracy_reproduction.py) to calculate linear contrasts at observed blocks using the full fixed-effect covariance, normal Wald intervals and two-sided tests. This is explicitly retrospective after seeing the paper/model, **not preregistered**, with 16 contrasts per full model and no multiple-testing correction; the 2011/2018 outcomes are RTs whereas these are conditional log odds of accuracy on retained, triggered rare trials. The [aggregate output](verification/shelat_2026_laplace_accuracy_reproduction.json) also includes individual-experiment fits, but these are not independent confirmations of a post hoc contrast.

| Pooled full model, fast trigger | Reward − neutral, b [95% Wald CI] | Reward − selected-unrewarded, b [95% Wald CI] |
|---|---|---|
| Block 1 | 0.420 [0.187, 0.652] | 0.186 [−0.047, 0.420] |
| Block 2 | 0.268 [0.116, 0.421] | 0.096 [−0.056, 0.248] |
| Block 3 | 0.117 [−0.041, 0.275] | 0.006 [−0.155, 0.166] |
| Block 4 | −0.034 [−0.278, 0.210] | −0.084 [−0.334, 0.165] |

The block-1 reward-versus-selection p is .118; other slow/fast, block-specific unadjusted contrasts are in the aggregate JSON. The fact that reward–neutral is individually significant while reward–selection is not **does not itself prove a difference between these two contrasts**, or show that reward and selection are equivalent. Log-odds contrasts are conditional on the model, not percentage-point effects. They do not isolate the proposed recoupling mechanism; colored distractors in the 2026 task also signal rare trials, so warning and response-preparation alternatives remain relevant.

## 4. Evidence and experiential boundary

These visual-search and sustained-attention studies concern behavioral selection and, in some separate studies referenced by the authors, gaze; none of the selected experiments above directly measures same-window phenomenal conjunction or subject incidence. Reward effects, their extinction and a working-memory correlation do not decide whether a divided brain has one or multiple experiencing subjects. A reward-induced attentional benefit or cost need not correspond to experiential ownership, and a lack of correct response does not establish absence of experience. The P/C/L alternatives remain underdetermined as set out in the [private nested fields comparison](PRIVATE_NESTED_FIELDS_AND_EXPERIENTIAL_CONTENT_COMPARISON.md).

The 2026 experiment-2 registration remains inaccessible, and our 2026 contrast calculation does not resolve its contents, the original R pipeline's exact execution, the learning-magnitude model's reported nonconvergence, or any causal neural mechanism. No survey or participant-level data are saved in this branch. Re-run the 2026 fit and focused tests using the commands in the [model reproduction report](SHELAT_2026_ANALYSIS_CODE_AND_MODEL_REPRODUCTION.md); an archive-backed test recomputes all contrasts as well as coefficients.
