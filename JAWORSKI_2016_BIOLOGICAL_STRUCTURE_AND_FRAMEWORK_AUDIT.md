# Jaworski on biological structure: assessment and relevance to the framework

## Bottom line

William Jaworski's [“Why Materialism Is False, and Why It Has Nothing To Do with the Mind”](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0031819116000036) (2016) makes a valuable case that **the organization of a living system is not dispensable from an adequate account of what it does and how it persists**. His examples challenge explanations that list material components while omitting their changing arrangement and interactions. This is directly relevant to our framework's search for concrete, dynamically organized subjects within a larger whole.

But the paper does **not establish that every physicalist account fails**, that biological organization is nonphysical, or that organization constitutes experience. In fact, Jaworski explicitly grants that his “structure realism” is compatible with materialism if physics includes irreducible structures (p. 206). His further hylomorphic claim—that specifically biological structures cannot be exhaustively described or explained by physics—is the critical extra premise, not a consequence of the fact that organization matters.

For our framework the strongest contribution is a **constraint on candidate explanations**, not confirmation of the proposed experiential, timeless, necessary, plural-subject whole. It reinforces the need to explain realized organization and persistence; it leaves phenomenal incidence and quality, fundamental time, necessity, and actuality open. Jaworski's suggestion that subjectivity was “invented” (pp. 191–192) also needs separate scrutiny: a critique of a Cartesian theory of subjectivity does not by itself eliminate actual experience, which this project takes as a datum.

## 1. What the argument actually says

**Definition.** Jaworski uses a strong materialism: in principle physics alone can exhaustively describe and explain every individual, feature and behavior. In a footnote he distinguishes weaker claims, including composition from physical parts and supervenience on physical properties (pp. 183–184). His argument should be assessed against this stated target, not automatically generalized to every theory called physicalism.

**Against the mind-first route.** In section 1 (pp. 184–192), he reconstructs Nagel's argument that accounting for life's origins depends on accounting for consciousness, reason and value. Jaworski challenges the necessity of that linkage: for example, a theorist might explain basic biological processes first and address consciousness through additional principles later (pp. 188–190). He questions whether Nagel's natural teleology eliminates rather than relocates unexplained psychophysical connections (p. 191). This challenges a particular proposed inference, not the reality or importance of conscious experience.

**The positive case.** Jaworski argues in sections 2–4:

1. Biological investigation successfully describes organisms by appeal to organization, including active, environmental and materially changing patterns, not merely static geometry (pp. 192–199).
2. As a working assumption, **ontological naturalism** gives prima facie ontological weight to entities needed by our best empirical descriptions, explanations and methods (pp. 200–201). He does not attempt a full defense of that assumption.
3. Structure is a real principle of unity, persistence and powers, not just an expression of our interests (pp. 199, 205). He argues for this using scientific success, a burden-of-proof comparison, and responses to parsimony objections (pp. 209–213).
4. His hylomorphism adds that some biological structures cannot be exhaustively described or explained by physics, even in principle (pp. 206–208). If this added claim and the strong definition of materialism are granted, the strong materialism is false.

He compares **structure eliminativism**, **structure reductivism**, **nonreductive structure antirealism**, and **structure realism** (pp. 201–206). In his usage reductivists identify structures with conditions exhaustively describable *without* structure, and nonreductive antirealists attribute the usefulness of structure talk to interests while retaining a world exhaustively describable without structure. His **structure realism is not the structural realism of scientific-realism debates**, which he explicitly distinguishes (pp. 193, 205 n. 32). Hylomorphism is his particular further position within structure realism, not a synonym for realism about any structure. He also warns that his version need not match every other philosopher's hylomorphism (pp. 206–207 n. 34).

## 2. What the evidence supports—and the central gap

### Organization is not a mere inventory

Jaworski's thought experiment (p. 194) asks us to compare a living person with the same materials after their organization has been destroyed. The intended contrast is between **the same material inventory** and different organization, not between two systems with the same complete physical state. It usefully shows why listing component types and quantities does not identify the organism or explain its capacities. The examples of renewal, metabolism and changing constituent matter likewise put pressure on an account that treats an organism as a fixed lump (pp. 197–199).

This is a substantive insight, but it does not establish that physical descriptions cannot include configuration, interaction, state, environment, history and dynamics. Those are not the same as a list of isolated constituents. Nor does a power possessed by a whole but not by a part *in isolation* establish a power additional to all the parts **as organized and interacting**. Whole-system and component-level explanations can be distinct without being rivals for the same causal role.

### Three claims must not be collapsed

| Claim | Assessment |
|---|---|
| **Organization is real and causally/explanatorily relevant.** | Strongly motivated: a disrupted or reorganized system can behave differently. Its role must still be specified in each case. |
| **Biological explanation is not practically replaceable by a list of microdetails.** | Plausible; higher-level models can reveal stable, counterfactual-supporting regularities and functional distinctions. Practical or explanatory autonomy need not by itself establish fundamental ontological independence. |
| **Even a complete, appropriately structured physical description cannot in principle describe or explain the relevant biological organization.** | Jaworski argues for this hylomorphic claim, but the examples and scientific success alone do not establish it. It requires a further account of what physical resources exclude, which biological facts cannot be recovered, and why. |

The last distinction matters especially because Jaworski acknowledges that if current or future physics postulates real irreducible structure, **structure realism and materialism can both be true** (p. 206). His concluding inference (p. 213) is best read as an inference to a preferred hylomorphic explanation of biological structure, not a deduction from structure realism alone. On that interpretation his position is not simply inconsistent; rather, the evidence offered for realism about structure does not by itself choose between physicalist and hylomorphic versions.

### A live physicalist rival

A physicalist can be a realist about organisms, interactions and organizational patterns while treating them as physically realized, without insisting that they are identified with **unstructured** conditions or exist only because observers are interested in them. This is a serious option not clearly captured by the three alternatives as Jaworski formulates them (pp. 201–205). The two approaches could agree that a particular regulatory network exists, that disrupting it changes an organism's capacities, and that a biological explanation makes a difference, while disagreeing about whether the network's organization is fundamentally beyond physics.

This rival is **not automatically correct**. It still owes a clear account of realization, system boundaries, physical relations and why biological explanations succeed. Jaworski's burden-of-proof point cuts both ways: he need not present a complete physical paraphrase to criticize eliminativism, but a thesis of biological structures beyond all possible physics likewise needs support beyond the fact that biology uses organizational terms. Exact reduction, loose functional realization, supervenience, and intertheoretic explanation are different proposals; failure of a simple one-to-one vocabulary translation does not refute all of them.

The issue also depends on what “physics” means. If it means laws and categories alone without actual state, environmental conditions or relational arrangement, lack of a biological description is unsurprising; if it includes suitable states, relations and dynamics, the paper has not shown in principle exclusion. Conversely, simply calling any biological fact “physical” does not demonstrate a derivation or make a disputed metaphysical dependence true. A physicalist's completeness claim itself needs defense.

For the sums `S_A=x₁+x₂` and `S_B=x₃+x₄`, the microscopic equations imply, for **all** microscopic initial states,

```text
dS_A/dt = 2ε(S_B − S_A)
dS_B/dt = 2ε(S_A − S_B).
```

The checked intertwining `P L = L_M P` ensures exact aggregate closure for that partition. Weak cross-pair coupling also produces distinct internal and between-pair relaxation scales. The resulting macro-description is genuinely informative about the model's organization; it is not a list of isolated parts. Yet it is derived from stipulated microdynamics and the coupling graph. It illustrates one mathematical possibility in which an exact higher-level law and a structured lower-level account coexist; it does **not** prove that all biological systems admit such a closure, that macro-organization is always derivative, or that living organisms are diffusion networks. The partition, variables, laws and applicability were inputs, not derived from the framework's ultimate constitution.

This example is not a refutation of hylomorphism: Jaworski could deny that the model captures the relevant irreducible biological unity. It does show why “the whole has powers its isolated parts lack” alone does not settle the metaphysical comparison. A stronger comparison would specify a concrete biological organization, the relevant explanatory task, and the rival physicalist and hylomorphic accounts. ## 4. Consequences for our framework

### A. Concrete dynamic organization

Jaworski supports the direction of the [concrete constitution account](constitutive-reality-thesis.md): actual organization, interactions, environmental exchange and maintenance matter. This also aligns with the earlier [Noble audit](DENIS_NOBLE_ORDER_DISORDER_EVOLUTION_AND_EXPERIENCE_AUDIT.md), which distinguishes context-sensitive regulation from a mere appeal to “order.” The framework should specify the physical or relational processes involved, their operating conditions, and what interventions change, not treat a graph or an abstract label as a living system.

A fundamentally timeless whole is not ruled out by biological dynamism: an account could represent ordered local processes relationally. But Jaworski's organism example does not derive timelessness or local clock structure, and a static diagram alone would not explain how biological maintenance and succession are represented.

### B. Unity, boundary and persistence are separate obligations

Jaworski treats structure as a principle of unity and persistence through changing matter (pp. 198–199, 207–208). This is an important challenge to the framework's treatment of local subjects. A proposed rule must explain which interactions belong to an individual, how boundaries respond to damage and environmental coupling, and how continuity is maintained as constituents change. A distinction between **organism persistence**, **continuity of psychological capacities**, and **persistence or recurrence of an experiencing subject** remains necessary: they need not coincide. Fission, fusion, replacement and overlapping processes also require specified treatment; the paper's material-flow analogy does not uniquely answer them.

Our existing four-component modules illustrate possible functional boundaries, not subject boundaries. Stability, causal integration or collective function by themselves do not determine whether one or more episodes of experience occur. The [constitution thesis](constitutive-reality-thesis.md#5-interventions-causal-integration-and-subject-boundaries) already treats this as an open problem.

### C. Experience cannot be read off biology

The paper deliberately argues from biological organization rather than claiming that life or self-maintenance suffices for experience. Organizational realism alone supplies neither an account of **which systems have experience**, nor who experiences, nor why a state has a particular qualitative character. Conversely, rejecting Jaworski's hylomorphism would not prove that physicalism explains experience. Our candidate maps from a concrete constitution `C` to public organization, subject/episode incidence and qualitative character remain obligations, not consequences of biological examples. See the [unified constitution map](UNIFIED_CONSTITUTION_MAP.md#2-where-the-current-constitution-work-sits).

On pp. 191–192 Jaworski proposes that “subjectivity” might have been invented as a residue of a historical objective/subjective conceptual division, and calls a Cartesian conception a theoretical posit. That passage can be read as criticism of **a particular theory** of subjectivity, not automatically a denial that anything is felt; it is not clear enough on its own to resolve his complete view of phenomenality. But if interpreted to deny actual experience altogether, it would conflict with this framework's governing datum. The framework must not infer that real pain is merely pain-related behavior or that the biological usefulness of pain capacity settles pain's unpleasant quality. Nor does Jaworski's discussion of the usefulness of pain capacity (p. 188) establish that every episode of pain is good or ethically desirable.

### D. Multiple realization remains open

Jaworski's dynamic structure is not just a static spatial shape or a numerical identity of material parts. That fits the project's distinction between a **realized** organization and a mere abstract specification. It does not establish that biological chemistry is indispensable for every experience, nor that computational or nonbiological realizations suffice. Both are competing claims requiring a criterion of relevant causal powers and phenomenal constitution. See the [substrate-independence audit](substrate-independence-thesis.md#3-the-substrate-dispute-needs-more-than-two-labels).

### E. No shortcut to the global ontology

Nothing in the article establishes that reality as a whole is intrinsically experiential, that possibilities are actual, that the whole exists necessarily, or that there are multiple subjects rather than one. A real organism-level unity is not automatically the unity of a cosmic subject; equally, it does not refute an encompassing non-subject whole. The paper discusses local living organization, not the project's entire metaphysical architecture.

## 5. How to make the comparison sharper

A useful next investigation would choose a well-characterized self-maintaining biological or artificial system and predefine:

1. **The system and organization:** its boundary, material flows, internal interactions, external resources, timescales and a measurable maintained capacity.
2. **Competing explanations:** an explicitly specified microphysical/open-system account, a mechanistic organizational account, and a hylomorphic interpretation. Say what each treats as fundamental and what each derives; allow a physicalist to be a realist about organization.
3. **Intervention and accounting:** a feasible, ethical perturbation that alters a selected interaction or regulatory pathway, with appropriate matched controls, leakage and environmental conditions recorded, and a measured recovery or failure outcome. A real perturbation changes physical conditions; it is not an intervention on organization while magically holding every microphysical fact fixed.
4. **Separation of claims:** assess predictive accuracy, causal intervention, robustness and explanatory compression separately from ontological fundamentality. If two ontologies predict the same observations, those observations alone cannot distinguish them. A claimed additional organizational causal effect under otherwise complete matched physical conditions would require a precise definition, a closure assumption, independent measurements and tests; it should not be attributed to Jaworski without a more explicit model.
5. **Phenomenal bridge, if relevant:** an independently justified experience/subject criterion with validated access and limitations. Biological maintenance, behavioral recovery and mechanism alone do not establish phenomenal character.

This is a proposed comparison, not a claim that the 2016 paper performed that experiment or that such an experiment can by itself settle metaphysical grounding. Its value is to prevent genuine organizational explanation, extra physical dynamics, and experiential constitution from being silently conflated.

## Overall judgment

**Retain:** structure, material exchange, realized powers, multilevel organization and persistence are central explanatory targets. **Do not infer:** the falsity of all physicalism, a separate vital force, the identity of life and experience, or the framework's preferred global metaphysics. The paper improves the questions our framework must answer, but it does not provide the missing answers.
