# Anderson, Laurent and Yantis 2011: what value-driven capture does and does not show

## Identification and scope

The supplied `Value-driven_attentional_capture.pdf` is Brian A. Anderson, Patryk A. Laurent and Steven Yantis, “Value-driven attentional capture,” *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences* **108**(25), 10367–10371 (2011), [doi:10.1073/pnas.1104047108](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1104047108). It is **not** the inaccessible experiment-2 registration for the 2026 Shelat and Giesbrecht study. The PDF has SHA-256 `b217a38de67740fdf1f9a778b371388e1ae0fa4fb5dae0ddeba940b06442e2be`. All five pages were read as extracted text; figures 1–2, tables 1–2 and methods were visually checked on pages 2–4. This is a retrospective reading, not an independent data/code reproduction; see the [reading plan](ANDERSON_LAURENT_YANTIS_2011_VALUE_CAPTURE_READING_PLAN.md).

**Later follow-up:** a [targeted review of later direct tests](VALUE_CAPTURE_REWARD_SELECTION_PERSISTENCE_FOLLOWUP.md) found a 2017 reanalysis reporting the original 2011 pooled high–low contrast, a new direct group comparison, and a 2018 within-person rewarded-versus-unrewarded contrast, alongside contrasting and task-dependent evidence. The original 2011 paper did not itself report that direct high–low test; its small control should not be treated as the whole subsequent literature.

**Bottom line:** the authors report that formerly rewarded but currently irrelevant colors slowed an unrelated shape-search task, including after a short training regimen and, among returning participants, a delay. A training-without-reward group and a no-training group are useful controls, but their nonsignificant results alone do not prove zero effects or a statistically established between-group difference. The study provides behavioral evidence compatible with reward-history-dependent attentional prioritization. It does not directly measure subjective awareness, phenomenal unity, hemispheric integration, or the number or identity of experiencing subjects.

## 1. The experiment and what was manipulated

In experiment 1, **26** Johns Hopkins participants searched for a red or green circle, one per trial, and identified the orientation of a line inside it. On correct responses, the high-value target color was followed by 5¢ on 80% and 1¢ on 20% of trials; the other color received these amounts with reversed probabilities. Thus **both** colors were rewarded, but one had a higher expected reward. The high-value color was counterbalanced red/green across participants. Training had **1,008** trials; participants were not explicitly told the contingency. Color identified the target but did not determine which response key was correct.

In the subsequent **480-trial** test, participants searched for a shape singleton and reported its line orientation; target shapes were never red or green. On half the trials a nontarget was red or green, each equally often; on the remainder neither trained color appeared. Participants were told color was irrelevant and no monetary reward feedback was provided, although correct/incorrect feedback remained. Displays had six differently colored items, with target identity, target and distractor location, and distractor color crossed/counterbalanced. In the reported RT analysis, only correct responses were included and RTs more than three within-subject/condition SDs from the mean were excluded. These exclusions define the estimated RT effect; the report does not make it an all-trials effect.

This tests persistence of a **joint history of target selection and differential reward**, not a reward-only association with a never-selected feature. The different expected rewards for two formerly selected colors, along with the counterbalanced assignment, help distinguish reward magnitude from mere prior selection, but particular higher-versus-lower contrasts need their own statistical support. Instructions and the change of task make intentional color search counterproductive; they do not directly establish that every attentional or response process was involuntary, nor rule out all lingering task sets or strategic effects.

## 2. Reported results

All numbers in this section are the authors' published results, not newly fitted estimates. RTs below are condition means in milliseconds; table parentheses in the paper are **within-subject SEM**, not between-subject SD or a confidence interval for the contrasts.

| Sample and test | N | Neither trained-color distractor | Low-value color | High-value color | Reported test |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---|
| Experiment 1, 1,008 training trials | 26 | 665 | 673 | 681 | three-condition F(2,50)=6.07, p=.004; high vs neither t(25)=3.49, p=.002 |
| Experiment 3, 240 training trials | 24 | 667 | 675 | 682 | three-condition F(2,46)=5.17, p=.009; linear trend F(1,23)=14.09, p=.001 |
| Experiment 3, delayed retest | 19 returnees | 614 | 624 | 630 | three-condition F(2,36)=5.81, p=.007; linear trend F(1,18)=11.55, p=.003 |

The high-versus-neither mean differences from printed means are approximately **16, 15 and 16 ms**, respectively. These are descriptive differences between conditions, not proportions, awareness measures or numerical estimates of a person's “lost consciousness.” Experiment 3 also changed trial deadlines and doubled reward amounts to 2¢/10¢, so its outcome does not isolate training length. The retest occurred 4–21 days after initial training (mean 8.8, SD 3.9), without additional training; the 19 returnees had already completed the earlier extinction test, and selective return and practice/exposure matter. A result on retest alone is not proof that effect magnitude was unchanged: no direct initial-versus-delay comparison is established here.

The printed pattern is ordered neither < low < high. The paper reports a linear trend, but we do not treat that alone as proof that the **low–high pair** is independently significant: an equally spaced linear contrast over three conditions also reflects the endpoint comparison. No direct high–low test is reported in the main text. Experiment 1 reported no significant error-rate difference among conditions, F(2,50)=0.41, p=.667; experiment 3 reported no significant error differences in the initial or delayed tests. This is not proof of equivalent error rates or that speed–accuracy tradeoffs are absent.

The authors also report that the fastest 25% of RTs were slower for high-value distractor than no trained-color distractor, t(25)=3.07, p=.005. This argues against an effect confined only to the slowest tail, but does not demonstrate capture on every single trial or identify a person's attentional state on a given trial.

## 3. Controls and spatial evidence

- **No-training visual-salience control:** eight new participants performed only the test; mean RTs were 698 ms with neither color, 696 red and 700 green, F(2,14)=0.13, p=.880. This is consistent with no large inherent red/green disadvantage under that setup, but n=8 and nonsignificance do not prove physical or psychological equivalence.
- **Training without monetary trial feedback:** ten other new participants trained and tested similarly, with correctness feedback but no per-trial monetary reward and a flat $25 payment. Table 2 gives 602 ms with neither color, 606 red and 593 green; the authors report t(9)=−0.39, p=.707 for the distractor effect. This condition is a useful challenge to the idea that former target status alone suffices, but the paper's “completely abolished” and “ruled out” language is stronger than a small nonsignificant control justifies. The quoted t is as described in the text; we have not reconstructed its precise contrast. A direct reward-training × distractor-condition comparison with uncertainty, not significance in one group and nonsignificance in another, is needed to establish their difference. Reward feedback also changes motivation and feedback history, so it is not a pure measurement of one neural process.
- **Location-specific sequential effect:** for a no-distractor test trial following a high-value-distractor trial, the authors report responses averaging **66 ms slower** when the new target appeared at the previous distractor location versus another location, t(23)=3.13, p=.005. This is compatible with a spatial aftereffect, interpreted as capture followed by inhibition of return, and is more specific than a purely spatially uniform slowing account. It is an indirect sequential behavioral measure, not direct observation of gaze or attention at the previous location; prior-trial and selection effects and the details of this comparison deserve further study. The text gives df=23 but does not explicitly identify the sample for this particular analysis in that sentence.

## 4. Working memory, impulsivity and generalization

The authors measured visual working-memory capacity by change detection, and BIS-11 trait impulsivity. In experiment 1, a simultaneous regression predicted high-value-minus-neither RT slowing with R²=.355, model p=.006; standardized coefficients were β=−.554 (p=.004) for capacity and β=.378 (p=.038) for impulsivity. Figure 2 reports Pearson capacity correlations of r=−.468 in experiment 1 (p=.016) and r=−.468 in experiment 3 (p=.021). Experiment 3's impulsivity correlation was r=.093, p=.666; this is not evidence of no true association and does not by itself establish that the impulsivity relationships differ across studies. The experiment-1 regression coefficient and experiment-3 simple correlation are different quantities. These small-sample individual-difference findings are associative, not proof that capacity or impulsivity causes distraction, addiction or any clinical condition. The no-distractor RT correlations also being nonsignificant does not eliminate all possible baseline or measurement confounds.

The article discusses addiction, obesity, ADHD and OCD as possible applications. Its healthy community samples and laboratory monetary colors do **not** test diagnosis, clinical treatment, generalization to those disorders, or a shared clinical causal mechanism. The paper reports behavioral RT and accuracy, not neural recordings, eye movements, subjective moment-to-moment reports or direct measures of conscious experience.

## 5. Relation to Shelat and Giesbrecht 2026 and the unity question

The 2026 paper itself cites Anderson et al. as prior work on reward-history capture. Both use earlier colored-target learning followed by a task with irrelevant colored distractors and a shape-defined target. Their contrasts and outcomes are nevertheless different:

| | Anderson et al. 2011 | Shelat and Giesbrecht 2026 |
|---|---|---|
| Training | Both red/green targets rewarded, with different expected amounts | One color rewarded on 75% of correct trials, another selected but unrewarded; third absent |
| Test | Distractor on half of trials; mean correct-response visual-search RT | Four distractor conditions on behaviorally triggered *rare* orientation trials in a sustained-attention task; primary recoupling outcome is accuracy |
| Claim of interest | Previously rewarded colors can be distracting during unrewarded search | Reward-history distractors may alter rare-response accuracy depending on behavioral trigger/state; exploratory pooled RT slowing also reported |
| State / registration | No three-response fast/slow triggering or same-trial mind-wandering measure in the supplied report | Fast/slow preceding RTs are behavioral proxies, not direct phenomenal reports; experiment-2 registration remains inaccessible to us |

The 2011 paper makes the later capture premise more intelligible: **a cue can carry learned priority even when it is currently irrelevant**. It neither automatically validates the 2026 recoupling interpretation nor contradicts an accuracy benefit there: slowing a search response in one task and altered accuracy on rare-response trials in another are different outcomes. In the 2026 design, colored distractors also predict rare trials, permitting warning/anticipation or response-policy alternatives; the absence of increasing block trends does not exclude early or stable learning. See the [2026 paper audit](SHELAT_GIESBRECHT_2026_SUSTAINED_ATTENTION_AUDIT.md) and [independent accuracy-model follow-up](SHELAT_2026_ANALYSIS_CODE_AND_MODEL_REPRODUCTION.md).

Neither 2011 nor 2026 is a split-brain experiment. Prioritized processing, distraction, working-memory correlation, and a location-specific aftereffect do not establish which contents occur together in one phenomenal field, whether distinct neural processes share one experience token, or whether there are one or multiple subjects. The stipulated L-exclusive, C-full, C-layered and private P cases in the [private-field comparison](PRIVATE_NESTED_FIELDS_AND_EXPERIENTIAL_CONTENT_COMPARISON.md) remain underdetermined: P specifically uses distinct private tokens even where content types correspond. No participant-to-four-spin mapping or subject-incidence rule is supplied by the attentional findings.

## 6. Remaining access and next steps

The supplied 2011 paper was read; its underlying participant-level dataset, experimental code, preregistration and any supplementary material were not supplied or independently inspected, and no 2011 numerical reproduction was performed. An informative next empirical step would directly compare reward versus no-reward training with a prespecified group × distractor interaction and uncertainty, include never-selected reward-related and selection-only conditions where feasible, and use eye tracking and time-anchored experience reports to separate gaze, behavioral interference and reported awareness. Even that would not by itself establish subject identity or phenomenal unity.
