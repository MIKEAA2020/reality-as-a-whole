# Split-brain dual-task and divided-cue evidence: primary-source audit

## Result in brief

The supplied full papers support **different, task-specific behavioral constraints**, not a single established attention mechanism or a count of experiencing subjects. In Holtzman and Gazzaniga's 1982 memory-probe task, the one patient was **10 percentage points more accurate** when the other field repeated one item than when it displayed the same three-item sequence as the probed field; the two controls showed the reverse pattern. Arguin et al. 2000 found a divided-cue RT benefit in their pooled callosotomy sample, and six of ten patients' individual benefits departed from their controls' distribution. Four of those six had anterior, not total, sections. These are genuine results with different designs and limitations, not contradictory measurements of the same quantity.

A further source discovery also matters: Arguin's comparison with an earlier *spatial-cue* result is **Holtzman, Volpe and Gazzaniga 1984**, not Holtzman and Gazzaniga's 1982 *dual-task memory* experiment. The 1984 chapter is now available in the [authors' archive](https://people.psych.ucsb.edu/gazzaniga/PDF/Spatial%20Orientation%20following%20commissural%20section%20(1984).pdf), and its extracted chapter text was read, including its first experiment. JW's 1,500-ms result and Arguin's 500-ms result come from different protocols; they do **not** establish a universal 1,000-ms boundary or isolate SOA as the cause. The chapter's text extraction dropped part of a subsequent experiment and its figures were not visually inspected; no full graphical audit is claimed.

## 1. The 1982 bilateral sequence task: what actually varied

**Participants and task.** One commissurotomy patient, V.P., selected for bilateral language, and two neurologically intact observers completed 576 trials each (p. 1326; note 7 explains why two other available patients were not tested). Three pairs of geometric forms were presented sequentially on both sides of fixation; each display was 150 ms, with a 500-ms interstimulus interval. After a one-second delay, a 150-ms unilateral probe prompted a single forced-choice keypress: did it match any item in the *probed field*? Visual fields, positive/negative probes, and responding hand were balanced; hands were counterbalanced across 48-trial blocks, and conditions randomized within blocks. Both fields were stimulated on every trial; one response was required. The authors sought to limit stimulus-feature swapping and simultaneous response interference, not to prove that every later stage or all routes are independent.

**Conditions and results** (Table 1, collapsed over field and hand; latency means ± SEM in ms, correct trials only):

| Condition | Probed field | Other field | V.P. correct; latency | Controls correct; latency |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Redundant-3 | Three distinct forms | Same three in same order | 64%; 1706 ± 49 | 96%; 922 ± 24 |
| Mixed-3 | Three distinct forms | One form repeated three times | 74%; 1618 ± 65 | 86%; 1034 ± 26 |
| Mixed-1 | One form repeated | Three forms | 87%; 1480 ± 79 | 94%; 912 ± 17 |

The principal patient contrast is mixed-3 versus redundant-3: 74% versus 64%, `χ²(1)=4.11, p<.05`; latency difference `t(288)=1.08`, not significant. The mixed-1 row also changes the *probed* load and is not the clean other-field-load contrast. No confidence interval for the 10-point difference is given. The two control observers individually showed fewer errors and faster responses in redundant-3, consistent with using redundant information across fields; the authors report patient-by-condition interactions against each control (note 8). These are repeated trials in one patient and two controls, not a population estimate.

**Controls and interpretation.** When the stimuli were repeated and V.P. explicitly judged whether the displays in the two fields were the same, accuracy was 45/96, not above chance; controls made no errors. An additional experiment (note 9, 192 trials per observer) compared redundant-3 with *different three-item sequences* in the two fields: V.P. 73% versus 77% correct, 1694 ± 71 versus 1860 ± 100 ms; neither comparison significant. Controls were 96% versus 80% and 903 ± 24 versus 1180 ± 44 ms. This supports task-specific loss of the controls' redundancy benefit for V.P.; nonsignificance does not prove absence of all transfer or perfect equality.

The authors infer competition for shared processing resources, while explicitly allowing either subcortical pathways or the **unsectioned anterior commissure** and leaving time-sharing versus dedicated-operation limits unresolved (p. 1327). The title's “exclusively” is not a separately established anatomical or causal exclusion. Crucially, the 1982 contrast also changes interfield redundancy, not only nominal other-field load: identical bilateral items versus repeated unilateral form differ in cross-field congruence and available strategies. Stimulus interactions, encoding/decision or memory, arousal, shared output preparation, and preserved routes remain candidates; they are not demonstrated confounds. Single response reduces concurrent response interference but does not localize the source of the effect. The results do not show that both streams were experienced together by any particular subject.

## 2. Arguin 2000: divided-cue benefit and individual heterogeneity

**Sample and procedure** (pp. 285–287). Three epilepsy patients had total callosal sections, in two stages; seven had anterior 50–80% sections; ten neurologically intact students were controls. Cue pairs were presented for 250 ms with **500 ms cue-onset-to-target-onset**: both arrows toward the future target (valid focused), both away from it (invalid), arrows pointing outwards to opposite boxes (divided), or two equal signs (neutral). One X target appeared in one peripheral box and remained until a simple speeded response; it was not a two-target simultaneous-report task. Valid focused trials comprised 52%, each other condition 16%; targets were balanced across fields. Both hands rested on a lever for patients or a keyboard spacebar for controls, and either or both hands could respond. An experimenter visually checked fixation, without reported quantitative eye tracking; the authors argued that divided and neutral cues offered no different advance information about which field would contain the target. That argument does not quantify missed saccades or eliminate all cue-specific strategies.

Patient blocks had 100 trials, with RT <250 ms or >3000 ms rejected online and repeated (6.0% anticipations, 1.0% delayed). Controls had 150 trials, different equipment and post-hoc exclusion below 150 ms or above 1000 ms (5.6% and 0.2%); the footnote gives their faster responses as reason for the lower cutoff. These unequal devices, thresholds and replacement procedures, plus unequal trial frequencies, limit direct group comparisons. Target remained visible, so momentary hemifield exclusivity is not established by duration alone.

**Reported results** (pp. 287–289):

- Controls' cue effect `F(3,27)=9.0, p<.001`; valid versus neutral benefit `F(1,9)=15.3, p<.005`; invalid cost `F(1,9)=4.9, p=.05`. Divided 319 ms and neutral 318 ms, `F(1,9)<1`. Neither this nonsignificance nor the authors' interpretation proves all intact observers have one indivisible focus.
- Combining the two patient subgroups, cue main effect `F(3,24)=5.8, p<.005`; valid versus neutral `t(8)=2.5, p<.05`, divided versus neutral `t(8)=2.21, p<.05`. Valid versus divided `t(8)=0.02, n.s.`; invalid versus neutral `t(8)=1.0, n.s.` The total/anterior group-by-cue interaction `F(3,24)=1.5, n.s.` is not equivalence. The degrees of freedom reflect the subgroup analysis; no direct patient-versus-control interaction test for the divided benefit is reported in this section.
- For the individual divided benefit (neutral minus divided), control mean was **−1 ms**, SD **16 ms**. The paper reports six of ten patient z-scores outside the control distribution at its stated significance level: two of three total cases, z=8.0 and 8.6; four of seven anterior, z=2.5, 3.7, 6.8 and 11.7. These are deviations against a small estimated control distribution, not six independently measured subjective divisions or a proven population rate; multiple individual comparisons and unequal measurement regimes complicate interpretation. The remaining four nonsignificant cases do not prove absence of a benefit. For invalid-cue cost, none was individually reported outside the control distribution; this does not establish equality either.
- The smallest anterior section case, z=11.7, also scored **51/56** on separate intermanual tactile localization. This supports a spared cross-manual tactile ability despite the visual cue pattern; it does not establish normal function of every posterior connection or uniquely locate the cue effect. Fig. 3c illustrates that one patient; Fig. 3a/b are groups with distinct axis offsets, not three individual total cases. The exploratory correlations (age, IQ, epilepsy and surgery variables) were nonsignificant with a small sample; no inference of no association is justified.

The authors favor independently directed visuospatial mechanisms and hypothesize that separated frontal control of posterior attention explains anterior-section cases; they also speculate about individual preoperative differences in reliance on subcortical pathways. Neither proposal was causally isolated. A cue benefit with a single subsequent target suggests available preparation at the possible locations under these task contingencies, but does not directly measure two simultaneously conscious scenes, the actual focus on each trial, or distinct subject identities.

## 3. The separate 1984 JW cue experiment and apparent timing conflict

The archived 1984 chapter's first experiment (pp. 382–384; figure numbers 10.1–10.2) tested JW on **720 trials** with bilateral central arrow cues and an odd/even judgment of a briefly displayed digit. Focused, divided, neutral and invalid conditions were approximately 44%, 22%, 22% and 11%; cue and target were each 150 ms, target onset 1.5 seconds after cue onset. Roughly 6% were excluded for incorrect responses or latencies over 2.5 seconds. The authors describe divided-cue latencies as closer to neutral than focused, and propose integrated orienting. Reported SDs were 253 ms divided, 248 neutral, 271 focused and 310 invalid; these were used to challenge a simple mixture account, not to prove every trial used the same allocation. The chapter's abstracted text does not give exact condition means or an equivalence test.

The chapter reports two full callosotomy patients across its studies, with anterior commissures spared; the divided-cue experiment itself is **JW only**. Arguin identifies JW as also in Mangun et al.'s 1994 sample, a relevant partial within-person link, but the Mangun task used peripheral cues, different target decisions and SOAs; Arguin's own sample is not established as JW. Arguin contrasts 500 ms with 1,500 ms and suggests time-dependent interhemispheric integration. This is a cross-study hypothesis, not a controlled dose–response result: patient composition, cue arrangement, physical stimuli, response demands, cue frequencies, apparatus, exclusions and other parameters also differ. It does not establish that benefit disappears at **all** intervals ≥1 s or that one pathway mediates both findings. The chapter also presents location-specific cross-field cuing and poor explicit comparison for JW and PS (pp. 386–388); this is a further functional dissociation, not proof of a common phenomenally unified field.

## 4. 2004 Figure 1 visual check and 2009 comparison

The 2004 Figure 1A schematizes a single sequential, masked-target trial with a T1 at lower left and T2 at upper right, not four continuous RSVP streams. It illustrates one cross-field arrangement; it does **not by itself** show all same-field configurations. The prose explicitly places T2 in one of the remaining three locations, including in same-field trials. Thus the earlier correction stands: the 2004 method does not place both same-field targets at one location. Within-field and across-field distances/geometries nevertheless differ, and a location is not automatically an RSVP stream.

Panel B's four conditional `T2|T1` curves for JW show much lower values for LVF T2 (squares) than RVF T2 (circles), with a marked improvement over lag for the LVF-T2 curves and a much flatter, high RVF-T2 pattern. Panel C shows the six-control group means, with all four curves rising with lag at high accuracy. The legend identifies SEM bars. This visual check supports the qualitative account and the paper's reported marginal interaction; it does **not** yield exact cell counts, establish within/across equivalence or identify a causal mechanism. See the [blink audit](SPLIT_BRAIN_BLINK_2004_2009_COMPLETE_RESULTS_AUDIT.md) for the exact published marginal percentages, patient block-based statistics, controls, and Ptito 2009's differing joint-count outcome and 233-ms patient versus 150-ms control SOA.

## 5. Consequences for subject and quality constitution

The observations separate **(a)** a load-related single-probe accuracy change, **(b)** cue-related single-target RT facilitation and cross-field orienting, and **(c)** conditional or joint-count lag effects. These need not have the same mechanism. A common functional dependence can occur without shared stimulus contents, experienced centrality, one subject or common phenomenal tokens. Conversely, evidence for functional differentiation does not by itself prove distinct subjects or absence of a global experience. None measures the prespecified simultaneous experiential conjunction, subject ownership, physical selector or q-quality. First-person reports are pertinent independent evidence; failed or absent responses are not evidence of no experience.

For the stipulated comparison `(D,G,N)`, L-exclusive `(0,0,0)`, C-full `(1,1,0)`, C-layered `(0,1,0)` and P `(0,1,1)` remain conditional targets, not clinical outcomes. A warranted global conjunction could challenge the specified L-exclusive package; distinct-owner incidence would additionally matter for C-layered versus P. Under P, any local and global experiences are **numerically distinct private tokens belonging to distinct subjects**: matching type or content does not mean token sharing. Neither the attentional data nor the toy domain supplies a justified patient-to-four-spin mapping. L is not identified with IIT; no IIT value is calculated. No inference to q-dependent unpleasantness follows from visual RT.

For progress, prioritize a feasible, ethically governed **same patient / same task / same time window** design pairing calibrated, non-leading first-person judgments of experienced focus, periphery and conjunction with independent functional cue/lag and gaze/response controls, including uncertainty and meaningful nonresponse. Separately specify how distinct-owner claims could be discriminated without equating channels to subjects, and fix any physical-to-experience mapping before target results. These are research requirements, not an already validated measurement or justification for new surgery.
