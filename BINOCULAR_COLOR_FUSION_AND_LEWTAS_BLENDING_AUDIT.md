# Binocular color fusion is not fusion of subjects

## Finding

Binocular vision provides a useful **empirical analogue of a change in reported visual appearance**, not a demonstrated instance of Lewtas's mental chemistry. In suitably chosen displays, different colors presented to the two eyes can produce an intermediate or achromatic appearance, while subsequent eye-specific tests reveal information about the inputs. Other configurations produce a spatial composite or rivalry instead. These results make a simple, context-free rule such as “two inputs always yield their average” untenable. They do **not** show that two earlier subjects or experiences merged, that their experiences were destroyed, or why any resulting quality feels as it does.

The most informative step for the framework is to separate **input combination, observer-level appearance, qualitative constitution, and subject identity**. The experiments constrain the first two, not the latter two without additional bridge assumptions. This sharpens, but does not resolve, the [Lewtas audit](LEWTAS_2016_MENTAL_CHEMISTRY_AND_FRAMEWORK_AUDIT.md) and its [fusion/private-copy gate](LEWTAS_FUSION_PRIVATE_COPY_IDENTIFICATION_GATE.md).

This is a reading and arithmetic audit of published articles, not a reanalysis of participant-level data. The article text was read online; no independent stimulus calibration, original data, or replication was performed.

## 1. What Lewtas needs, and what the analogy changes

Lewtas distinguishes *choosing*, *placing* and *blending*: which experiences participate, where they belong in a subject's field, and how qualities might yield a different quality. He asks why red and yellow **experiences**, not merely paints or light, might constitute orange experience, and acknowledges that he has no satisfactory direct solution (user-supplied paper, printed advance pp. 30–34; PDF pp. 31–35). He argues that selected subjects can cease when their experiences transform, while allowing unselected experiences to persist. The empirical eyes in the studies below are **sensory input channels of an observer**, not demonstrated subjects with separately owned red and yellow experiences.

| Level | What can be tested or specified | What the observations do not by themselves establish |
|---|---|---|
| Physical input | Two measured, separately presented eye images; optics, timings, spectra and spatial structure | That each input has a subject or a felt quality |
| Observer-level appearance | Report, discrimination and matching under a defined task; an intermediate hue, a mosaic or a switch | Complete identity of experiences, or that both inputs were separately experienced before combination |
| Qualitative constitution | A hypothesis about which experiential ingredients and relations constitute a quality | A derivation of the feel of yellow or orange from neural combination, matching coordinates or labels |
| Subject incidence and identity | A specified rule for which subjects exist and persist through an interaction | A subject census or persistence verdict from two eyes, one report, an afterimage or an EEG classifier |

In other words, “fusion” in vision science usually names a **perceptual or computational outcome**. It is not equivalent to Lewtas's proposed fusion of subjects. His red/yellow-to-orange question remains even if a visual model predicts an intermediate color perfectly.

## 2. Color targets disappear but input-specific effects remain

[Anstis and Rogers (2012)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0042698911003889), *Vision Research* 53, 47–53, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.visres.2011.11.005), experiments 2–4 and 6, used a mirror stereoscope. For the target-discrimination experiments, spatially corresponding spots had reversed orange/lime target–surround assignments across eyes. There were four possible target orientations; monocular and binocular conditions were compared. Their designs used small positive-contrast spots and brief presentation to encourage fusion, not a universal condition for all dichoptic colors.

* **Experiment 2:** for eight observers, their staircase found a substantially larger chromatic separation at threshold for the opposed binocular configuration than for monocular views. The authors report a **6.5-fold** separation in CIE coordinates. This is a task-specific threshold, not a ratio of amounts of consciousness. The printed aggregate coordinates do not directly reproduce 6.5 under one simple distance calculation; see the source-quality check below.
* **Experiment 3:** in four observers the reported orientation accuracy was **90% left eye, 92% right eye, 25% binocular**, the last equal to the four-choice chance rate. Dynamic luminance noise was used to reduce luminance cues. This is performance in a particular display, not proof of zero visual experience or of universally inaccessible information.
* **Experiment 4:** yellow targets in both eyes were set against backgrounds presented orange to one eye and lime to the other. The authors report an elevated binocular threshold and interpret it as the background's intermediate binocular appearance approaching the directly presented yellow target. This is a discrimination result at threshold, **not a direct proof that all aspects of the two full experiences are identical**; lustre and other cues may remain, as the authors discuss. The figure caption says seven observers while the discussion summary says four; the count is unresolved here.
* **Experiment 6:** five observers viewed gradually introduced opposing magenta/green adapting spots, with a grid encouraging fusion; they reported a gray appearance rather than the adapting colors. After adaptation a white disk was flashed in one eye while the other saw black. The paper reports the predicted pink/green afterimage response for all **80 trials per observer**. This supports eye-specific adaptation effects revealed by the changed test, not concurrent persistence of two private color experiences. The probe changes the viewing condition and yields a later observer report; it is not a time machine or subject-identity assay.

The authors use “information discarded” for the binocular outcome but also demonstrate recoverable eye-specific effects. More carefully, **a feature may be inaccessible to the tested report or discrimination while stimulus-dependent processing persists**. Neither the result nor the later afterimage proves an unreported phenomenal subject exists; nor does non-report prove that one ceased.

## 3. Composite arrangements and rivalry are different outcomes

[Cha, Blake and Chong (2018)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5973937/), [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-018-26679-9), studied dichoptic arrays of colored circles. In experiment 1a, items in the two eyes were placed at **non-overlapping locations**; a composite binocular percept meant simultaneously visible elements from both arrays, not the creation of a single intermediate hue at a corresponding point. Participants held keys to report the visible color or colors during 90-second trials. Ten were analyzed after two of twelve were excluded because insufficient percept changes prevented duration estimates. The authors report longer composite episodes with similar colors, and 0.81 mean fraction of viewing time in that condition (SD 0.03); result categories depend on the report and exclusions. Experiment 1b manipulated item overlap, but also used 24 versus 48 circles per eye, so overlap is not the only change. Its shorter non-unitary episodes in the overlap condition are not a clean one-variable identification of overlap's causal effect.

In experiment 2, seven participants including two authors viewed three conditions, including arrays in which each eye received both red and green circles. Participants reported one or two brief probes. Correct probe-number discrimination was **conditional on at least one detected probe**; poorer detection/discrimination was interpreted as evidence of interocular suppression. This is a behavioral proxy with assumptions, not independent access to a suppressed eye's experience. The authors suggest that binocular ensemble likelihood influences rivalry; their ecological interpretation and proposed neural operations are explanatory hypotheses rather than direct measurements of a likelihood computation. Small samples, some author participants and condition-dependent changes limit generalization.

These studies concern different stimuli and tasks; they should not be combined into a numerical law. They show why at least three distinctions are necessary: **intermediate hue**, **simultaneous spatial arrangement of distinct elements**, and **alternation or suppression**. Calling all three “a blend” masks the very differences a theory must explain. Likewise a mixed report in the [Roy EEG exploration](ROY_MIXED_TRANSITION_CONTEXT_EXPLORATORY_RESULTS.md) is not demonstrated to be any one of these particular visual structures, nor a validated marker of nested subjects.

## 4. Neural processing does not settle qualitative presence

[Moutoussis and Zeki (2002)](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.142305699), *PNAS* 99, 9527–9532, studied seven participants using opposite color-contrast face/house images. Each eye saw a recognizable image, but the opposed binocular configuration usually prompted “nothing” in a face/house/nothing task; the uniform field and fixation were not literally absent. Brief ~50 ms flashes separated by ~150 ms gaps promoted fusion. Before scanning, two-alternative face/house performance was 98.2% for same-contrast images and 52.7% for opposite images (SDs 2.5 and 4.2 percentage points). During scanning, unexpected reports were modeled separately. Under opposite conditions, the authors reported stimulus-specific fMRI differences in face- and house-associated areas despite the absence of the corresponding face/house report, generally weaker than in same-contrast conditions.

These findings qualify a simple claim that a feature absent from report leaves no measurable processing. They do not prove that all relevant input was unconscious, that these regions are or are not constitutive of a percept, or that intensity alone causes consciousness. The same versus opposite displays differ physically, attention and decision may differ, fMRI has limited resolution, and analysis used fixed effects and specified corrections. The article's own broader neural conclusions go beyond the narrower observation. No inference about distinct subjects follows from category-specific activation.

## 5. Source-quality and arithmetic checks

The accompanying [script](verification/anstis_rogers_2012_reported_arithmetic.py), [tests](verification/anstis_rogers_2012_reported_arithmetic_test.py), and [receipt](verification/anstis_rogers_2012_reported_arithmetic.json) check **printed aggregate numbers**, not raw data or the validity of statistical tests.

1. Experiment 3's article HTML prints lime `(x,y)=(0.627,0.667)` and orange `(0.894,0.608)` as CIE chromaticities. Their sums are **1.294** and **1.502**. Under the usual `x=X/(X+Y+Z), y=Y/(X+Y+Z)` with nonnegative XYZ, each sum must be at most one. Thus the displayed numbers are inconsistent with that interpretation. We do not know whether this is an article, transcription, labeling or conversion error; do not use them to reconstruct the stimuli.
2. Taking Euclidean distances between the *rounded aggregate* experiment 2 and 4 xy coordinates in the article gives ratios about **6.038** and **4.034**, not the stated **6.5** and **4.67**. Ratios of aggregate coordinates need not equal mean individual ratios, and other analytic details or rounding could matter; the calculation does not show the paper's ratios are false. Raw data and the underlying computation would be needed to reconcile them.
3. For experiment 6, the article's displayed `2^−400 ≃ 10^120` has the wrong exponent sign: `2^−400 ≈ 3.87×10^−121`. Even that number is only the probability of 400 specified successes under an ideal independent fair-binary null, not a verified p-value for these repeated trials. Dependence, response biases and the design must be considered.
4. Experiment 4's sample-size conflict is noted above. These checks do not invalidate the reported phenomena or resolve replication; the paper's original figure images, underlying individual data and apparatus calibration have not been independently checked here.

## 6. A practical, explicitly conditional next comparison

A study could separately test a **specific visual response model**, without pretending to test subject fusion:

1. Independently measure each observer's color/luminance calibration and alignment. Randomize corresponding-point dichoptic pairs, same-eye physical-mixture controls, equal-input binocular controls, and monocular conditions. Include sufficiently different hues and spatial composites; keep durations, adaptation, fixation and contrast specified. Check rivalry/lustre and spatial heterogeneity rather than forcing every trial into “average.”
2. Predefine the quantitative null: for example, an independently fitted, fixed weighted-average model of a chosen **psychophysical match coordinate** on a restricted stimulus range. Reserve other pairs for testing. Record color matches, target-orientation choices and confidence separately; set a measurement-error tolerance and preregister exclusion and observer-level analyses.
3. If held-out matches fall systematically outside the preregistered predictive intervals or the model fails under a planned contrast reversal, reject **that fitted model on that task**. If they remain within a prespecified equivalence margin with adequate precision, report practical compatibility in that range, not proof of exact equality or universal averaging. No specific numerical prediction or margin is claimed here: they require a calibrated pilot and a frozen plan.
4. In separately randomized adaptation/test trials, use monocular and binocular probes and appropriate no-adaptation, same-color, and eye-swap controls. A reproducible eye-specific aftereffect that differs across matched-report conditions would challenge a model that retains *only* the common report state as all information needed to predict future probe reports. It would not prove private ongoing experiences. Later probes are interventions, not readouts of a simultaneous hidden subject.

A comparison of Lewtas-style selected-subject termination with persistent subjects needs an additional **independently defended subject-individuation and persistence criterion**, plus an assay demonstrably sensitive to it in the relevant condition. The [previous formal gate](LEWTAS_FUSION_PRIVATE_COPY_IDENTIFICATION_GATE.md) shows how error bounds would yield a probability separation *if* such an assay existed; its example bounds were invented. The binocular tasks above do not calibrate it. Any claimed discriminator derived solely from the same color match, brain response or report channel would be circular if the competing accounts assign those observations alike. An indeterminate subject result must remain indeterminate.

## 7. Consequences for the framework

The earlier [metamer bridge](COLOR_METAMER_PHENOMENAL_PHYSICAL_BRIDGE.md) demonstrates, in an idealized physical colorimetric model, that a single standard-observer match can hide spectral differences that filter interventions reveal. The present papers provide a complementary **empirical** pattern: a restricted observer-level appearance or task can hide differences between eye-specific inputs that a later probe can expose. Neither proves the spectra, channels, signals or experiences are identical; neither gives a phenomenal composition law. Colorimetric metamers and dichoptic fusion are related by an observation-versus-hidden-input distinction, not the same physical mechanism.

For a fundamentally timeless experiential whole, locally ordered input, report and probe events may be represented internally without imposing an external universal clock. That formal accommodation does not derive local subjects, their identities or the particular qualitative character. The target remains a non-circular account of selection, organization, felt quality and incidence, constrained by data but not replaced by a label or a fitted report function. C3 and the subject-constitution question remain open.
