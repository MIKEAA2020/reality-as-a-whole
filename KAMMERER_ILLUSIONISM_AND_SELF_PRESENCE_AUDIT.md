# The illusion of conscious experience: source audit and the self-presence challenge

## Result

The supplied paper gives a serious challenge to treating the *apparent obviousness* of experience as an argument that no rival is coherent. It does **not** establish that experience is illusory. Its own author presents the central cognitive theory as a speculative “how possibly” explanation, not as an empirically confirmed mechanism or a direct proof of illusionism.

The useful separation for this project is:

1. A functionally specified system may represent or report “I am experiencing”, whether or not its representation is correct.
2. The occurrence of an actual experience, if present, is not merely a report of one. A first-person experiential datum should not be replaced by a third-person description without an argument.
3. A representation or verbal denial of experience is not automatically itself an experience. Therefore the sentence “even an illusion must be experienced” does not, without qualification, defeat the rival.
4. Even granting an actual experiential datum, neither an experiential **fundamental ground**, self-sufficiency, plurality of subjects, nor necessary existence follows without further premises. The strongest neutral-ground rival in the [grounding battery](EXPERIENTIAL_GROUNDING_RIVAL_BATTERY_GATE.md) already accepts real experience; it survives even if strong illusionism fails.

The framework can continue to take experience as its starting datum, while acknowledging that this is disputed by strong illusionism and is not settled by a report or a formal diagram. This distinction strengthens the assessment rather than replacing the user's experiential target.

## 1. The paper's proposal, not a straw man

**Strong illusionism**, in the paper's sense, denies that phenomenal consciousness exists while maintaining that it robustly *seems* to exist. It does not deny all introspection, information processing, report, or scientifically tractable mental properties. It allows introspective judgments to track real quasi-phenomenal properties, while denying that there is something it is like to have them (PDF pp. 3–5). Weak illusionism, by contrast, grants experience but denies some properties commonly attributed to it; the author distinguishes and criticizes that view. Phenomenal physicalists can also affirm experience, so they must not be equated with strong illusionists.

Kammerer's **illusion meta-problem** is more specific than the ordinary problem of explaining why people report experience: why does it seem uniquely hard even to *conceive* that experience only appears to exist? Ordinary hallucination can be entertained despite a strong visual conviction; the analogous separation of phenomenal appearance from phenomenal reality seems much harder (pp. 5–7). The paper claims that a strong disposition alone does not explain this extra difficulty; this is an argumentative claim, not a measured universal fact about all people.

The author's proposed answer is **TCE**: phenomenal concepts are *theoretically determined concepts of epistemologically special states* (pp. 8–13). On this account a partly encapsulated, domain-specific theory-of-mind system supplies a general experience operator, combined with sensory recognitional concepts. The system implicitly represents experiences as internal receptive affections that make states of affairs appear in virtue of **maximal resemblance**. Four folk-theory principles concern appearance, receptive affection, resemblance and individuation. These are claims about the *content of the proposed folk theory*, not claims that real experiences in fact resemble external things.

Applied to experiences themselves, the proposed resemblance principle makes the folk theory treat an experience as self-intimating and its introspective appearance as necessarily veridical (pp. 14–16). Thus, *if* the cognitive theory is correct, the felt difficulty of entertaining illusionism is explained by the structure of the ordinary concepts we deploy. The author also notes that variants using other relations with suitable structural features could yield a similar result (footnote 24); maximal resemblance is not claimed as the only possible implementation.

Most importantly, the paper distinguishes two senses of “seems” (pp. 15–19):

- **Naive/intuitive appearance**, as the proposed folk theory construes it, brings with it a receptive affection and, in the case of an experience, entails that experience.
- **Functional seeming**, in the stipulated theory, is a momentary, non-cognitively penetrable disposition to believe. It is not defined as a phenomenal experience.

The illusionist can say that it functionally seems that experience occurs, while denying both experience and intuitive appearance. If one substitutes the naive sense into the conclusion while the premise only supplies the functional sense, the apparent contradiction results from an equivocation. Conversely, simply redefining experience as a disposition does not establish that experience in the realist's intended sense is unreal.

## 2. What the paper does and does not establish

The paper explicitly says it does **not** provide independent empirical evidence for TCE or directly argue that TCE or illusionism is true (pp. 8, 18). It proposes high-level speculative psychology. Its account is restricted to **introspection of perceptual phenomenal states**; it leaves extension to bodily sensations, pain, pleasure and hypothetical cognitive phenomenology open (pp. 8–9). We cannot use this paper alone as an established account of pain, valence, unreported experience or the neural basis of introspection.

TCE as a theory of concepts is explicitly compatible with either realism or eliminativism; the author combines it with eliminativism as a further step (p. 12). His eliminativist proposal says no state satisfies the complete folk-theoretical characterization, in particular the maximal-resemblance requirement (p. 12, footnotes 16–17). Whether that characterization captures *everything meant by phenomenal existence*, rather than adding a questionable theory to a minimal notion of something-it-is-like, is a substantive issue. If a putative experiential state fails maximal resemblance, it does not follow by logic alone that there is no phenomenal occurrence under a different, thinner concept. Nor does invoking a thinner concept alone settle what its referent is or refute the author. The inferential gap is a **semantic and metaphysical dispute**, not an experimental finding.

Likewise, a cognitive explanation of why one believes `E` is not by itself evidence that `E` is false: reliable belief formation also has cognitive causes. Yet the realist must say why the actual first-person datum is not exhausted by the very kind of functional seeming the paper describes. Simply repeating “it seems experienced” without distinguishing the sense of seeming will not resolve that dispute.

## 3. A scope-controlled performative test

Let `E` be “at least one phenomenal occurrence is actual” and `F` be a physical/functional event representing or asserting `E`. Let `A` mean a **phenomenal** appearing of E, defined so that `A → E`. Consider:

```text
A and not E           impossible, given the definition of A
F and not E           not excluded by that definition
E and not F           not excluded by that definition
F and E               not excluded by that definition
```

If a particular act of considering or denying `E` is itself experienced, that particular performance conditionally establishes that some experience occurs: its **experienced performance** entails `E`. It does not show that an electronic text generator, an unexperienced computational process, or a hypothetical functional representation is experienced. This is the difference between an experienced denial and a mere sentence or disposition. Neither the conditional argument nor this truth table alone provides neutral evidence that the author of a report is phenomenally conscious. Likewise, a phenomenally performed denial can be false even if its speaker's occurrence is real; it does not validate every detail or interpretation of an experience.

A toy interpretation assigning `F=1, E=0, A=0` is syntactically consistent with these stipulated implications; `F=1, E=1, A=1` is consistent too. This only shows what the chosen definitions do and do not entail. It is **not** proof that an experience-free person or world is genuinely possible, or that phenomenal evidence is reducible to a variable. In the project's strongest actuality–possibility interpretation, consistency of an alternative description is not the same as its genuine possibility.

## 4. A positive challenge for each side

**For the experiential framework:** State the datum without building a particular theory of external resemblance, a separate inner observer, infallibility of every phenomenal classification, or the fundamentality of the event into it. Explain why the actual experiencing is evidence of itself rather than merely a functional classification that *claims* to disclose it. Then show how this evidence bears on an experiential **ground**, confronting the neutral ground that accepts the same real experience. The earlier self-presence argument is a motivation for a one-level account, not a demonstration that self-presence entails metaphysical independence.

**For TCE plus strong illusionism:** Independently justify the proposed subpersonal module and its content; explain why the relevant folk theory includes a resemblance or structurally equivalent relation; and defend treating failure to satisfy the complete theoretical operator as absence of phenomenality rather than mistaken beliefs about phenomenal properties. The account owes both an explanation of public reports and an answer to the realist's claimed first-person datum. The paper acknowledges that its distinctive mechanism is not empirically established. Treating the dispute as a matter of terminology alone risks missing the disagreement over whether anything is felt.

**For both:** distinguish representational content, actual referent, and epistemic access; avoid judging the whole question by whether a premise feels natural. An intuitive explanation cannot substitute for a detailed account of what makes subject boundaries, felt qualities, and physical behavior cohere. Conversely, a highly productive cognitive account of reports would not automatically settle whether its subjects feel anything.

## 5. A discriminating research design, not a proposed experience detector

TCE makes substantive cognitive claims that can be investigated independently of the metaphysical verdict. One possible research program would preregister competing computational models of perceptual introspection: one with an independently specified relatively encapsulated operator linking appearance, self-intimation and resistance to contrary information, and another with a more flexible or non-theory-based architecture. Define their model families, observable tasks, measurement noise and held-out predicted distributions *before* collecting data. Probe generalization across sensory modalities, time pressure and counter-instruction, while separately measuring basic discrimination, verbal ability, memory and confidence. Use manipulations and controls justified independently of the desired result; quantify uncertainty and include recoverability or model-confusion simulations.

Resistance to instruction alone is not diagnostic: habit, task misunderstanding, incentives and several other architectures can mimic it. Nor is a verbal theory-of-mind measure a direct test of an innate, inaccessible module. A study must show that the proposed task actually targets the claimed mechanism, specify rival predictions rather than merely post hoc explanations, and avoid extrapolating this paper's visual/perceptual scope to pain. Better prediction of reports could support or challenge an introspective architecture; **it would not, without a separately justified phenomenal assay, establish the existence or absence of experience**. No new data have been collected here.

## 6. Framework impact

- The first-person experiential premise is a serious starting position, not a dialectically uncontested theorem.
- An experienced denial can conditionally secure actual experience against that particular performance; it does not establish that every denial must be experienced.
- Under the project's adopted global possibility–actuality schema, actual experience yields necessary global experience **conditionally**; the paper neither proves nor disproves that schema. That conditional result does not establish experiential fundamentality.
- If illusionism fails, the more demanding comparison of an experiential ground with a neutral ground retaining real experiences still stands. If illusionism succeeds, the project's premise of real experience fails. The two comparisons must not be conflated.
- No universal subject, creator, external time, experimental experience detector, or solution of the hard problem follows.
