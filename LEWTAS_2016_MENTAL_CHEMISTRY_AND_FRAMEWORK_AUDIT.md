# Lewtas on mental chemistry: what it adds and what remains open

## Conclusion

Pat Lewtas’s *Building minds: solving the combination problem* is directly relevant to the framework’s hardest problem: how distinct experiences and subjects might yield a unified, qualitatively rich experience. Its strongest contribution is to take **transformation**, not the mere piling together of unchanged micro-subjects, seriously as a candidate. It also explicitly identifies choosing experiences, placing them in a field, and blending qualities as different explanatory tasks.

It **does not establish a solution**. Its split-brain argument is conditional on contested interpretations and on panpsychism; its conservation principle and fundamental experiential dispositions are assumptions, not measured laws. Most importantly, Lewtas himself admits that he cannot solve the blending problem: he offers a reason for hope, not a derivation of an orange experience from red and yellow experiences. The proposed electromagnetic mechanisms are speculative. The paper is a valuable rival and a set of sharper requirements for our framework, not confirmation that it or any particular subject-constitution rule is true.

## 2. The paper's actual proposal

Lewtas develops a **mind-dust** panpsychism, not simply the experiential-whole view. Basic physical objects are posited to have basic conscious properties in addition to physical properties; some basic objects may lack consciousness. His view admits irreducibly non-conscious physical properties, and does not identify the conscious properties with the grounds of all physical dispositions. Quarks are explicitly stand-ins for basic objects, not an empirical finding that quarks experience (PDF pp. 3–4, 11, 19–20). He allows a field-ontology adaptation as a possibility, but does not develop quantum field theory in the paper (p. 11, note 28).

The proposal is stronger and more unusual than merely saying that particles have tiny minds. On his assumed model, a single basic object can host **multiple basic experience types**, each with a separate phenomenal field and a separate subject. At higher levels, a structure can select certain experiences and merge their associated subjects and fields. Inputs selected for a higher subject cease to exist as independent subjects **in that selected respect**; the same basic objects may retain other, unselected experiences and subjects. Lewtas does not claim that all consciousness on an object must always vanish whenever some of its experiences contribute upward (pp. 20, 27–29). Later he allows that a higher-level entity might surrender only part of its experience rather than all of it (p. 42).

He distinguishes three operations (pp. 12–13, 27–34):

1. **Choosing:** which kinds of basic experience are recruited.
2. **Placing:** how selected experiences become jointly structured within one field—for example, different regions of a visual presentation.
3. **Blending:** how selected qualities yield a different quality, illustrated by red and yellow experiences and an orange experience.

He treats presentation, awareness, phenomenal field and subject as conceptually distinguishable aspects of one conscious property. Their merger is therefore one operation described in different ways, not four independently established transformations (pp. 11, 29).

## 3. Why this challenges the framework

The project's experiential whole is not automatically a single cosmic subject: the user’s governing correction requires genuinely **plural subjects and perspectives**, with no universal agent assumed. Nor does connectedness alone determine which finite subject owns a given experience. Lewtas’s distinction between aggregation and transformation sharpens that task: saying that aspects are related, or that a global structure exists, does not yet explain **who experiences what together**, what happens to prior subjects, or how the new experience feels.

But his bottom-up picture and the project's intrinsically experiential, fundamentally timeless whole start from different ontologies. Importing his separate non-conscious physical properties, basic-particle subjects, directed temporal causal powers or physical location into the whole would require independent argument. Conversely, saying that the whole is experiential does not by itself supply the selection, field-unity, quality or subject-identity rules he seeks. An abstract, locally ordered process might be represented within a timeless whole, but representing an ordered history is not yet a derivation of its experiential constitution or causal significance.

His demand that explanations be intelligible in both metaphysical and experiential terms is a serious ally of the project's **C3: full structural and experiential intelligibility**. The proposed realization does not establish C3. A name for the mechanism, an abstract structure, a stipulated subject or an asserted necessary connection is not yet an explanation of experiential presence, particular quality, subject individuation or the structural–experiential bridge.

## 4. The split-brain argument: possibility versus observation

Lewtas considers two interpretations (PDF pp. 14–17):

- If a patient has two separate streams, he imagines a **hypothetical reconnection** of the hemispheres and argues that a reunified experience is a plausible possible outcome. This is a thought experiment, not a reported operation or demonstration that the old subjects literally became one.
- If, as Tye and Dennett suggest in the account he discusses, a single stream temporarily divides during special tasks and rejoins later, he regards the return to unity as actual mental chemistry. His note 35 explicitly makes this conclusion conditional **on panpsychism**. He acknowledges multiple interpretations of split-brain behavior before preferring this one.

Even if a well-controlled patient exhibits a changed pattern of experience or reporting, the move from that finding to merger of **basic** subjects, preservation of a conserved experiential quantity and the cessation of the former subjects needs additional premises. Other interpretations include partly unified experience, a unified agent with limited access, multiple interacting fields, or changes in which information a report system can access. These are not interchangeable, and none is established merely by naming it.

The project's [split-brain timing and rivalry audit](SPLIT_BRAIN_TIMING_RIVALRY_AND_POOLED_EVIDENCE.md) distinguishes detection, response speed, content combination and experiential unity; its pooled case studies show both dissociation and coordination, not a definitive count of subjects. The [subject-incidence audit](SUBJECT_INCIDENCE_INDIRECT_COMMUNICATION_AND_NESTING_AUDIT.md) describes a conditional, case-specific argument from indirect psychological communication, while explaining why physical overlap or one relay does not settle local-versus-global subject identity. These are qualifications on the inference, not a denial of patients' experience or testimony.

## 5. Conservation and subject count

Lewtas's Constraint 11 asserts that consciousness comes in amounts and is neither created nor destroyed (PDF pp. 20–22). He argues from anti-emergence, analogies with some physical properties, and intuitions about degrees of consciousness. He explicitly distinguishes **amount** from **number of subjects**: several subjects could transform into one without losing the total amount, as smaller drops can form a larger one. His model further argues for conservation of each basic experience type, with transformations preserving it in some manner (pp. 27–29).

That is an intelligible *proposal*, but the argument does not independently establish:

- a noncircular unit or operational measure of phenomenal amount, a rule for adding it, or a conservation law across sleep, death, merger or fission;
- that no emergence **by definition** entails conservation of an experiential quantity, rather than a different account of dependency or changing organization;
- that a property ceasing to be instantiated amounts to creation of a thing called “nothing,” or that consciousness must be redistributed to microscopic subjects when a person dies;
- that an analogy to conservation of some physical quantities establishes this new phenomenal law.

These are objections to the attempted inference, not proof that consciousness can be created or destroyed. The paper's point that amount and subject count differ is important and survives those objections. But conservation by itself cannot determine *which* subject count or ownership rule applies.

### A small accounting check—not a model of consciousness

The accompanying [finite check](verification/lewtas_conservation_subject_gate.py) stipulates two ingredient tokens `a,b`, each with unit weight. A fused case has one represented subject supported by `{a,b}`; a separate case has two represented subjects supported by `{a}` and `{b}`. Both have total distinct-token weight 2, and both also have a sum of subject-support weights of 2. Thus **even exclusive, additive accounting leaves the one-versus-two ownership choice open** unless an additional constitution rule selects one.

A third, nested stipulation represents supports `{a}`, `{b}`, and `{a,b}`. Counting each distinct token once gives 2; summing all subject-support weights gives 4. If conservation is explicitly defined by the latter additive subject-weight rule, the nested case is excluded. If it is defined by the former inventory rule, nesting does not automatically violate it. This exposes a choice of quantity and ownership convention; it does **not** prove that shared tokens, nested subjects, or either conservation law are physically or metaphysically possible. Nor does it refute Lewtas's stronger exclusion rule once that rule is assumed.

The check also allows two different qualitative labels for the same inventory and support. That is a formal underdetermination witness: amount and set membership alone do not choose a quality. It does not establish that those labels are possible experiences or that Lewtas's fully specified causal dispositions would leave the same ambiguity. Identical behavioral records in the check are stipulated, not evidence from patients.

## 6. Blending, explanation and the causal-base reply

Lewtas recognizes the central difficulty. Red and yellow paint producing orange does not by itself explain why red and yellow **experiences** could constitute an orange experience. He rejects two simple proposals and concedes that he cannot solve blending directly (PDF pp. 31–35). He proposes that similarity relations between orange and red/yellow support a constitutive hypothesis, while explicitly saying that this does not prove it. Similarity may motivate a hypothesis; it does not uniquely identify the constituents, show the relevant relation is composition, or make the felt quality deducible.

In responding to Chalmers and Goff, he says their deduction base should include basic causal dispositions as well as basic conscious properties (pp. 43–44). That is a genuine answer to a **too-thin** base: an inference can fail if relevant powers have been omitted. But adding a disposition whose content already says that specified inputs produce an orange experience establishes a conditional consequence only **given that disposition**. Whether its nature explains the orange quality or simply encodes the desired answer remains an additional question. His own solution depends on resolving blending (p. 44, note 74). One should not summarize his paper as having derived orange phenomenology from independently established ingredients.

Nor is diachronic transformation automatically excluded as incoherent. The disputed question is whether a lawful temporal production relation, with the original subjects no longer independently present, suffices for **constitutive** explanation and subject identity, or leaves a further psychophysical bridge. Lewtas argues for the former; his critics deny or question it. A model of succession and conserved tokens alone decides neither side.

## 7. Electromagnetic powers and causal relevance

The paper first gives simplified choosing, placing and blending structures and then warns that directly assigning fundamental powers to high-level structures threatens his anti-emergence requirement. Its amended proposal assigns basic conscious properties level-independent, **passive receptivities** to electromagnetic influences; higher structures channel fields that trigger them (PDF pp. 35–40). The paper considers a 40-Hz alternating-field version but **rejects that second amendment as unacceptable on its own anti-emergence terms**; its tentative preferred proposal is the first, based on specified receptive dispositions. It also explicitly allows that electromagnetism might not be the relevant mechanism, or that no such mechanism exists (p. 42). The paper does not establish its frequencies, receptors or mental-chemical laws experimentally.

His passive/active distinction is worth taking seriously: a property need not itself be an active producer in order to be a condition for how another process unfolds. Still, the model must state exactly what changes a physical outcome. If two cases have the same **complete physically sufficient state** and the same physical dynamics but differ only in an extra experiential property, those assumptions leave the same physical outcomes. To obtain a different physical outcome, one must instead specify a physical difference, relax physical sufficiency, identify the difference with part of the physical causal story, or give another explicit account of the dependence. Which option is correct is disputed; the word “passive” alone chooses none of them. This is a conditional clarification, not a proof that experience is causally inert or identical to physics.

Neural oscillations or stimulus frequency tags are physical signals, not direct evidence of Lewtas's proposed basic receptivities. The project's [S02 EEG transfer](ROY_S02_FROZEN_REPORT_CENTER_TRANSFER_RESULTS.md) tested a simple frozen report-prediction rule and performed poorly; the [transition-context diagnostic](ROY_MIXED_TRANSITION_CONTEXT_EXPLORATORY_RESULTS.md) found important timing and window limitations. Neither test measured mental chemistry or the existence, persistence or number of subjects. A negative decoder does not refute Lewtas, and the existence of oscillations does not confirm him.

## 8. What a better comparison would require

A useful next formulation would compare Lewtas's transformation model, nested/overlapping-field alternatives, and an experience-respecting physical or emergent rival on the **same** cases:

1. **Specify the target:** which experiential tokens, qualities and subject-indexed conjunctions are claimed at which local windows; distinguish numerical identity from similarity and an agent from an experiencer.
2. **Define the amount and ownership rule:** if a conserved quantity is claimed, state what it measures, how it composes, when it applies, whether overlap is counted once or per subject, and what fission/merger predicts. State separately which input subjects cease or persist.
3. **Give a non-question-begging qualitative map:** specify independently constrained inputs and admissible operations, and explain what fixes the resulting quality rather than simply labeling the desired output. Admitting an unresolved primitive is more informative than calling it a derivation.
4. **Specify the physical interface and alternatives:** identify what differs between candidate mechanisms, what the observed neural, task and report channels can and cannot show, and whether a proposed effect survives timing and artifact controls. Predictions should discriminate rival packages rather than merely restate that organized brains correlate with reports.
5. **Respect limits of evidence:** use existing ethically collected observations and subject-indexed reports where available; account for response access, hidden or duplicated content, temporal changes and partial unity. No surgery, patient contact, correspondence, new participant acquisition or causal intervention is proposed here. A report-based discriminator still needs a justified experience bridge.

These requirements sharpen the framework's research programme. They do not derive a unique experiential constitution, establish consciousness in quarks or bacteria, settle split-brain subject counts, prove a conservation law, or close the project's explanatory criteria. The [relational-whole subject model](RELATIONAL_WHOLE_SUBJECT_FACET_CONSTITUTION.md) already exposes how nested and overlapping subjects depend on ownership assumptions; the present toy accounting check adds a distinct warning about how a proposed conserved quantity is counted.
