The most faithful starting point is **not** “we are definitely in a computer simulation.” Bostrom’s Simulation Argument is a conditional trilemma: at least one of these is true—civilizations rarely reach a posthuman stage, posthuman civilizations rarely run many ancestor simulations, or simulated observers greatly outnumber unsimulated ones. The argument does not establish which option is true, and it depends on assumptions about consciousness, observer-counting, and what counts as a relevant reference class.

To get a maximally intelligible account of reality from it, I’d make one main change: **treat “simulation” as a relation between patterns, not as a claim that our universe is running on some external computer.**

**The reconstruction**

1. **Reality is a coherent, evolving structure.**  
   What physics describes as states and laws can be understood as a structure of possible states and their relations or transitions. “Computation” is a useful way of describing that organization; it need not imply silicon, a programmer, or a clock outside the universe.

2. **A simulation is an implemented pattern.**  
   One system simulates another when its internal states reproduce the other system’s relevant structure and dynamics. This can happen *inside* reality. A simulated world need not be unreal: its inhabitants and events are real as patterns in the system that implements them.

3. **There need not be an absolute “bottom-level” simulation.**  
   There may be nested simulations, but the ultimate account is not “our universe is in somebody else’s machine.” It is the total structure in which all such implementation relations occur. That totality is not itself simulated in a further environment; it is the meaning of “the whole.”

4. **Observers are internal to the structure.**  
   We are processes within reality that build partial models of it. Our experience is not a view from outside the system. A complete account would have to explain why some such processes have experience—but Bostrom’s argument itself does not do that.

This preserves Bostrom’s central possibility—that our world could be an implemented world—while removing the unnecessary extra picture of a familiar, external computer running it.

**What “zero residue” can mean**

In a **weak but useful sense**, this reconstruction aims at zero *external* residue: there is no unexplained outside simulator, no hidden hardware beyond reality, and no further physical level that must be invoked to make the account work. “Simulation” describes relationships within the whole rather than an escape hatch to something else.

In the **strict sense**, though, it cannot deliver zero residue. It still has to leave something as fundamental: that this structure exists; that it has these laws or relations; and, unless experience is fully derived from those relations, that some processes are conscious. Calling the structure “necessary,” “mathematical,” or “self-explanatory” does not by itself explain why it exists. It names the stopping point.

So the smallest plausible upgrade is:

> **Reality is a self-contained, observer-containing structure of lawful relations. Simulations are real substructures implemented within it; the total structure is not a simulation in any further external medium.**

That is a compact and fairly faithful reconstruction. It makes the simulation idea more intelligible as a theory of *levels of implementation*, but “zero residue” remains an ideal of explanatory closure—not something Bostrom’s argument can establish. The deepest remaining question is whether the total structure is a brute fact, a necessity, or not ultimately the right kind of thing to ask “why” about.
