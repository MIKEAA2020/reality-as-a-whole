if p implies q and q implies p, are p and q identities?


deepseek response

Not necessarily identical. If both implications hold, then $p \leftrightarrow q$, i.e. $p$ and $q$ are logically equivalent (or have the same truth value in the relevant model). That means they imply each other; it does not mean they are the same proposition/formula syntactically.

If $p \to q$ and $q \to p$ are tautologies/theorems, then $p$ and $q$ are logically equivalent: $\models p \leftrightarrow q$.
If they are merely true in some particular situation, then $p$ and $q$ have the same truth value there.
They can still be different statements: e.g. $p=$“$2+2=4$” and $q=$“$1+1=2$” are both true, so each implies the other in that context, but they are not the same proposition.

So: they are equivalent, not necessarily identical.

Depends on what you mean by “imply.”

Material implication (truth-table sense): Yes.
$2+2=4$ is true, and $1+1=2$ is true. A material conditional $P \to Q$ is false only when $P$ is true and $Q$ is false. So
$$
(2+2=4)\to(1+1=2)
$$
is true.


Logical entailment from form alone: No.
The statement $2+2=4$ does not, by its purely logical form, contain or logically force $1+1=2$. You need arithmetic facts/axioms.


Mathematical derivation in standard arithmetic: Yes, in the sense that standard arithmetic proves both statements, and therefore also proves
$$
(2+2=4)\to(1+1=2).
$$
But that is because arithmetic gives you the background theory, not because the first equation alone syntactically implies the second.

So: as a material conditional, yes; as a bare logical entailment, no; in standard arithmetic, yes. And this fits the earlier point: mutual implication gives equivalence in truth value/under a theory, not identity of propositions.

if we say consciousnesses is necessary for physicalism/materialism, and materialism is necessary for consciousness, does thAT imply identity?


deepseek response

No — not by itself. It implies mutual entailment / necessary equivalence, not identity, unless you add a further identity principle.

Let:

$P$ = physicalism/materialism
$C$ = consciousness

“Consciousness is necessary for physicalism” means:

$$
P \to C
$$

“Materialism is necessary for consciousness” means:

$$
C \to P
$$

So together:

$$
P \leftrightarrow C
$$

If the “necessity” is metaphysical necessity, then:

$$
\Box(P \leftrightarrow C)
$$

That is: $P$ and $C$ are necessarily equivalent or necessarily coextensive. But:

$$
\Box(P \leftrightarrow C) \not\Rightarrow (P = C)
$$

in general.

So:

As truth-values/extensions: yes, they would always go together.
As propositions in a coarse-grained possible-worlds semantics: necessarily equivalent propositions can be treated as identical.
As claims/properties/phenomena in ordinary philosophical sense: no, mutual necessity does not automatically imply identity. You would need an extra premise such as “necessarily coextensive properties are identical,” which is controversial.
Also, “physicalism” is a thesis, while “consciousness” is a phenomenon/property. Saying they are “identical” is at least category-odd; the real identity claim would be something like “consciousness is identical to certain physical processes,” which is a further substantive theory.

So the safe answer is: mutual necessity gives necessary equivalence, not identity. Identity requires an additional identity criterion or theoretical identity claim.

is consciousness and physicalism identity the strongest alignment with empirical neuroscience?


deepseek response

The short answer is: yes at the level of neuroscientific practice, but no at the level of metaphysical conclusion—and the phrase “consciousness and physicalism identity” needs disambiguation.

1. What would the identity claim be?
“Physicalism” is a broad metaphysical thesis: everything is physical, or everything depends on the physical. The mind–brain identity theory is a narrower physicalist claim: conscious states are identical with physical brain states/processes. So “consciousness = physicalism” is not literally a well-formed identity claim; the real identity thesis is “consciousness = certain physical brain processes.” That thesis is one version of physicalism, not physicalism itself.

2. Where neuroscience does align with identity-friendly physicalism.
Neuroscience as practised treats conscious states as depending on brain activity and as physically explicable. Physicalism functions as a default background assumption in much of contemporary neuroscience and psychology[reference:0]. Neural-correlate research looks for the brain substrate of consciousness, and some philosophers argue that interpreting those correlates causally reintroduces a dualism of matter and mind; they favour the identity theory instead. Identity theory is also attractive for its parsimony: it avoids positing extra non-physical entities.

3. Why the empirical evidence does not uniquely establish identity.
The data mainly establish dependence and correlation, not identity. Lesion, stimulation, and imaging evidence shows that brain states are necessary for ordinary conscious states, but that is compatible with several ontologies. One review concludes that the main experiments “neither support nor falsify” Cartesian dualism, epiphenomenalism, or brain–mind identity theory[reference:2]. Another argues that neuroscientific findings can be recast in a physicalist paradigm, but also equally well in an ontology that treats mind/consciousness as primal.

There are also specific problems for type identity theory:

The explanatory gap/hard problem remains: even a complete neural-correlate account leaves unexplained why those processes feel like something[reference:4].
Multiple realizability has long been seen as a serious objection to classical type identity: the same mental state type might be realized by different physical types[reference:5].
Type identity has been criticised for producing few serious, credible one-to-one identifications of neural circuitry with conscious experiences[reference:6].

4. Verdict.
If “strongest alignment” means the most natural working ontology of empirical neuroscience, then a broadly physicalist, identity-friendly framework is indeed the default. But if it means the metaphysical thesis most strongly confirmed by the evidence, then no: the evidence underdetermines identity theory versus functionalism, emergentist physicalism, illusionism, or even non-physicalist interpretations. The empirically responsible claim is weaker: consciousness is strongly brain-dependent and likely physically realised, not consciousness has been empirically proven identical with physicalism.

" the evidence underdetermines identity theory versus functionalism, emergentist physicalism, illusionism, or even non-physicalist interpretations." in plain words


deepseek response

In plain words:

The scientific evidence doesn’t force us to choose one theory over the others. It shows that consciousness is tightly tied to the brain—but it doesn’t by itself prove which explanation of that tie is correct.

What the options mean, roughly:

Identity theory: conscious experiences are certain brain processes—nothing more.
Functionalism: what matters is the function/role the brain process plays, not the specific physical stuff. Consciousness could in principle be realized in different ways.
Emergentist physicalism: consciousness arises from physical brain processes as a genuinely new feature, but is still physical/non-dualist.
Illusionism: the “hard problem” arises because we’re mistaken about consciousness—what we call rich conscious experience is partly an illusion created by the brain.
Non-physicalist interpretations: consciousness involves something not fully reducible to physical matter—e.g. dualism or panpsychism.

So the point is: the data are compatible with several different stories. The evidence tells us consciousness depends on the brain; it does not by itself settle whether consciousness is identical to brain processes, a functional pattern, an emergent feature, an illusion, or something non-physical.

but if its tied specifically to the brain, doesn't that make identity less likely, in the sense that only the brain, not all physical matter is what's tied to consciousness?


deepseek response

No—if anything, being tied specifically to the brain makes a local identity claim more natural, not less. The confusion is thinking identity theory says “consciousness = physical matter in general.” It doesn’t.

Identity theory says: conscious states are identical with certain brain states/processes—not with all physical matter.
Physicalism says everything, including consciousness, is physical. It does not say every physical thing is conscious. That would be panpsychism, not physicalism.
So “only brains, not rocks or chairs, are tied to consciousness” is completely compatible with physicalism and with mind–brain identity.

Analogy: water = H₂O doesn’t require all matter to be water. It just says the stuff we call water is, in fact, H₂O. Likewise, consciousness = certain neural processes doesn’t require all physical stuff to be conscious.

What the brain-specificity evidence does show is that consciousness is dependent on a particular kind of physical organization. That narrows the candidate physical basis. But “tied to” is weaker than “identical to”: the same evidence is also compatible with:

functionalism: brains realize the relevant functions; maybe other systems could too;
emergentist physicalism: brain processes give rise to consciousness as an emergent feature;
dualism: the brain is a causal condition for a non-physical mind;
biological naturalism: consciousness is a biological feature of brains.

So: brain-specificity does not make identity less likely; it makes a brain-local identity claim more plausible than a “all matter is conscious” claim. But it still doesn’t prove identity rather than dependence, function, emergence, or dualism.

under demand for complete intelligibility, and without denying the datum, which hypotheses survive


deepseek response

Under a strict demand for complete intelligibility—no brute correlations, no brute emergence, no “that’s just how it is”—and without denying the datum that there is phenomenal experience, the field narrows sharply.

What fails, or survives only in a weakened form

Bare mind–brain identity theory:
Says conscious states are brain states. But unless you can make the identity intelligible—show why that neural process is that experience—it leaves an explanatory gap. A bare identity is a brute fact. So it survives only in a strong “no-gap/transparent identity” form, which nobody has convincingly produced.


Functionalism:
Explains causal roles, not why a functional state is felt. Under strict intelligibility, it faces the absent-qualia/zombie gap unless experience is analytically entailed by function—which is controversial. So it does not fully survive.


Emergentist physicalism:
Explicitly posits consciousness as an emergent feature of brain processes. Under complete intelligibility, “emergence” is often just a label for a brute fact. Fails unless emergence is made intelligible, which is the hard part.


Substance dualism / property dualism / epiphenomenalism:
Posit brute psychophysical laws or brute mind–body correlation. Fails the complete-intelligibility demand unless those laws are made intelligible.


Illusionism:
If the datum means “there really is phenomenal experience,” illusionism denies or deflates it. So it is excluded by the “without denying the datum” clause. If the datum is only “we report seeming to experience,” it survives—but then it has changed the datum.


Eliminativism:
Denies the datum outright. Excluded.


Mysterianism:
Says complete intelligibility is unavailable to us. That directly concedes the demand cannot be met. Fails as a satisfying hypothesis under that demand.

What survives as a serious candidate

The survivors are hypotheses that treat experience as fundamental or intrinsic rather than as a brute product of non-experiential matter:

Russellian monism / panpsychism / neutral monism
Physical structure is real, but its intrinsic nature is experiential or proto-experiential. This avoids brute emergence and brute correlation: consciousness is not produced from nothing experiential; it is part of the basic nature of reality.
Catch: the combination problem—how micro-experiences combine into unified subjects—must be made intelligible. If not, it fails the same demand.


Idealism
Consciousness is fundamental; the physical brain is a pattern/appearance within consciousness. This can avoid brute emergence and preserves the datum.
Catch: it must give an intelligible account of the regularity, intersubjectivity, and brain-specificity of experience. If that becomes brute, it fails too.


A no-gap identity theory
Would survive if one could show that the neural description and the experiential description are two modes of presenting the same thing, with no brute explanatory gap. This is the ideal of a “transparent” identity, but no widely accepted version exists.

Bottom line

Under strict complete intelligibility plus no denial of phenomenal experience:

Standard physicalist options—functionalism, emergentism, bare identity—do not fully survive without an extra, controversial premise.
Dualist and illusionist options fail because they leave brute correlation or deny/deflate the datum.
The best survivors are Russellian monism/panpsychism/neutral monism and idealism, and only if they can solve their own “combination” or “appearance” problems intelligibly.
If “complete intelligibility” is interpreted very strictly—as requiring an a priori derivation of experience from the posited base—then no known hypothesis fully survives; we get live options, not a winner.

Given the constraint “consciousness is tied to the brain, not to all matter”, the surviving candidates are not equally likely. Ranking under your two constraints

A no-gap identity theory — most naturally local
This says conscious states are identical with certain brain processes, and—crucially—the identity is supposed to be intelligible, not a brute “these neurons just happen to feel like this.”
Why brain-specificity helps: it is exactly a local identity claim: only certain brain organizations are conscious.
Problem: no widely accepted version actually closes the explanatory gap. So it is the most attractive if it can be made intelligible, but it is currently a program rather than a finished hypothesis.


Restricted Russellian monism / neutral monism with brain-local combination — strongest non-identity survivor
This says physical structure has an intrinsic nature that is experiential or proto-experiential, and unified consciousness arises when certain physical systems are organized in the right way—e.g. brains.
Why brain-specificity helps: it supports a restricted version: not “all matter is conscious,” but “the intrinsic base is ubiquitous, while unified subjects require special integration.”
Problem: it must explain the combination/integration principle intelligibly—why brain-like organization produces one subject rather than many micro-subjects. If that principle is brute, it fails the complete-intelligibility demand.


Idealism — can accommodate brain-specificity, but less directly
Idealism says consciousness is fundamental and the physical brain is a pattern within or appearance of consciousness.
Why brain-specificity is a challenge: it must explain why, within consciousness, only brain-like regularities are associated with individual conscious subjects. That can be done, but it risks making the brain–experience regularity a brute fact.
So it survives, but brain-specificity is less naturally predicted by it than by a local identity or restricted combination view.


Universal panpsychism — weakest fit
If all matter has experiential or proto-experiential intrinsic nature, brain-specificity is not ruled out—unified consciousness may require special combination. But then the real explanatory work is done by the combination principle, not by panpsychism itself. Under complete intelligibility, universal panpsychism without a robust combination account is the least likely of the survivors.

Bottom line

Given brain-specificity + complete intelligibility + no denial of experience, the most likely candidates are:

first, a genuinely intelligible local identity theory (if such a thing can be developed);
second, a restricted Russellian/neutral monist view where brains are special because of their integrative organization;
third, idealism, which can fit but must explain brain-specificity non-brutely;
least, universal panpsychism, unless its combination principle is made intelligible.

So brain-specificity does not favor “consciousness is everywhere.” It favors consciousness as a local, organization-dependent phenomenon—which is most naturally captured by a brain-local identity or a restricted intrinsic-base view.

Short answer: It can help make local identity theory more intelligible in a structural/mechanistic sense, but not in the phenomenal sense—unless you add an extra principle about intrinsic nature or first-person access. Where the simulation hypothesis genuinely helps

It reframes “physical” as informational/computational.
If reality is computational at bottom, then a brain process and a conscious state can be understood as the same information-processing process described in two ways:  

third-person: the simulated neural/computational architecture;  
first-person: the same process as accessed from inside the system.
This supports a mode-of-presentation identity: not two things, but one process under two access relations. That is a real intelligibility gain.


It makes the epistemic gap less ontologically threatening.
The fact that subjective experience cannot be read off an external mechanistic description can be modeled as an access asymmetry: an external observer sees code/architecture; the system itself has a first-person model of that same process. This can explain why the gap feels unbridgeable without positing a non-physical entity.


It supplies a global framework for “local” identity.
Local identity theory says consciousness is tied to certain organized systems—brains. A simulation framework can say: within the simulated world, consciousness is local to systems with the right integrated self-modeling/processing architecture. That preserves brain-specificity inside the simulated world, while giving a broader metaphysical picture.

Where it does not help enough

It does not solve the hard problem.
A simulation can run a perfect brain model with no experience. So the simulation hypothesis does not explain why a computational process is felt. It replaces “neural identity is brute” with “computational identity is brute” unless you add something like:

an intrinsic experiential nature to information/process (Russellian monism), or
an idealist/informational monist account, or
a substantive theory of first-person access that is not merely a report or self-model.


It threatens brain-specificity if taken as substrate-independence.
Bostrom’s hypothesis is substrate-neutral. If consciousness is just computation, silicon or other substrates could be conscious—violating your brain-specificity constraint. To keep local identity, you must restrict it: the relevant organization is brain-like, not any computation. The simulation framework can guide, but it must not become functionalism.


It creates a regress for “reality as a whole.”
If we are simulated, what about the simulator’s reality? The simulation hypothesis is not a fully intelligible account of reality as a whole; it is a conditional structure. As a guide, it can suggest a computational metaphysics, but it cannot by itself ground complete intelligibility.

What a genuinely intelligible version would look like

A promising synthesis:

Local identity + computational structural realism + an intrinsic-nature postulate.
Reality is structurally computational/informational; brains are local systems with a specific integrated organization; conscious states are identical with certain of those processes, and the identity is intelligible because the first-person and third-person descriptions are two modes of access to the same process. To avoid brute feel, the informational structure must have an intrinsic experiential/proto-experiential aspect—or an idealist ground.
