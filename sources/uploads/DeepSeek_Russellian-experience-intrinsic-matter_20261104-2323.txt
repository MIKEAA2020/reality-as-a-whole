Yes—process panexperientialism (Whiteheadian-style) is not merely coherent with treating time and space as non-fundamental; it is one of the view’s defining features. Whitehead’s entire metaphysical project is built around the claim that instants of time and points of space are abstractions, not concrete realities.

Whitehead’s explicit rejection of fundamental space and time

Whitehead states this directly in his philosophy of science. He calls “absolute time” a “metaphysical monstrosity” just as much as absolute space, and insists that “the notions of instants of time and points in space have been mistaken as fundamental realities by the classical tradition, when in fact they are abstractions”[reference:0]. They lack concrete reality because they can never be directly experienced; what is given in experience is always perception through a duration, never a durationless instant[reference:1].

This is not a marginal remark. It drives his whole event ontology. In An Enquiry Concerning the Principles of Natural Knowledge, he argues that we must stop conceiving events as “in a given Time, a given Space”; instead, “Time, Space, and Material are adjuncts of events”[reference:2]. Space and time are “each partial expressions of one fundamental relation between events which is neither spatial nor temporal”—the relation he calls extension[reference:3].

How time and space are derived, on Whitehead’s account

The derivation runs through two closely connected notions.

First, the extensive continuum. This is the general relational structure of extension between events, not a container in which events are placed. Space-time is not a pre-existing fabric; it is “any other entity that comes into and goes out of existence: it is not a container”[reference:4]. Points and instants are obtained from this continuum only by Whitehead’s method of extensive abstraction: they are defined as limits of nested sets of extended events. As one commentator summarises: “time and space are derivative concepts, they are abstractions which express relations between events”[reference:5].

Second, the epochal theory of time. The fundamental process is not a continuous flow in time. Each actual occasion is a discrete, atomic “drop of experience” that becomes; temporal succession is a product of this becoming, not its medium. Whitehead’s formula is that there is “a becoming of continuity, but no continuity of becoming”[reference:6]. Time is “atomic or epochal,” not a primitive linear succession[reference:7]. The process of concrescence—the becoming of an actual occasion—is therefore not itself temporal in the ordinary sense; time arises from the order among completed occasions.

Why this fits your broader package

Your constraints were: experience = existence = essence, time and space are constructs, and the fundamental is either one or both (not merely many). Whiteheadian process panexperientialism satisfies all three in a distinctive way.

Experience as fundamental: actual occasions are literally “drops of experience”; their subjective becoming is the basic ontological category.
Time and space as non-fundamental: they are abstractions from the extensive relational order among those experiential occasions, not prior containers.
One-and-many: the process is plural (many occasions) but unified through the extensive continuum and through God’s primordial/consequent nature; it is a one-many process, not a collection of substances.

So the coherence is not accidental. Whitehead’s system was designed precisely to avoid treating space, time, and matter as fundamental substances. The “Fallacy of Misplaced Concreteness” is exactly the error of taking the abstractions of space, time, and simple location as concrete realities[reference:8].

The one tension to watch

The only place where the coherence needs care is the status of becoming itself. If time is not fundamental, what does it mean for an occasion to “become”? Whitehead’s answer is that becoming is not an event in time; time is an abstract order that supervenes on completed becomings. But this means your view must accept a non-temporal or pre-temporal sense of process—a dynamic of concrescence that is not itself successive in the ordinary sense. That is a genuine metaphysical commitment, not a trivial one. It is coherent, but it rules out any reading of “process” that treats temporal passage as the most basic feature of reality. On Whitehead’s view, process is more fundamental than time; time is one of its products.

In short: yes, coherent—indeed, Whiteheadian process panexperientialism is one of the most systematic attempts to combine fundamental experience with derivative space-time. The cost is accepting that “becoming” is not itself a temporal flow, but the productive ground from which temporal order is abstracted.
