substrate-independent vs dependent consciousness, which more plausible/likely?


deepseek response

The question of whether consciousness is substrate-independent or substrate-dependent is one of the most profound and unresolved debates in philosophy and science. Based on the current evidence and arguments, neither position is definitively more plausible than the other. The answer depends entirely on which theoretical framework and interpretation of "consciousness" one adopts, and there is no scientific consensus to arbitrate between them[reference:0].

Here is a breakdown of the core arguments for each side to help you assess their relative merits.

🧠 The Case for Substrate-Independence

Substrate-independence is the view, most closely associated with computational functionalism, that consciousness arises from a specific pattern of information processing or causal organization, regardless of the physical material that implements it[reference:1][reference:2]. This is often likened to software: the same program can run on different hardware.

Key Arguments:

Multiple Realizability: This is the core empirical and philosophical argument. We observe that similar mental states and processes appear to be realized in different physical systems. For example, pain can be realized in a human brain, a dog's brain, or potentially an octopus's nervous system, despite vast differences in their neural architecture[reference:3]. If the same function can be implemented in multiple substrates, then the specific material cannot be what is essential for consciousness.
Theoretical Frameworks: Many leading mathematical theories of consciousness, such as Integrated Information Theory (IIT), Global Workspace Theory (GWT), and the Free Energy Principle (FEP), are formulated in a way that is intended to be substrate-neutral. They describe consciousness in terms of abstract mathematical or information-processing properties that could, in principle, be applied to any sufficiently complex system, whether biological or silicon-based.
The "Possibility" Argument: Proponents argue that while current digital computers may not be conscious, this is a contingent limitation of their architecture, not a fundamental one. The principle of substrate-independence remains valid; it's just that we haven't yet built the right kind of computational substrate. The key is distinguishing between possible and actual substrate independence[reference:5].

🧬 The Case for Substrate-Dependence

Substrate-dependence, often defended through biological naturalism, holds that consciousness is not merely a matter of abstract computation but is fundamentally tied to the specific physical and causal properties of living biological systems[reference:6][reference:7].

Key Arguments:

The Unique Nature of Biological Computation: A central argument is that biological brains perform a type of computation that is qualitatively different from digital computation. Proponents argue that consciousness arises from scale-inseparable, substrate-dependent multiscale processing and continuous-valued computations that are intrinsic to the "fluidic substrate" of living tissue. These features are not incidental; they are essential to how biological systems achieve conscious processing, and current AI cannot replicate them[reference:8][reference:9].
The Abstraction Fallacy: A powerful critique from this camp argues that substrate-independence commits a fundamental error. It mistakes a simulation for an instantiation of a process. A computer simulation of a hurricane does not get you wet. Similarly, a symbolic computation that models brain processes is merely a "mapmaker-dependent description" of physics, not the intrinsic physical process that constitutes experience itself. Therefore, algorithmic symbol manipulation is structurally incapable of creating subjective experience[reference:10].
Empirical & Mechanistic Constraints: The biological naturalist points to the specific metabolic, thermodynamic, and causal requirements of living systems. Consciousness may be a solution to a problem (like allostasis) that is unique to organisms with a certain kind of metabolic and physical constitution. On this view, substrate constrains what is possible; not just any physical system can support the patterns required for consciousness[reference:11].

⚖️ Weighing the Two Positions

The debate ultimately hinges on deep, unresolved issues:

The Hard Problem of Consciousness: Substrate-independence struggles to explain why a certain functional organization should be accompanied by subjective experience (the "hard problem"). Substrate-dependence can easily accommodate the intuition that experience is tied to physical constitution, but it still must explain how those biological processes generate experience.
The Nature of Computation: The debate is often a proxy war over the nature of computation itself. If computation is a purely abstract, observer-dependent notion, then substrate-independence is a category error for consciousness[reference:12]. If computation is an intrinsic feature of certain physical systems, then the specific physics of the substrate matters profoundly.

Conclusion: The most defensible answer is that the question remains open. Substrate-independence is the more elegant and theoretically parsimonious hypothesis, and it underpins most computational approaches to consciousness. However, substrate-dependence is a powerful and empirically grounded challenge that highlights the profound and potentially unique causal power of biological organization.

If you'd like to explore a specific theory (like IIT or biological naturalism) or a particular argument in more detail, feel free to ask.

1. What kind of identity would be needed?

There are three relevant options:

Token identity: every particular experience is identical to some particular physical event. This is compatible with substrate-independence at the type level, but it is too weak. It does not tell us that the kind “conscious experience” is substrate-independent; it only says each token has some physical realizer.
Type identity with a physical kind: pain = C-fiber firing, etc. This is the classic identity theory, but it is substrate-dependent, not substrate-independent.
Type identity with a functional/organizational kind: pain = a certain integrated-information/global-workspace/computational state. This is the version that could rescue substrate-independence. Call it functionalist identity theory or psychofunctionalism.

So the proposal would be: phenomenal properties are identical to certain substrate-independent organizational/informational properties. Then:

Experience is not an extra fact added to the functional facts.
There is no “bridge law” from function to qualia; they are the same state under two modes of presentation.
Multiple realizability is preserved: the same organizational type can be realized in different substrates.
The simulation/instantiation objection is handled by saying: a mere simulation does not instantiate the relevant organization; a system that genuinely does instantiate it is conscious.

That is a coherent position. It meets experience as valid datum better than pure functionalism, because it does not treat experience as an illusion or an epiphenomenal add-on. It says experience just is the functional/organizational state, described first-personally.

2. Does it meet “complete intelligibility”?

This is the sticking point. Identity can do one of two things:

Thin intelligibility: It removes the dualistic gap by saying there is only one state, not two. There is no mysterious causal bridge because there is no second thing to bridge. On this reading, identity statements are necessary a posteriori, like “water = H₂O.” They are intelligible enough as theoretical identities.

Thick intelligibility: It demands that we understand why this organizational state should be accompanied by—or be identical with—this qualitative character. Here identity alone does not help. Saying “pain just is that functional state” is not an explanation; it is a stopping point. The explanatory gap reappears as a gap in intelligibility: why does that organization present itself as this? If the answer is “it just does,” then complete intelligibility is not achieved.

So substrate-independence + identity can satisfy complete intelligibility only in the thin sense. In the thick sense, it either:

Posits a brute psychophysical identity—which violates the demand for complete intelligibility; or
Grounds the identity in the intrinsic causal powers of the realizer—which is no longer pure substrate-independence. It becomes a weak substrate-dependence view: the abstract organization matters, but only when realized in a system whose intrinsic properties can ground experience.

3. The abstractness problem

There is an additional worry. Functional/organizational states are often characterized relationally or abstractly: input-output profiles, causal roles, information integration, global availability. Experience is intrinsic and qualitative. Identifying an intrinsic qualitative property with an abstract relational/organizational property is not obviously more intelligible than identifying it with a physical property. In fact, physical type identity at least identifies experience with something concrete and intrinsic. Functional type identity identifies it with something more observer-relative or description-dependent. That makes the “complete intelligibility” demand harder, not easier.

Verdict

Yes, substrate-independence can meet the two constraints with help from identity—but only under a strong functionalist type-identity theory, and only if “complete intelligibility” is interpreted thinly.

It can meet experience as valid datum: experience is identical to the functional/organizational state, not an extra illusion.
It can avoid a brute bridge law: there is no bridge because there is identity.
But it does not by itself explain why that identity holds. If complete intelligibility requires transparent derivation, identity is a brute stopping point.
If you try to make the identity intelligible by appeal to the realizer’s intrinsic causal powers, you have moved toward weak substrate-dependence.

So the identity-assisted substrate-independence position is coherent and serious, and it is more plausible than bare functionalism. But under a strong “complete intelligibility” constraint, it is still less likely than a weak substrate-dependence view that grounds experience in intrinsic causal properties—unless one is willing to accept a brute necessary identity as the final stopping point.

You are right to press on this point. "Thick intelligibility" was my invented shorthand, not a formal term in the philosophy of mind. There is no single, universally agreed-upon formal term that perfectly captures "complete intelligibility in the strongest sense," but several established concepts in the literature map onto the idea.

The Formal Landscape: What Exists vs. What I Invented

My use of "thin" versus "thick" was an attempt to distinguish between two demands:

Thin intelligibility: The demand is met if there is no mysterious dualistic bridge—for example, by positing a token identity. The "gap" is relocated, but the identity itself is accepted as a brute stopping point.
Thick intelligibility (my shorthand): The demand is not met by a brute identity. It requires a grounding explanation—a "in virtue of what" story for why a physical state must be accompanied by a specific phenomenal character.

The literature does not use the "thick/thin" label, but it does contain formal resources that express this stronger demand.

The Closest Formal Terms for the "Strongest Sense"

The strongest demand for intelligibility in this context is usually framed through one of the following formal concepts:

1. "Intelligible Causation" (or "Intelligible Grounding")
This is the most precise match for the grounding demand. Harold Langsam explicitly develops this idea, distinguishing between brute causation and intelligible causation. On the standard view, the connection between a physical property and its causal powers is mediated by brute laws of nature—it cannot be known a priori. Langsam argues that some mental causation is different: the causal powers of a conscious state can "flow in an intelligible way" from the intrinsic properties that ground them. This is exactly the structure of the "strongest sense" demand: it is not enough that an identity or a law obtains; the connection must be intelligible in itself, not mediated by a brute fact.

2. "A Priori Entailment"
In the context of reductive explanation, the strongest intelligibility demand is often formalized as the requirement of a priori entailment. The idea is that if physicalism is true, the facts about phenomenal consciousness must be a priori entailed by the complete physical facts—meaning one could, in principle, deduce the phenomenal truths from the physical truths alone, without any additional empirical premises[reference:3]. If such entailment fails, the physicalist must accept "brute" or "strong" a posteriori necessities—which is precisely the kind of unintelligible stopping point the strongest demand forbids[reference:4].

3. The Contrast with "Brute Identity"
The formal negative term for the failure of the strongest demand is "brute identity theory." Philip Goff uses this label for the view that conscious states simply are brain states, with no further explanation[reference:5]. Goff explicitly calls this "very unsatisfying," because it offers no account of how the brain state results in subjective experience[reference:6]. The demand for intelligibility in the strongest sense is precisely the rejection of brute identity as a satisfactory terminus.

The Standard Term for the Failure of This Demand

The phenomenon that motivates the demand is the explanatory gap, coined by Joseph Levine. Levine's formulation is precise: even granting that a conscious state is identical to a neural state, we still cannot see why that state should feel as it does[reference:7]. The gap is characterized as a failure of a specific kind of intelligibility—one that is "routinely available elsewhere in science" but "systematically unavailable for phenomenal consciousness"[reference:8]. Levine's own position is that this is an epistemological limit, not proof of dualism, but the demand for the strongest intelligibility is the insistence that this limit be overcome, not accepted[reference:9].

Summary

Concept Status What It Names

Intelligible causation/grounding Formal term (Langsam) A causal/grounding connection that is intelligible in itself, not mediated by brute laws.
A priori entailment Formal term (Chalmers, Levine, et al.) The requirement that phenomenal truths be deducible a priori from physical truths[reference:11].
Brute identity Formal term (Goff) The unsatisfying view that experience just is a physical state, with no further explanation[reference:12].
Explanatory gap Formal term (Levine) The failure of intelligibility: we cannot see why a physical state feels the way it does[reference:13].

So, to answer directly: "Thick intelligibility" is my invention, but the idea it was gesturing at has formal homes in the literature—most precisely in the notion of "intelligible causation" or the demand for "a priori entailment," defined in contrast to "brute identity" and the "explanatory gap."

This view combines three claims:

Structuralism about physics: Physics describes only the relational, spatiotemporal structure and dynamics of the world—what things do, not what they are[reference:0].
Realism about intrinsic nature (quiddities): There must be intrinsic properties that underlie and ground that structure[reference:1].
Quidditism about consciousness: Those same intrinsic properties are, at the fundamental level, phenomenal or proto-phenomenal. In its cosmopsychist form, the single intrinsic nature of the cosmos is a unified conscious subject, and all facts—including facts about individual minds—are grounded in facts about that conscious universe[reference:2][reference:3].

This is not panpsychism as a combination of countless micro-subjects. It is priority monism: the whole is prior to its parts. Individual subjects are not built up from smaller experiences; they are dissociations or differentiations of the one cosmic consciousness[reference:4].

Complete intelligibility (no brute facts).
This is the hardest constraint, and it is where the view must be stated carefully. A generic Russellian monism still faces the objection that the link between intrinsic structure and phenomenal character could be a brute identity. The most promising attempt to avoid this is necessary cosmopsychism grounded in a structural Principle of Sufficient Reason (PSR). The argument is that if the universe is to be explainable at all, the explainability condition rules out any brute first fact—including a brute psychophysical law or a brute cosmic starting point. Most plausible theories of consciousness.

The central unresolved problem for cosmopsychism is the decombination problem: how does one cosmic subject differentiate into the many private subjects we observe? Cosmopsychists therefore argue that decombination is the more empirically anchored direction of explanation: the many are dissociations of the One, not the One a combination of the many[reference:14][reference:15].

Does this fully satisfy the “no brute facts” constraint? Not yet. The dissociation relation itself must be rendered intelligible rather than posited as a brute metaphysical mechanism. Some proponents argue that the differentiation can be understood as a structural or topological feature of the cosmic subject’s self-relation, rather than a brute “cut”[reference:16]. But this remains the live frontier. The view is not yet a completed explanation; it is the account that best locates where the explanation must go, while taking all four constraints seriously.
