Short answer: **neither implication holds as stated**. The surface narrative implies at most a **conditional, contingent plenitude**; the probabilistic structure implies at most an **indexical singularity**—one actual location for us—not metaphysical “one-worldness.”

**1. Surface narrative: conditional plenitude, not necessary plenitude**

The familiar narrative is:

> Posthuman civilizations could run enormous numbers of ancestor simulations; simulated minds would vastly outnumber biological ones; therefore we are probably simulated.

That is a **conditional scenario**, not a modal necessity. Bostrom’s formal conclusion is a disjunction:

1. civilizations go extinct before becoming posthuman; **or**
2. posthuman civilizations are not interested in running ancestor simulations; **or**
3. we are almost certainly in a simulation.[1]

The “many simulations” picture is only the third branch. It depends on contingent facts: survival, interest, resources, and choices. Bostrom explicitly leaves open that the interest factor may be near zero[1]. So the narrative does **not** imply that simulations must exist, let alone that *all possible* simulations exist. At most it implies:

> **If** posthuman civilizations survive, are interested, and run many simulations, **then** simulated observers may dominate numerically.

That is contingent plenitude, not necessary plenitude. “Necessary plenitude” would be the much stronger principle that every genuine possibility is realized, or that simulation is metaphysically unavoidable. Bostrom’s argument does not assert that.

**2. Probabilistic structure: indexical probability, not one world**

The probabilistic core is an **observer-selection** argument. Bostrom defines the fraction of observers who are simulated:

\[
f_{\text{sim}}=
\frac{f_P f_I N}{f_P f_I N+1}
\]

and then argues that, given no special information about our own observer-location, our credence in being simulated should equal that fraction:

\[
Cr(\text{sim}\mid f_{\text{sim}}=x)=x.
\]

This step is sanctioned by a “bland indifference principle” over observer positions[1].

That does **not** imply there is only one world. It quantifies over a reference class of observers that may include:

- biological observers in a base reality;
- simulated observers in many simulations;
- possibly nested simulations.

So the structure **presupposes multiplicity of observer-locations**, not metaphysical monism. What it implies is weaker:

> For us, there is a single actual answer to “are we simulated or not?”

That is an **indexical singularity**—one actual location—not the claim that only one world exists. The probabilistic structure is compatible with one base world containing many simulations, with nested simulations, or with a broader multiverse. It is not a cosmology; it is a model of self-location within whatever ensemble the simulation scenario describes.

**3. The apparent tension dissolves**

The two levels are doing different jobs:

| Level | What it supplies | What it does **not** imply |
|---|---|---|
| Surface narrative | A vivid, conditional many-simulations scenario | Necessary plenitude |
| Probabilistic structure | A credence about our observer-location | That only one world exists |

So:

- **Surface narrative → contingent/conditional plenitude**, not necessary plenitude.
- **Probabilistic structure → indexical singularity for us**, not metaphysical one-worldness.

Bostrom’s argument is best read as a **conditional epistemic trilemma**, not as a proof of plenitude and not as a proof that reality contains only one world. The surface story motivates one branch of the trilemma; the probability calculus tells you how to locate yourself within that branch if it obtains.
