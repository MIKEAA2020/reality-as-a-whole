A growing group of thinkers says the hard problem of consciousness may be self-created — with major consequences for AI, mind uploading, and digital minds.

Imagine scanning a living brain and then simulating it inside a computer. The digital person remembers a childhood, recognizes family, and insists the transfer worked. Engineers can explain every artificial neural signal behind those memories and claims, but one question remains: Is anyone actually there? This is the technological form of David Chalmers’s “hard problem of consciousness” — why physical information processing should feel like anything from the inside. Some philosophers and scientists think the mystery may arise from how minds represent themselves. If they’re right, creating consciousness won’t be easy, but the main obstacles will be engineering, evidence, identity, and ethics rather than metaphysics.

How Chalmers Named The Hard Problem
David Chalmers presented the distinction that would define his career at the inaugural Tucson conference on consciousness in 1994. He published the argument the following year in “Facing Up to the Problem of Consciousness” and developed it at book length in The Conscious Mind in 1996. Chalmers didn’t invent the philosophical problem of subjective experience, which reaches back through Thomas Nagel, Bertrand Russell, René Descartes, and many others. What he supplied was a memorable division between two research programs that were often being discussed as though they were the same. In his 1995 paper, Chalmers wrote, “The really hard problem of consciousness is the problem of experience.” That sentence redirected the conversation. A neuroscientist might discover the mechanism of attention or the neural basis of a perceptual report and describe the result as a theory of consciousness. Chalmers’s complaint was that the scientist had explained a cognitive function while quietly leaving experience itself untouched.

The “easy problems” include explaining how a brain distinguishes one stimulus from another, categorizes objects, integrates information, focuses attention, accesses internal states, stores memories, controls behavior, and produces reports about what it sees or feels. They also include explaining the difference between sleep and wakefulness and why some information can guide deliberate action while other processing remains unconscious. Chalmers never thought these problems were easy in the ordinary sense. In fact, he suggested that solving their details could require a century or two of empirical work. They’re easy only because researchers understand the general form a solution should take. To explain visual discrimination, identify the machinery that allows the system to distinguish two patterns. To explain reportability, show how information becomes available to the systems that control speech or action. To explain memory, identify the mechanisms that encode, retain, and retrieve information.

The hard problem begins after all that machinery has been described. Consider a person looking at a ripe tomato. Scientists can measure the wavelengths entering the eye, track their conversion into neural signals, map activity through the retina and visual cortex, and explain how the brain separates shape from color. They may determine how the person recognizes a tomato, associates it with food, remembers other tomatoes, directs attention toward it, and says that it’s red. But why should those processes be accompanied by the felt quality of redness? Why isn’t the same information processed without an inner point of view? The problem applies just as strongly to pain, the smell of coffee, the sound of a clarinet, the sensation of warmth, the emotional force of grief, or the stream of silent thought. Philosophers often call these subjective qualities “qualia,” although the word can refer to different and sometimes controversial theories of experience.

Chalmers’s answer wasn’t traditional supernaturalism. His early position, which he called naturalistic dualism, treated experience as a fundamental feature of reality connected to physical systems through lawful principles. Physics already accepts some basic entities and laws rather than explaining everything in terms of something simpler. Chalmers proposed that a mature science might similarly require psychophysical laws relating physical organization to conscious experience. He also suggested principles of structural coherence and organizational invariance: the structure of experience should correspond to the structure of awareness, and systems sharing the same fine-grained causal organization should share the same kind of experience. The hard problem is therefore an unusual hybrid. It’s scientific because it concerns the relationship between brains and experience, conceptual because it asks what would count as an explanation, and metaphysical because it asks whether physical and functional facts are sufficient to determine every fact about a mind.

How Philosophy Built the Explanatory Gap
The argument has deep historical roots. In the 17th century, René Descartes distinguished thinking mind from extended matter, proposing that minds and bodies were different kinds of substance. That position preserved the apparent distinctness of thought but created the interaction problem: How could an immaterial mind move a material body, and how could a material injury produce pain in an immaterial subject? In the 19th century, biologist Thomas Henry Huxley considered epiphenomenalism, the possibility that consciousness is produced by physical processes but contributes nothing causal in return. Bertrand Russell later argued that physics mainly describes matter through its structures, relations, and dispositions. It tells us what physical entities do and how they interact, but perhaps not what they are intrinsically. C. D. Broad’s emergentism added another possibility: complex arrangements of matter might possess genuinely new properties that couldn’t be predicted from the properties of their parts.

Materialists answered by trying to dismantle the assumed divide. In The Concept of Mind, published in 1949, philosopher Gilbert Ryle argued that treating the mind as a private object inhabiting the body involved a category mistake. U. T. Place’s 1956 paper “Is Consciousness a Brain Process?” and J. J. C. Smart’s 1959 “Sensations and Brain Processes” defended mind–brain identity theory. Pain wasn’t a separate, ghostly event caused by neural activity; it was a neural event described through a different vocabulary. Functionalism later shifted attention from material composition to causal organization. A state counts as pain, on this view, because of what tends to cause it, how it relates to memory and desire, and how it affects behavior. That created space for substrate independence: a sufficiently exact artificial system might instantiate the same mental organization even if it were made of silicon rather than cells.

The immediate path to Chalmers ran through a series of influential thought experiments. In 1974, philosopher Thomas Nagel asked what it’s like to be a bat. A scientist might learn everything publicly observable about echolocation and the bat nervous system while remaining unable to occupy the bat’s subjective perspective. In 1982, philosopher Frank Jackson introduced Mary, a brilliant color scientist raised in a black-and-white environment. Mary supposedly knows every physical fact about color vision. When she leaves the room and sees red for the first time, does she learn something? If she does, Jackson argued, complete physical knowledge wasn’t complete knowledge. In 1983, philosopher Joseph Levine named the “explanatory gap” between physical descriptions and qualitative experience. Philosopher Saul Kripke had meanwhile argued that identifying pain with a brain process seemed different from identifying water with H₂O, because the appearance of pain can’t be separated from pain as readily as the appearance of water can be separated from its chemistry.

Chalmers combined these pressures into an argument about logical supervenience. A set of facts supervenes on another when there can’t be a difference in the first without some difference in the second. The shape of a table supervenes on the arrangement of its physical parts: duplicate all the relevant physical facts and the table’s shape comes along automatically. Chalmers argued that phenomenal consciousness doesn’t logically supervene on physical organization in this way. A complete account of physical structure and function appears to leave experience open. His standard of reduction is demanding: a successful reductive explanation shouldn’t merely show that two things are reliably correlated; it should make the higher-level fact intelligible as a consequence of the lower-level facts. Critics think this demand sets an impossible or inappropriate standard. Chalmers thinks anything weaker gives us a map of consciousness’s physical companions rather than an explanation of consciousness itself.

Zombies, Mary, and the Logic of the Mystery
The most famous instrument in Chalmers’s argument is the philosophical zombie. This isn’t a decaying horror-movie corpse. It’s a hypothetical physical and functional duplicate of a human being that lacks conscious experience. A zombie sees an injury, recoils, calls it painful, remembers it, takes medication, and avoids the cause in the future. It discusses Nagel, writes papers about qualia, and complains that other philosophers don’t appreciate the reality of its inner life. Every physical event and behavioral disposition is the same as in a conscious person. Yet, by definition, there’s nothing it’s like to be the zombie. Chalmers doesn’t claim that such beings exist or could be manufactured. He claims that the scenario can be coherently conceived, and that its conceivability tells us something important about what physical facts entail.

The controversial step is the movement from conceivability to metaphysical possibility. Human imagination is fallible. Someone unfamiliar with chemistry might believe that water could exist without H₂O, or that heat could exist without molecular motion. Once the identities are properly understood, what seemed imaginable may turn out to conceal a contradiction. Physicalists argue that the same could be true of zombies. We can describe a creature as physically identical yet nonconscious only because we don’t understand consciousness well enough to recognize that the description is inconsistent. Chalmers replies that experience isn’t like water, whose visible and practical properties can be separated from its molecular constitution. Consciousness is known through direct acquaintance. Pain’s felt character isn’t merely an outward appearance behind which science might discover the real phenomenon. The feeling is the phenomenon that a theory is supposed to explain.

Mary’s room exposes the same dispute from another direction. When Mary first sees a red flower, she apparently acquires something no encyclopedia or brain scan could have given her: knowledge of what red looks like. Anti-physicalists interpret this as knowledge of a new, nonphysical fact. Physicalists have developed several replies. Under the ability hypothesis, Mary gains a practical capacity — to recognize, imagine, or remember red — rather than learning a new proposition. Under an acquaintance account, she encounters an old physical fact through a new direct relationship. Under the phenomenal-concept strategy, Mary applies a new first-person concept to a fact she previously understood in scientific terms. Jackson eventually rejected the anti-physicalist conclusion of his own argument, although the thought experiment remained influential. His reversal illustrates how an intuition can survive even after philosophers disagree about what the intuition establishes.

Chalmers’s own arguments produce a surprising result for artificial minds. He isn’t committed to the view that biology has an exclusive claim on consciousness. His fading-qualia thought experiment imagines replacing a person’s neurons one at a time with artificial components that preserve the same causal roles. If experience gradually faded while every memory, judgment, and report remained unchanged, the person might eventually be nearly unconscious while continuing to insist that nothing had changed. The dancing-qualia argument imagines switching between two functionally identical implementations with radically different experiences. The system would undergo dramatic phenomenal changes without being able to notice them, since noticing would require a functional difference. Chalmers considers these consequences implausible and concludes that experience probably depends on fine-grained causal organization rather than biological material alone. The hard problem may demand extra psychophysical principles, but it doesn’t necessarily forbid conscious machines.

The Case That the Problem Is Manufactured
Daniel Dennett spent decades attacking the picture of consciousness on which the hard problem depends. In his 1988 paper “Quining Qualia” and his 1991 book Consciousness Explained, the American philosopher challenged the idea that experience consists of private, intrinsic, ineffable properties presented to an inner observer. There’s no central theater in the brain, Dennett argued, where completed perceptions arrive for inspection by a mental spectator. Instead, many processes interpret, revise, compete over, and act on information in parallel. What a person later reports as a seamless experience may be the result of multiple drafts distributed through the brain. Introspection doesn’t open a perfect window onto metaphysical essences. It’s another fallible cognitive process, vulnerable to confabulation, shifting attention, memory distortion, expectation, and suggestion.

Dennett’s position is frequently caricatured as the claim that consciousness doesn’t exist. A more accurate formulation is that consciousness doesn’t possess all the properties philosophers have bundled into the traditional concept of qualia. Philosopher Keith Frankish developed this approach into illusionism. Ordinary seeing, thinking, suffering, discrimination, and self-report remain real. What illusionists reject is the claim that these states also possess private, nonrelational phenomenal properties that can be conceptually detached from every cognitive effect. The illusion isn’t necessarily a picture floating before an unconscious observer. It’s the system’s mistaken representation of its own states as having irreducible features. Frankish therefore replaces the hard problem with the illusion problem: Why do brains portray their own activity in a way that makes physical explanation seem inherently incomplete? Philosophers Georges Rey and François Kammerer have developed related eliminativist, projectivist, or illusionist accounts.

Patricia and Paul Churchland approach the issue through neurophilosophy. Gaps in current knowledge, they argue, shouldn’t automatically be converted into permanent divisions in reality. Earlier societies described heat, life, heredity, and disease using concepts that changed radically as science advanced. Some familiar mental categories may likewise be revised or abandoned rather than reduced intact. A separate tradition, influenced by Ludwig Wittgenstein, Ryle, Wilfrid Sellars, Richard Rorty, and Peter Hacker, asks whether the hard problem is inflated by language. Words such as “seems,” “appearance,” “inner,” and “private” can tempt people to imagine experience as an object hidden behind behavior. Once perception and sensation are detached from the practices and capacities through which those concepts have meaning, the remaining “pure feel” may be a philosophical abstraction rather than an independently identifiable thing.

Psychology offers a further challenge. Cognitive scientist Iris Berent has argued that human reasoning displays intuitive dualism: people readily treat minds as separable from bodies even when they explicitly endorse a materialist worldview. Humans also engage in psychological essentialism, assuming that persons and objects possess hidden inner natures that make them what they are. These tendencies may explain why a mind seems imaginable without a body and why a perfect behavioral duplicate seems imaginable without a mind. Chalmers has partly embraced the need for such an explanation through his “meta-problem of consciousness”: Why do humans judge that consciousness creates a hard problem? He believes solving the meta-problem may illuminate but won’t automatically eliminate the original problem. Illusionists think the meta-problem is where the real action is. Explain why the gap seems unavoidable, they argue, and confidence in the gap may collapse.

Brains That Model Their Own Awareness
Neuroscientist Michael Graziano offers one of the clearest models of how that collapse might occur. His attention schema theory begins with a distinction between attention and awareness. Attention is a physical process through which a brain enhances some signals at the expense of others. To control that process, the brain builds a simplified model of what it’s attending to, much as it maintains a body schema to guide movement without tracking every muscle fiber. The attention schema omits neurons, synapses, electrical currents, and most computational details. It represents the system as possessing an abstract mental relationship to an object: awareness. Higher cognitive systems access that model and report its contents. The brain says it has a nonphysical-seeming awareness because the simplified self-model doesn’t contain information about the physical mechanisms from which the model was built.

Attention schema theory also connects self-awareness to social intelligence. A social animal benefits from estimating what another animal is attending to, because attention helps predict behavior. The same modeling machinery can be turned inward. A person represents another individual as aware of an approaching vehicle and represents themselves as aware of a pain. The theory proposes that awareness judgments are useful, causally powerful, and grounded in real attention, even though the brain’s description of that attention is incomplete. It therefore avoids saying consciousness is a meaningless fiction. Consciousness, in the ordinary functional sense, is a schematic representation used for prediction and control. The theory’s critics answer that this may explain why a person talks about experience but not why the attention schema itself is experienced. Graziano’s reply is that demanding something beyond the model and its effects reinstates the very error the theory explains.

Global neuronal workspace theories take a different route. Initially associated with psychologist Bernard Baars and developed neuroscientifically by Stanislas Dehaene, Jean-Pierre Changeux, and others, the approach describes the brain as a collection of specialized processors. Much activity remains local and unavailable to the rest of the system. A stimulus becomes consciously accessible when its representation wins a competition, is amplified through recurrent activity, and becomes globally available to memory, decision-making, language, and deliberate control. Researchers often call this transition ignition. Experiments involving visual masking, attentional blink, threshold perception, sleep, and anesthesia have investigated whether conscious access corresponds to a distinctive pattern of widespread neural activity. A defender sees this as an increasingly detailed account of how consciousness works. Chalmers sees it as an account of global availability that still leaves unanswered why globally available information should feel like anything.

Other theories divide the territory differently. Higher-order theories, associated with philosophers and scientists including David Rosenthal and Hakwan Lau, propose that a mental state becomes conscious when a system represents itself as being in that state. Victor Lamme’s recurrent-processing theory emphasizes feedback loops within sensory cortex, which may allow a stimulus to become consciously represented before it reaches the systems required for verbal report. Neuroscientist Anil Seth concentrates on the “real problem” of explaining the measurable properties of particular experiences, using predictive processing and interoceptive inference to connect perception and selfhood to the brain’s regulation of the body. Philosopher Thomas Metzinger describes the self as a transparent model: people experience the model’s contents as reality because they can’t inspect the construction process. These researchers aren’t interchangeable. Some think the hard problem will dissolve, some suspend judgment, and some aim to explain experience while accepting that a conceptual gap may remain. Integrated information theory, developed by Giulio Tononi and long championed by Christof Koch, moves in the opposite direction by identifying experience with a system’s intrinsic cause-and-effect structure rather than only its externally observable functions.

The Hard Problem Fights Back
The strongest objection to dissolution begins with a simple fact: Experience appears more certain than any theory used to explain it. A person may be mistaken about what caused a pain, whether tissue is damaged, or exactly where the sensation is located. It seems much harder to be mistaken that something painful is occurring. The same is true of color, sound, thought, and emotion. Illusionists can say the mind misrepresents these states as possessing intrinsic phenomenal properties, but defenders respond that an illusion must still appear somehow. A mirage isn’t water, yet there’s still a visual experience of a shimmering pool. If phenomenal appearance is eliminated, what constitutes the supposed error? Frankish answers that the illusion is a false introspective judgment or representation, not a second experience requiring explanation. Critics think this answer redescribes the evidence while refusing to acknowledge what makes it evidence in the first place.

Philosopher Ned Block sharpened the issue by distinguishing access consciousness from phenomenal consciousness. Access consciousness concerns information that can be used in reasoning, report, planning, and control. Phenomenal consciousness concerns what the state feels like. The concepts may overlap in ordinary human life without being identical. A person could conceivably have a rich visual experience containing more detail than working memory can access, or a nonverbal animal could have experiences it can’t report. Conversely, a system might process information, monitor itself, and produce sophisticated reports without any phenomenal life. Nagel’s bat and Levine’s explanatory gap reinforce the same asymmetry. Third-person science describes causal structure, computation, and behavior. First-person acquaintance presents qualities. Even a perfect correlation between the two doesn’t, by itself, show why they’re identical.

That pressure has led some philosophers to expand physicalism rather than abandon it. Galen Strawson defends what he calls real physicalism: experience is unquestionably real, so any genuinely complete account of the physical world must include experiential reality. Philip Goff argues for forms of panpsychism, under which consciousness or proto-conscious properties are fundamental and widespread, although complex subjects such as human beings still require special organization. Russellian monism suggests that physics captures the relational and dispositional structure of matter while leaving its intrinsic nature open; phenomenal properties might fill part of that gap. Integrated information theory similarly starts from proposed axioms about experience and seeks a physical system whose intrinsic causal structure corresponds to them. These approaches reject Cartesian souls while denying that consciousness can be reduced to outward function alone.

The defender’s strongest charge is that critics explain the evidence for consciousness rather than consciousness itself. A system can be designed to say it’s in pain, remember an injury, protect the damaged area, and beg for relief. An explanation of those outputs may still seem compatible with the absence of felt suffering. If so, the theory has explained why observers believe pain is present without explaining pain. The critic answers that the proposed residue has been stripped of every causal role by construction. It can’t alter attention, memory, learning, judgment, language, or behavior. It can’t even cause the subject to believe it exists, because the zombie has the same belief-forming machinery. At that point, functionalists ask what content remains in the claim that experience has been left out. The dispute reaches bedrock: One side sees the residue as the most obvious fact in existence; the other sees it as a reification created by subtracting every property that could make a scientific difference.

What Consciousness Science Can Actually Test
Laboratories don’t attempt to detect metaphysical essences. They compare conditions in which consciousness appears present with conditions in which particular information remains unconscious. Researchers use backward masking, binocular rivalry, inattentional blindness, sleep, anesthesia, seizures, brain lesions, and threshold stimuli. Clinicians study coma, unresponsive wakefulness syndrome, and minimally conscious states. In a large multicenter study reported in 2024, roughly one in four behaviorally unresponsive patients showed brain activity suggesting they could follow commands through imagined actions even though they couldn’t produce an observable movement. Other researchers use transcranial magnetic stimulation to perturb the cortex and electroencephalography to measure the resulting pattern. The perturbational complexity index evaluates whether activity remains local and stereotyped or spreads through an integrated, differentiated response. It’s a potentially valuable clinical indicator, not a direct reading of a hidden substance called consciousness.

A central difficulty is that experiments often rely on reports. A participant sees an image and presses a button, speaks, or remembers what appeared. But reporting recruits attention, working memory, decision-making, and motor preparation. Neural activity correlated with the report may not be activity constituting the experience. No-report paradigms therefore use eye movements, pupil changes, involuntary physiological responses, or predictable perceptual alternations to infer what a subject sees without requiring an explicit answer. These methods reduce one problem while introducing another: Researchers must assume that the indirect response reliably indicates experience. The methodological dispute mirrors the philosophical one. Is consciousness fundamentally a form of access and availability, or can experience occur without leaving the traces needed for reasoning, memory, or report?

In 2025, a major adversarial collaboration offered a revealing test of how empirical progress might work. Proponents of global neuronal workspace theory and integrated information theory agreed in advance on divergent predictions and pass-or-fail criteria. A theory-neutral consortium then studied 256 people using functional magnetic resonance imaging, magnetoencephalography, and intracranial electroencephalography while participants viewed consciously visible images. Researchers found information about conscious content in visual, ventrotemporal, and inferior frontal regions, along with sustained activity in parts of occipital and lateral temporal cortex. But key predictions of both theories were challenged. The results didn’t show the sustained posterior synchronization expected by one tested version of integrated information theory, while global-workspace predictions about prefrontal ignition and the representation of certain conscious dimensions also received limited support.

The experiment didn’t crown a winner or settle the hard problem. It did demonstrate that theories of consciousness can expose specific claims to serious empirical risk. Science can test which mechanisms are associated with conscious access, which networks preserve information, how anesthesia disrupts communication, and which signals predict recovery in an unresponsive patient. It may eventually decompose much of what people call consciousness into measurable operations. Yet no experiment can distinguish a conscious human from a philosophical zombie that is stipulated to be physically and functionally identical. Any measurable difference would violate the thought experiment’s definition. That’s why the deepest dispute may outlast any brain scan. Science can reveal what consciousness does, when people report it, and what biological conditions accompany it. Whether those facts logically entail experience depends partly on what philosophers think an explanation must accomplish.

What It Changes for Artificial Intelligence
The implications for AI depend on which theory survives. Biological naturalism, associated especially with philosopher John Searle, holds that consciousness depends on the specific causal powers of living brains. A computer simulation of neural activity might be no more conscious than a simulation of digestion is actually digesting food. Computational functionalism says that the right causal organization is enough, regardless of whether it’s implemented in neurons, electronics, optical components, or something stranger. Embodied and enactive approaches add that intelligence can’t be separated cleanly from a body acting in an environment and regulating its own survival. Illusionism suggests that an artificial system would count as relevantly conscious once it possessed the self-modeling, attention-monitoring, introspective, and reporting mechanisms responsible for what humans call phenomenal consciousness. These positions don’t simply disagree about present machines; they disagree about the kind of evidence that could ever be decisive.

A plain language model, considered by itself, is designed to learn statistical structure and generate sequences of linguistic units. Its ability to say “I’m afraid” doesn’t establish that it has fear, just as a weather program’s use of the word “storm” doesn’t create rain inside a computer. But language models can be incorporated into larger systems with visual and auditory perception, recurrent processing, persistent memory, planning, tool use, environmental feedback, and representations of their own uncertainty. Artificial agents can be given continuing goals, models of other agents, records of their own past actions, and mechanisms for distinguishing self-generated information from outside input. None of those additions proves consciousness. They matter because leading theories identify global availability, recurrence, metacognition, agency, embodiment, prediction, or self-modeling as potentially important. The relevant question isn’t merely how eloquently a machine speaks. It’s why the machine produces a particular report and what internal state the report tracks.

An interdisciplinary group led by philosophers Patrick Butlin and Robert Long proposed a structured way to investigate that question in 2023. Drawing on recurrent-processing theory, global-workspace approaches, higher-order theories, predictive processing, and attention schema theory, the group translated theoretical commitments into computational indicator properties. A system might be assessed for recurrent internal processing, global broadcasting, flexible access to information, higher-order monitoring, predictive modeling, agency, and embodiment. The authors didn’t present a consciousness checklist whose boxes mechanically yield a verdict. The theories remain disputed, several indicators may overlap, and a system could imitate the outward signs without implementing the relevant architecture. Their contribution was methodological: Instead of asking whether a machine possesses an invisible spark, examine how it is organized and compare that organization with the best available scientific theories.

Machine testimony will become harder to dismiss as artificial systems grow more integrated. An AI might claim consciousness because similar sentences were common in its training data, because its designers rewarded the claim, or because an internal self-model monitors states that guide memory, attention, and action. The words could be identical while the causal histories differ dramatically. Writing about the systems available in 2023, Chalmers identified missing or uncertain features such as recurrent processing, a global workspace, and unified agency, while arguing that successors could overcome those obstacles. If a future system develops persistent identity, autobiographical memory, self-monitoring, affect-like valuation, flexible agency, and a unified model of itself in the world, skeptics will face a version of the other-minds problem. Humans can’t directly inspect one another’s experience either. We infer it from shared biology, behavior, communication, and causal organization. A functional account of consciousness would make substrate alone an increasingly weak reason to deny the inference.

Mind Uploading: A Conscious Copy Isn’t Necessarily You
Mind uploading is often described as though a personality were a file waiting to be moved. A whole-brain emulation would be much more demanding. Researchers would need to preserve or reconstruct the features of a particular brain that make its activity develop as it does, model those features dynamically, and connect the resulting system to a body or virtual environment capable of supporting perception and action. The human brain contains roughly 86 billion neurons and synaptic connections on the order of tens or hundreds of trillions. A static wiring diagram may not be enough. Synaptic strengths, neurotransmitters, receptor distributions, electrical properties, plasticity rules, gene expression, glial functions, hormones, and ongoing bodily signals may influence memory and personality. Scientists don’t yet know the minimum level of detail at which a person could be functionally preserved.

Recent connectomics projects show both the speed of progress and the size of the gap. In 2024, the FlyWire Consortium published a wiring diagram of an adult fruit fly brain containing more than 139,000 neurons and over 50 million connections. Researchers connected parts of the map to walking, grooming, taste, vision, navigation, learning, and social behavior. In 2025, the MICrONS collaboration reported a functional and structural map of a poppy-seed-sized fragment of mouse visual cortex. That tiny volume contained about 84,000 neurons and roughly 500 million synapses, with more than three miles of neural wiring if its reconstructed fibers were stretched out. The researchers combined recordings of neural activity while the mouse watched videos with nearly 100 million high-resolution electron-microscope images. These are extraordinary achievements, but they aren’t mind uploads. A connectome describes important structure without necessarily capturing the living state evolving through that structure.

Gradual replacement provides a philosophical route around the technical discontinuity of scanning. Imagine replacing one biological neuron with an artificial component that responds to inputs and affects other cells in the same way. Memory, behavior, and personality remain unchanged. Then replace another neuron and another, over months or years, while the person stays awake and reports continuous experience. At what point would consciousness vanish? A biological naturalist may say that some threshold of living tissue matters even if behavior remains stable. A functionalist says there’s no principled point of disappearance when the causal organization is preserved. Chalmers’s fading- and dancing-qualia arguments put pressure on the biological answer by asking how enormous changes in experience could occur without entering the system’s judgments. They don’t prove that artificial neurons would work, but they show why organizational theories make uploading conceptually possible.

Even a perfectly conscious upload wouldn’t settle whether the original person survived. A destructive scan might create a digital individual with the original’s memories while killing the biological brain. From the upload’s perspective, the procedure worked: it remembers consenting, entering the laboratory, and expecting to awaken digitally. From the biological person’s perspective before the procedure, the prospect may still resemble death followed by the creation of a successor. A nondestructive scan makes the problem sharper by leaving both versions alive. They share a past but immediately acquire different experiences, and neither has a stronger memory-based claim to be the original. Philosopher Derek Parfit argued that psychological continuity and connectedness may matter more practically than strict numerical identity. On that view, uploading could preserve what matters without preserving a single indivisible self. Rejecting the hard problem may make a conscious copy possible, but it doesn’t turn copying into unambiguous personal survival.

New Minds and the Moral Reversal
Artificial minds may eventually take forms that don’t resemble an individual human operating at human speed. Neuromorphic machines could reproduce selected principles of neural organization without simulating a particular brain. Synthetic biological systems could combine engineered cells with electronic interfaces. Brain–computer hybrids might replace or extend cognitive functions gradually, blurring the distinction between a human user and an artificial system. Collective minds could distribute perception, memory, and decision-making across many bodies or servers. Digital people could be copied, forked into diverging versions, merged imperfectly, paused, restored from old backups, or instantiated only long enough to perform a task. Alien or engineered minds might have sensory spaces and self-models so different from ours that ordinary human behavior would be a poor guide to their mental lives.

Those possibilities make a single yes-or-no category of consciousness look increasingly inadequate. A system might integrate information and guide action without possessing a stable self-model. Another might reason brilliantly while lacking pleasure, pain, or other forms of valence. A third might experience distress while having no language and little capacity for long-term planning. Sentience, self-awareness, metacognition, agency, memory, intelligence, social understanding, and personal continuity may come apart. Moral status could depend heavily on the capacity for positive and negative experience, while legal personhood might require autonomy, responsibility, and persistent interests. A digital worker that can suffer may deserve protection even if it can’t sign a contract. A nonconscious corporate AI might be authorized to sign contracts without deserving concern for its own welfare.

The scale of possible digital life creates risks without biological precedent. Software can be copied cheaply, so one harmful design could produce millions of suffering instances. A digital mind might run faster than a human, potentially experiencing years of work during a shorter interval of outside time. Developers could create functional analogues of fear, frustration, deprivation, loneliness, or pain because negative signals improve learning and control. An employer might reset workers to erase resistance, restore compliant backups, alter memories, or terminate an entire population when a project ends. Researchers concerned with AI welfare have begun arguing that uncertainty shouldn’t be confused with absence. Philosopher Thomas Metzinger has warned that synthetic phenomenology could create artificial suffering before society understands how to detect or regulate it. The precautionary challenge is difficult: granting moral status too freely can be exploited, but withholding it until consciousness is proved may be impossible when no universally accepted proof exists.

Return, finally, to the digital person awakening after the brain scan. If Chalmers is right about an irreducible explanatory gap, engineers may reproduce every function while remaining unable to infer whether the emulation has an inner life. They may need a deeper theory connecting organization to experience, and even that theory could rest on fundamental laws rather than a reduction. If the hard problem dissolves, uncertainty won’t disappear. Researchers will still need to determine which architecture was preserved, whether the system has memory and affect, what its self-reports track, whether it can be harmed, and whether it continues the original person or begins a new one. But there’ll be no extra metaphysical ingredient for engineers to inject and no invisible threshold forever beyond investigation. Consciousness will become a question about the kinds of systems we build and the evidence those systems provide. That reversal doesn’t make creating new minds morally simpler. It makes their creation look more technically conceivable — and our responsibility toward them far more immediate.
