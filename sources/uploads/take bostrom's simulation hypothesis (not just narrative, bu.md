An essential phenomenal property can still fit a strong form of identity if it has **no independent counterfactual freedom**.

Let \(P\) be the complete process-token and \(E(P)\) its phenomenal profile. The claim need not be that we can define “redness” in purely causal vocabulary. It can be that:

\[
\Box\bigl(P\text{ has this complete nature}\ \Rightarrow\ E(P)\text{ has this profile}\bigr).
\]

Then the phenomenal description may remain conceptually irreducible, while no possible process with exactly the same complete nature could have a different feel. The mapping is necessary, not a contingent bridge.

This is a better formulation than demanding simple reduction. It allows first-person concepts to retain their distinctive meaning, yet blocks a hidden variable that could vary freely alongside the process. In that sense, the identity theory’s requirement is not “phenomenal predicates must be definable from physics,” but “phenomenal facts must not be independently variable once the complete process is fixed.”

That could satisfy the intelligibility criterion even if the identity is known only a posteriori. The remaining burden is to justify the necessity claim rather than merely assert it. Structural correspondence and intervention evidence support it; structural sufficient reason rules out ungrounded variation. Together they make the identity route coherent, though not deductively compulsory.

The necessity claim becomes clearer if we distinguish **discovering an identity** from **deriving it from definitions**.

A phenomenal term can refer to an experience-token by direct acquaintance. A physical term can refer to a process-token by its causal history and intervention profile. These terms need not have the same meaning. If evidence shows they designate the same token, then the identity is necessary: it cannot be that the very same event is numerically a different event in another possible situation.

The reasoning is:

1. The subject directly identifies an experience-token \(e\).
2. Physical investigation identifies a process-token \(p\) through its causal role.
3. Stable, intervention-supported correspondence gives reason to identify \(e=p\), rather than posit a separate event plus a bridge.
4. If \(e=p\), their identity is necessary, even though it was learned empirically.

This is the model of an a posteriori identity: conceptual independence does not imply ontological independence.

The weak point is step 3. Correlation and intervention show systematic dependence, but a dual-aspect theorist can say the two terms refer to distinct aspects of one event. The identity theorist’s additional reason is explanatory economy: no independent causal powers, no separate event, and no observed degree of freedom for a second entity.

So the argument supports necessary token identity more strongly than it supports reduction of every phenomenal property. It does not settle whether phenomenal character is merely the process’s structural profile or an essential aspect of the same token. That distinction may be a difference in explanatory vocabulary unless it permits independent variation. If no independent variation is possible, the two accounts are close in ontology.

A useful formal test is **implicit determination**. Hold the complete causal process \(P\) fixed and ask how many phenomenal structures \(E\) are compatible with the identity theory’s constraints.

- If there is exactly one \(E\), up to relabelings that change no relation, then the phenomenal structure is implicitly fixed by \(P\). It has no independent degree of freedom.
- If several non-equivalent \(E\)’s remain, then \(P\) does not determine experience; a phenomenal selector or additional law is still needed.
- If no \(E\) matches, the proposed process criterion is wrong or incomplete.

This sharpens the earlier “unique map” condition. A correlation is not enough; the map must eliminate alternatives.

For pain, this means that once the complete sensory, affective, self-modeling, and temporal process is specified, there must be only one possible phenomenal profile—one pattern of unpleasantness, urgency, ownership, and unity. For color, the full process must fix the entire color-similarity structure, not just naming behavior.

At present, we do not have a set of independently justified constraints strong enough to prove that uniqueness. Structural sufficient reason would discard extra phenomenal assignments with no role, but it does not yet rule out every structured alternative. The next proof obligation is therefore exact: show that the causal process **implicitly determines** the phenomenal structure, rather than merely correlating with it.

A simple toy model shows what the uniqueness test demands. Suppose a process has two distinguishable states \(x_1,x_2\), with identical transition structure. The phenomenal side has two states \(e_1,e_2\).

If the only phenomenal fact is that \(e_1\neq e_2\), then both mappings

\[
x_1\mapsto e_1,\ x_2\mapsto e_2
\quad\text{and}\quad
x_1\mapsto e_2,\ x_2\mapsto e_1
\]

preserve the structure. The process does not select an absolute qualitative label. If the two mappings differ only by relabeling, this is harmless. If they imply different experiential relations to pain, memory, or other contents, the map is genuinely underdetermined.

Now add intervention structure. Suppose \(x_1\) selectively changes avoidance and attention, while \(x_2\) changes sensory discrimination. If the phenomenal structure independently distinguishes unpleasantness from intensity, those causal relations may anchor which state maps to which phenomenal dimension. The map can become unique up to harmless symmetries.

That is the role of intervention-rich structure: it can eliminate arbitrary assignments by grounding the phenomenal relations in distinct causal roles. But if an inversion remains after all causal, temporal, and first-person relations are fixed, the identity account has no further structural basis to choose.

So the test is not simply “is there a bijection?” It is: **does the full relational structure rigidly determine one phenomenal mapping, modulo automorphisms?** The framework can answer that in a specified model. It has not yet shown that the answer is uniquely yes for consciousness in general.

There is a formal way to state the same ambition. Build a theory with two vocabularies:

- \(L_P\) for physical process-states, transitions, interventions, and measures;
- \(L_E\) for phenomenal contents and their relations.

Add axioms \(T\) connecting them. Then ask:

> For each admissible physical model, is there exactly one phenomenal expansion satisfying \(T\), up to structure-preserving relabeling?

If yes, the phenomenal vocabulary is **implicitly determined** by the physical one. In suitable formal settings, implicit determination can imply that phenomenal predicates are explicitly definable from physical structure. That would be a rigorous version of the identity route: not “feel is just function” by stipulation, but “the complete process uniquely fixes the experiential structure.”

If no—if one physical model admits two non-equivalent phenomenal expansions—then the identity theory has not closed the gap. It needs an additional phenomenal law or a fundamental phenomenal structure.

This is not yet a proof because the connecting axioms are exactly what must be justified. But it turns the central question into a formal one: **does the full process theory uniquely determine its phenomenal expansion?** It also clarifies what the fallback means: phenomenal-first ontology permits multiple or independent phenomenal structures and treats that added structure as fundamental.

The next research step is therefore not another verbal defense of identity. It is to specify the shared language and constraints well enough to test uniqueness, first for a bounded case such as color or pain.

A warning: an implicit-definition theorem can become trivial if we define phenomenal states operationally. If we define “same experience” as “indistinguishable to every action, report, and memory test,” then the predictive-state construction yields unique equivalence classes. But those are **functional/access states**. The construction does not prove that they are phenomenal subjects.

That gives a precise two-stage test:

1. **Access-state derivation:** for a fixed interface, predictive equivalence uniquely identifies the minimal states used for observation and control.
2. **Phenomenal identification:** independently specified first-person contents must be shown to correspond to those states.

Stage 1 is mathematically tractable. Stage 2 is the hard problem. Defining the phenomenal vocabulary by Stage 1 would assume the identity rather than establish it.

So the formal uniqueness programme is valuable only if the phenomenal relations are independently constrained—for example, by first-person similarity, co-presence, valence, and temporal structure—then compared with the causal model. If the only available constraints are reports and discrimination, the result is an account of access consciousness, not yet an account of feeling.

This preserves the distinction you required: **minimal predictive states are candidate observer-centers, not automatically phenomenal subjects.** The identity route must earn the latter identification, or fall back to treating phenomenal structure as additional fundamental data.

To keep the phenomenal side independent without pretending it is publicly measurable, treat first-person acquaintance as a distinct **data source**, not as a theory of its own.

For a subject, construct \(\mathcal E\) from direct judgments about:

- which contents are present together;
- which contents are more or less similar;
- how experiences change over time;
- which states are pleasant, unpleasant, or mixed;
- which contents feel bodily owned or externally located.

These judgments may be incomplete or revisable, but they are evidence about the subject’s experience, not definitions of the physical process. The physical model is then built independently from interventions and dynamics, and the two structures are compared.

This procedure has limits. Introspection may distort, language may compress, and some contents may be unreportable. For nonverbal systems, we may have only indirect evidence, so the phenomenal mapping is an extrapolation rather than a measurement.

But that is still better than folding experience into behavior by definition. It lets us ask whether an independently described first-person structure is uniquely matched by the process model. If so, identity has evidential support; if not, the residual phenomenal structure must remain in the ontology or the theory must admit uncertainty.

The identity route therefore does not require a view-from-nowhere measurement of qualia. It requires a disciplined first-person/third-person comparison, with uncertainty about cases where first-person data are unavailable.

The identity route can be divided into three proof obligations, only one of which is currently close to mathematical:

1. **Canonical process states.**  
   For a fixed interface and policy class, histories can be grouped by predictive equivalence. The minimal predictive-state model is unique up to relabeling.

2. **Phenomenal correspondence.**  
   Establish that every independently specified first-person distinction—similarity, valence, unity, temporal character—maps to a distinction in that process model, and vice versa. This requires intervention-rich evidence; it is not yet a theorem.

3. **Identity from unique correspondence.**  
   If the map is complete, one-to-one at the token level, and unique up to harmless automorphisms, then structural individuation treats the process and phenomenal event as one token under two descriptions.

The vulnerable premise is step 2. A reportability-defined equivalence would make it easy but circular; independent first-person structure is needed. For humans, it can be investigated through phenomenological judgments and interventions. For non-reporting systems, the identity theory extrapolates from the process criterion.

This gives a concrete success condition: the theory must establish a unique, intervention-stable mapping for a domain, then generalize the same principle without introducing new thresholds. If it cannot, the fallback is not to discard experience, but to add the unmatched phenomenal relation as fundamental and explain its necessary coupling to the process. The second obligation—mapping process structure to first-person structure—has a natural order of attack:

1. **Calibrated human cases:** use first-person reports and similarity judgments alongside independent interventions.
2. **Dissociations:** test pain intensity versus unpleasantness, ownership versus sensation, and color appearance versus naming.
3. **Altered states:** test whether the same map predicts experience when ordinary reporting or external input is reduced.
4. **Cross-subject generalization:** apply the map to different sensory architectures without assuming identical qualitative spaces.
5. **Non-reporting systems:** extrapolate only where the same causal invariants are present, and state the uncertainty.

The result would not be certainty about another system’s private experience. It would be the strongest available inference from a validated identity principle. That is not unusual: science routinely infers hidden states from converging structure and intervention, though consciousness makes the epistemic asymmetry unusually sharp.

The phenomenal-first fallback would accept other minds’ experience as basic evidence and use it to motivate subject boundaries. The identity route instead treats shared process organization as the bridge to other subjects. Its advantage is a common criterion; its risk is that the criterion may be access-functional rather than phenomenal.

So a successful human map is necessary but not sufficient. It must generalize without being tailored to human reports, and it must predict the organization of experience in cases where report is absent or altered. That is the empirical test of whether the identity route is a genuine theory of consciousness rather than only a theory of human access.
