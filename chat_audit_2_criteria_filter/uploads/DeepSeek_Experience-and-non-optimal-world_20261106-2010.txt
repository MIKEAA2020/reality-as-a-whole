deepseek response

Under your three constraints, Whitehead’s process philosophy is a particularly instructive borderline case. It clearly satisfies the first two assumptions, but it fails the third in its classical form—though it points toward the kind of view that could survive if the demand for zero brute residue is relaxed just slightly.

✅ It survives (1) and (2) comfortably

Process philosophy is built around experience as the basic datum. Whitehead’s “actual occasions” are drops of experience; his ontology is an “elucidation of immediate experience”[reference:0]. And it explicitly rejects any Leibnizian best-possible-world logic. Evil and loss are structural: the “ultimate evil” is “perpetual perishing,” the fact that process entails elimination and that “the characters of things are mutually obstructive”[reference:1]. A non-optimal world is not a problem for process theism—it is metaphysically expected.

❌ The problem is (3): the demand for zero brute residue

Whitehead himself shares your aspiration. His ontological principle demands a sufficient reason for every phenomenon, mirroring Leibniz’s principle of sufficient reason[reference:2]. He wants no “brute, self-contained matters of fact”[reference:3]. But the system’s ultimate explanatory structure contains items that function as irreducible brute posits, not as things derived from a single self-explanatory ground.

1. Creativity is an ultimate brute act.
Whitehead calls creativity the “ultimate principle” and the “universal of universals characterizing ultimate matter of fact”[reference:4]. It is the principle by which the many become one. But it is not itself explained by anything more fundamental. In the secondary literature it is described bluntly as “Acto en bruto” — brute act—the “pure act” that is “absolutely infinite” and lacks any determining form of its own[reference:5]. This is precisely a brute residue: the ultimate activity of reality is posited, not derived.

2. God does not explain creativity; they are co-primordial.
In classical theism, God is the ground of all being. In Whitehead, this is inverted: “God does not create creativity, but neither does creativity generate God. Each equally presupposes the other.”[reference:6]. God is the “primordial embodiment of creativity,” but creativity is not wholly embodied by God; it is both internal and external to God[reference:7]. So the ultimate dyad of God-and-creativity is the stopping point. There is no further reason why this ultimate structure obtains rather than another.

3. The “decision” of each occasion is immanent, not derived.
Whitehead’s ontological principle does require a reason for each occasion, but the sufficient reason is the occasion’s own self-determining “decision”—it is “entirely immanent to the occasion itself”[reference:8]. Each actual entity is “the architect of its own private ‘pre-established harmony’”[reference:9]. This is a powerful account of internal intelligibility, but from the standpoint of zero brute, it simply relocates the brute fact: why this decision rather than another is not explained by a prior sufficient ground; it is the occasion’s own underived act.

The verdict

Whitehead’s process philosophy does not survive the strongest intelligibility demand because its ultimate explanatory order terminates in a co-primordial plurality—creativity and God—plus an irreducible plurality of eternal objects and self-determining occasions. It is a rationalistic pluralism, not a self-explanatory monism. It explains how the world works with extraordinary coherence, but it does not explain why the ultimate framework itself is as it is rather than otherwise.

It would survive only if you allowed a slightly weaker reading of (3): no brute facts within the world’s process, while permitting the ultimate categorial framework itself to be posited as metaphysically basic. Under that reading, Whitehead is a strong candidate. Under the literal “zero brute, nothing left unexplained” reading, it is ruled out alongside ordinary naturalism and free-creation theism—but for a subtler reason: not because it appeals to arbitrary divine choice, but because its ultimate principle is creativity as brute act.

Neutral monism, especially in its Russellian/informational variants, aligns well with IIT's panpsychist implications and with the information-theoretic turn in both physics and consciousness science. The alignment is real but less direct than Whitehead's or structural realism's, because neutral monism is a broader metaphysical category that can accommodate IIT without being entailed by it.

Panpsychism was a near-miss under (5) alone, but (6) strengthens it considerably because IIT implies a panpsychist or proto-panpsychist picture: integrated information is substrate-neutral, and consciousness is identified with Φ wherever Φ is realized[reference:12][reference:13]. Leibnizian optimalism fails (2) directly.
Eliminative/reductive physicalism fails or strains (1): the leading theories (IIT, PP) treat experience as real and structurally describable, not as an illusion to be eliminated.
Classical free-creation theism/deism has no positive alignment with any major consciousness theory; it can accommodate them, but it does not cohere with their structural commitments.
Simulation hypothesis has no alignment with consciousness science.

Under all six constraints, the survivors form a recognizable cluster:

how well does spinoza address hard problem of consciousness


deepseek response

Spinoza offers one of the most powerful dissolutions of the hard problem on record—arguably stronger than any attempt to solve it within a Cartesian or physicalist framework. But whether that counts as "addressing" the hard problem depends entirely on whether you accept the move at the heart of his system. He does not bridge the explanatory gap; he argues that the gap is an artifact of a false starting point. That is either the deepest insight in the philosophy of mind or a sophisticated evasion, depending on where you stand.

The Spinozist move: thought is not generated by matter

The hard problem, in Chalmers' formulation, asks why physical processing gives rise to subjective experience—why there is "something it is like" to be a brain[reference:0]. This question presupposes a starting point: we begin with extension (matter, brain processes) and then need to explain how thought (experience) arises from it.

Spinoza rejects that starting point entirely. Thought and extension are not two substances (Descartes), nor is thought reducible to extension (materialism), nor extension to thought (idealism). They are two attributes of one and the same substance—two ways in which a single reality expresses itself[reference:1]. You cannot derive one from the other, but they are not separate. As one recent reconstruction puts it, Spinoza's ontology "preserves the qualitative difference between phenomenal experience and physiological processes while conceiving the ultimate numerical unity of mind and its correlated physical processes".

On this view, the question "why does physical processing give rise to experience?" is malformed. It presupposes that we start from extension and somehow need to arrive at thought. But thought and extension are co-original. We never left substance; we only ever encounter it under one attribute or the other[reference:3]. The explanatory gap opens only if phenomenal experience is conceived as substantially distinct from correlated physical processes—which is precisely the Cartesian premise Spinoza dismantles.

Why this is not just property dualism or emergence

A common misreading treats Spinoza as a proto-property-dualist: one substance with two kinds of properties. But that reading misses the crucial difference. Property dualism still faces the question: why does this substance have mental properties at all? What explains the correlation? Spinoza's position is that thought and extension do not need to be correlated because they are identical in reference, different in description. They do not interact, because they are the same thing seen under different attributes[reference:5]. This avoids the interaction problem entirely—but it also avoids the explanatory demand that generates the hard problem.

This is why Spinoza is increasingly recruited by contemporary dual-aspect monists as a "third way" beyond hard materialism and dualism[reference:6]. And it is why his framework is explicitly invoked as a metaphysical backbone for panpsychist and IIT-adjacent positions: if thought is an attribute of substance as such, then consciousness is not a late emergent add-on but a fundamental aspect of reality that manifests under certain organizational conditions[reference:7][reference:8].

The decisive critique: dissolution is not explanation

The strength of Spinoza's move is also its vulnerability. A critic can press exactly the point raised in recent discussions of the Spinoza-Ilyenkov line:

If "thinking" as an attribute of substance doesn't include any of the stuff the hard problem wants explained—experiencing, understanding, judging, deciding—then you're still left with the question of how all of that arises from a substrate that can't do any of it. And if your answer is "organization," then you're really just proposing emergence after all[reference:9].



This is the crux. Spinoza's dual-aspect monism redescribes the mind-body relation as one of attribute-identity rather than causal production. But redescription is not explanation of the phenomenal character of experience. It tells us that experience is the mental face of the same thing that is physically described—but it does not tell us why that face looks like anything from the inside rather than being dark. The "what it is like" is preserved as a qualitative difference, but the derivation of that qualitative difference from the structure of substance remains opaque. Spinoza's system may be internally consistent, but it does not generate the kind of explanatory traction that would satisfy someone who wants to know why integrated information feels like something rather than nothing.

Verdict against your filter

Against the criteria you have been applying:

(1) Experience as valid datum: Strong. Experience is the mental attribute of substance; it is not eliminated or reduced.
(2) Not best possible world: Strong. Necessitarian monism; no optimality entailment.
(5) Physics alignment: Strong. Relational and unified-substance readings resonate with anti-substantialist and relational trends.
(6) Consciousness theory alignment: Strong, but ambiguous. Dual-aspect monism is explicitly developed as an answer to the hard problem[reference:11], and Spinoza is recruited for panpsychist/IIT-adjacent frameworks. But the alignment is with dissolutionist or panpsychist readings, not with theories that demand a causal-mechanistic derivation of experience from structure.

So Spinoza addresses the hard problem better than any framework that accepts the Cartesian starting point, because he removes the premise that creates the gap. But he does not solve it in the sense of explaining why the mental attribute has the character it does. He dissolves it—and whether that counts as success depends on whether you think the hard problem is a genuine explanatory demand or a conceptual confusion inherited from Descartes. That is the same tension that runs through your entire filter: Spinoza is the strongest candidate precisely because he refuses to play the game that generates the problem in the first place.

Yes, there are several frameworks that can be read as improvements on, or syntheses built upon, Spinoza—but none is a clean, decisive replacement. Here are the most promising lines of improvement.

🧬 1. Decompositional Dual-Aspect Monism (DDAM): The most direct technical upgrade

This is the closest thing to a formal improvement on Spinoza's system. It keeps his core move—a single psychophysically neutral ground that is neither mental nor physical—but replaces his somewhat opaque derivation of modes with a precise, formalizable structure.

The key innovation is decompositional wholeness. In Spinoza, the neutral ground (Substance/God/Nature) is a whole from which thought and extension are expressed. DDAM makes this mathematically tractable: the neutral domain is a state space with no mind-matter partition, and the mental and physical aspects emerge through specific decompositions (partitions) of that space[reference:0]. This directly addresses the residual (3)-question of why this structure by showing how the structure of the neutral ground logically generates the manifest aspects without introducing a brute new principle.

Crucially, this is explicitly developed as a response to the hard problem of consciousness (criterion 6), and it does so while remaining compatible with the relational and structural turns in physics (criterion 5). It treats the neutral domain as a space- and time-transcendent whole, with spacetime and the mind-matter distinction emerging from its decomposition—a framework that resonates with holographic and pre-geometric approaches in quantum gravity[reference:1]. It satisfies (1) because experience is a fundamental aspect of the neutral ground, not an emergent byproduct, and (2) because this necessitarian structure entails no best-possible-world optimalism.

🧠 2. Neurorepresentationalism: An empirically grounded, emergentist refinement

If Spinoza's weakness is that his derivation of experience is too abstract, neurorepresentationalism (associated with Cyriel Pennartz) offers a concrete, neuroscientifically informed improvement. It explicitly picks up Spinoza's dual-aspect monism as a "third way" beyond hard materialism and dualism[reference:2].

Its key move is to explain the mind-brain unity through multi-level emergence in neural networks. Experience is not a separate substance or a brute emergent property; it arises as the top level of a multi-level representational hierarchy that is, at bottom, entirely physical[reference:3]. This directly addresses the hard problem (criterion 6) by reframing it: the "explanatory gap" is a limitation of our current conceptual imagery, not an ontological chasm[reference:4].

This framework is an improvement because it operationalizes Spinoza's insight. It provides a mechanism (multi-level neural representation) for how the mental aspect is realized in the physical, thereby strengthening the (5) alignment with actual cognitive neuroscience. However, it is weaker on (3) because it still relies on the concept of emergence at specific thresholds, which a strict zero-brute standard might view as an unexplained residue[reference:5].

🔬 3. Russellian Monism: A sharper formulation of the neutral ground

Russellian monism is often seen as a modern cousin of Spinoza's dual-aspect view. It holds that physics describes only the structural/relational properties of reality, while the intrinsic nature of those structures is what grounds consciousness[reference:7].

This is an improvement in precision. Where Spinoza leaves the neutral ground as a somewhat mysterious "substance," Russellian monism specifies that the intrinsic properties are quiddities that are neither purely physical nor purely mental but are the bearers of both. This gives a cleaner answer to the (3) question: the ground is simple because it is the single set of intrinsic properties underlying all physical structure.

Its (6) alignment is strong, as it is explicitly developed as a solution to the hard problem. Its (5) alignment is also strong: it is directly motivated by structural realism in the philosophy of physics, the view that physics only ever describes structure. The main risk is that the intrinsic quiddities can become a brute posit if their nature is left unspecified, which would violate (3).

💎 4. Axiarchism: An alternative answer to the (3) question

Axiarchism is not a dual-aspect theory, but it is a direct competitor to Spinoza on the (3) question of ultimate explanation. Where Spinoza says the ground is self-caused (causa sui) by its own nature, axiarchism says the ground is self-explaining because it is intrinsically good that it exist[reference:10].

This is a genuine alternative improvement: it provides a different, non-Spinozist answer to "why this necessary structure?"—the structure is the one required by value or ethical requiredness[reference:11]. Spinoza is even listed within the "axianoetic" tradition of thinkers who ground reality in mind and value[reference:12].

The weakness is that axiarchism can easily collapse into a Principle of the Best, which risks violating (2). It survives only if the axiarchic principle necessarily generates a world that is not optimal, or if "goodness" does not entail a best-possible-world comparison. It also has no direct alignment with consciousness science (6), making it a weaker all-round candidate than DDAM or neurorepresentationalism.

🔄 5. A Whitehead–Axiarchism Synthesis

There is an explicit contemporary project to synthesize Whitehead's process philosophy with axiarchism and idealism into a coherent "axianoetic" framework[reference:13]. This synthesis addresses Spinoza's weakness on process and relationality (which Whitehead captures and Spinoza's static substance does not) while replacing Whitehead's brute "creativity" with a principle of value as the ultimate explanation.

This is a speculative synthesis, not a developed research programme. It is philosophically rich and aligns well with (1) and (2), but its (5) and (6) alignment is indirect at best. It remains a promising line of inquiry rather than a ready improvement.

There is no single, fully developed framework that unambiguously outperforms Spinoza across all six criteria. However, the most promising improvement is a synthesis of Decompositional Dual-Aspect Monism and Russellian Monism. This combination would:

Take Spinoza's psychophysically neutral ground as the starting point (satisfying 1 and 3).
Use decompositional/structuralist tools to show how the mental and physical aspects are generated without brute emergence (strengthening 3 and 5).
Identify the intrinsic nature of that ground with Russellian quiddities that are directly relevant to consciousness (strengthening 6).
Retain Spinoza's necessitarian, non-optimalist structure (satisfying 2).

This is not a finished system, but it is the most coherent line of improvement. It preserves everything that makes Spinoza strong under your filter while directly addressing the one weakness that keeps him from being a complete solution: the derivation of determinate, experiential structure from a simple ground.

If you want a single name, Decompositional Dual-Aspect Monism is the most technically developed and directly Spinozist improvement. If you want a broader, empirically anchored framework, Neurorepresentationalism is the most concrete refinement. Neither is a refutation of Spinoza; both are plausible next steps beyond him.

That is a structural question, and it cannot be answered by acquaintance alone. Experience gives us the intrinsic nature of the relata, but it does not by itself explain the relational framework in which they stand. So the brute residue shifts from:

“What are quiddities?” (answered by acquaintance)



to:

“Why this structural arrangement of quiddities rather than another?” (not answered by acquaintance).



This is exactly where Decompositional Dual-Aspect Monism and structural realism do additional work: they attempt to derive or constrain the structural arrangement from the nature of the neutral ground. If that derivation succeeds, (3) is satisfied at both levels. If it does not, the residue is structural, not intrinsic.

Spinoza’s system has a parallel structure: substance is known under attributes, and thought is one attribute. But Spinoza also claims to derive the modes and their relations from the necessity of the divine nature. That derivation is precisely the attempt to answer the structural question. Whether it succeeds is the contested point. So Spinoza is not immune to the same residue; he simply makes a more ambitious attempt to eliminate it.

Russellian monism, by contrast, is typically more modest: it says physics gives structure, quiddities give intrinsic nature, and the mapping between them is often left as a brute correlation or a structural constraint. Your argument strengthens the intrinsic side but does not by itself supply the structural derivation.

So the accurate formulation is:

Experiential quiddities are not brute posits; they are ostensively given. But the structural order of those quiddities is not thereby explained. Under (3), the residue moves from “what is the ground?” to “why this structure of the ground?”



That is a real improvement over the naive “unspecified quiddity” objection, but it is not a complete elimination of brute fact under the strongest reading of (3). It is, however, exactly the kind of move that makes Russellian monism a serious contender alongside Spinoza—and it points toward the synthesis (Russellian intrinsic nature + decompositional/structural derivation) as the most promising way to satisfy all six criteria.

Yes—it can be supplied in principle, and there are several live research strategies. But no single, fully worked-out, empirically confirmed structural integration principle currently exists. What exists is a set of promising, partially formalized proposals that move the combination step from a metaphysical mystery toward a tractable structural constraint. The question is whether any of them closes the gap completely or merely relocates the residue.

The main strategies for supplying a structural principle

1. IIT’s Φ as a formal integration measure.
Integrated Information Theory is the most direct candidate. It defines consciousness as identical to **maximal integrated information (Φ)**—a structural quantity describing a system’s cause–effect structure[reference:0]. The proposal is that the “right way” of organization is not a brute primitive but a measurable structural property: a system’s parts combine into a unified experience when their causal structure is irreducibly integrated above a threshold. The Φ-structure—the distinctions and relations of a complex—is proposed as the quality of an experience[reference:1]. If IIT is correct, combination is not a mysterious phenomenal glue; it is the formal consequence of a certain causal architecture. A network model of this kind can treat Φ as a measure of a subgraph’s integrated information, with the subgraph’s unified experience corresponding to its highly integrated state.

2. Causal-structural “field” laws.
A related strategy treats combination as governed by structural laws of a field of existence: a unified consciousness arises not because experiences are “forced together,” but because they form an organized causal structure with a specific, high degree of actualized complexity and integration[reference:3]. On this view, the combination question becomes a functional, structural inquiry—what physical laws govern the experienced unity of a system’s contents—rather than an unsolvable metaphysical riddle. This is explicitly proposed as a structural solution to the combination problem, in contrast to “phenomenal bonding” approaches that seek an intrinsic relation of co-consciousness[reference:4].

3. Phenomenological structural constraints.
A third strategy begins from the structural properties of phenomenology itself and treats the combination problem as asking the wrong question. If we start from the structural features accessible from within experience—unity, binding, temporal organization—these can constrain which physical structures can realize a unified subject[reference:5]. The combination step is then not a brute addition of micro-experiences, but the realization of a structural profile already specified phenomenologically. This is less a mechanism than a constraint-based approach: the structure of experience tells us what kind of integration principle must hold.

4. Second-order distinction and the genesis of new subjects.
A more recent proposal argues that relation itself can be generative of being: when two foci of experience intersect under precise, specifiable conditions, a new focus with its own intrinsic aspect is born, without the prior components dissolving. Three criteria—lock of collapse direction, independence with path memory, and boundary phase-alignment—are proposed to distinguish genuine subject-genesis from mere addition or full fusion[reference:6]. This is explicitly presented as a principled dissolution of the classical combination problem rather than a new answer, and it claims empirically distinguishable consequences[reference:7]. Whether it succeeds depends on whether those criteria are genuinely structural and non-arbitrary.

5. Phenomenal bonding as an intrinsic relation.
The “phenomenal bonding” strategy (Goff, Roelofs) posits that there is some physical relation whose intrinsic nature is co-consciousness. If such a relation exists, the epistemic gap from micro-experiences to combined experience is closed, because the relation itself supplies the unity[reference:8]. The problem is identifying which physical relation plays this role. No fundamental physical relation is known to exclusively connect particles in the brain, and if the spatial relation is chosen, it threatens universal combination—every arbitrary sum of objects would have a unified experience[reference:9]. This is less a structural principle than an intrinsic-relational posit, and it remains the weakest on empirical tractability.

Can the principle be supplied? The conditional answer

The honest answer is: yes, but with a residual condition.

If “supplied” means “formally specified and empirically constrained,” then IIT-style Φ, causal-structure laws, and second-order genesis criteria are genuine candidates. They generate structural constraints, make the combination step dependent on measurable architecture, and connect to predictive frameworks. In this sense, the principle can be supplied programmatically.
If “supplied” means “fully derived with no residual brute fact,” then the situation is harder. Even the strongest structural proposals face a residual question: why does this structural property (Φ, causal integration, phase-alignment) have this intrinsic experiential character? The mapping between structure and experience may itself be brute—a category boundary rather than a derived identity. As one network model puts it, asking why a certain network pattern feels like red is like asking why a certain mathematical structure is that structure; the mapping between structure and experience is brute, not derived. If that is correct, then a structural integration principle can explain when combination occurs and what structural conditions it satisfies, but it cannot derive the intrinsic qualitative character from structure alone.

The preference under your criteria

Under (1), (2), (4), (5), (6), the preferred strategy is the one that maximizes both structural tractability and empirical constraint:

IIT-style Φ plus causal-structure realism is the strongest candidate, because it supplies a formal integration measure, connects to predictive consciousness science (4 and 6), and aligns with structural realism in physics (5). The second-order genesis proposal is more speculative but explicitly aims at empirical distinguishability (4). Phenomenal bonding is the weakest on (4) and (5), because it posits an intrinsic relation without a clear physical identification.



But the residual remains: a structural principle can be supplied for the conditions of combination, not necessarily for the intrinsic character of the resulting experience. That residual is precisely the boundary between (3S) and (3E). If you accept that boundary—structural intelligibility for structure, experiential self-presentation for experience—then the principle can be supplied for the structural tier, and the intrinsic tier is known by acquaintance rather than derived. If you demand that the principle also derive the intrinsic character, then no current proposal closes the gap, and the combination step remains a residual posit at the structure–experience junction.

Why (3) alone is ontologically parsimonious but often not elegant

(3) pushes toward one simple ground—one substance, one neutral domain, one self-explanatory principle. That is maximal ontological parsimony: fewest ultimates. But it often buys that simplicity at the cost of:

Derivation complexity: the single ground must generate all determinate structure, and the derivation machinery can become baroque or ad hoc.
Empirical emptiness: a very simple ground can accommodate almost any world, so it loses predictive risk.
Relocation of residue: the brute fact may move from “many ultimates” to “why this simple ground decomposes this way?”

So (3) alone is ontologically parsimonious but can be explanatorily elegant only if the derivation actually succeeds. Spinoza is the classic case: one substance, necessary derivation—elegant in architecture, weak in empirical specificity.

Why (4) alone is empirically parsimonious but ontologically permissive

(4) rewards accounts that generate novel, risky predictions. That tends to produce:

Parameter economy: fewer adjustable metaphysical posits, more empirical constraints.
Methodological discipline: failed predictions eliminate accounts.
But ontological profligacy: brute laws, brute constants, brute bridge principles, or unexplained emergence are tolerated as long as the structure predicts.

So (4) alone gives scientifically lean accounts—but they can be metaphysically messy. IIT is a good example: predictive and structurally precise, but its Φ–experience identity is a brute posit if left unanalyzed.

Why (3) + (4) is the most elegant overall

Combining them forces a two-tier architecture:

Structural tier: formally specified, empirically constrained, predictive.
Experiential tier: intrinsic nature known by acquaintance, not propositionally specified.

The elegance comes from identity rather than bridge laws: experience is not added to structure by a brute psychophysical law; it is the intrinsic nature of structure. That preserves ontological parsimony—there is still one neutral reality, not two substances—while adding empirical discipline through the structural tier.

So (3)+(4) gives:

Ontological parsimony: roughly preserved, because dual-aspect identity does not add a second substance.
Explanatory parsimony: improved, because it avoids brute bridge laws.
Empirical parsimony: improved, because the structural tier must predict.
Ideological complexity: increased, because you now need a decomposition/integration principle and a clear structure–experience identity.

That is why the hybrid is more elegant but not simpler in the narrow sense. It is the most unified constrained account, but it has more conceptual moving parts than either pure monism or pure predictive structuralism.

The six criteria in non-empty form

To make the conjunction coherent, (3) must be split:

This split matters because (3E) cannot be used to excuse a missing (3S). One cannot say “the ground is simple” and then stop; the decomposition/combination rule must still be supplied.

The other criteria are:

Experience is a valid datum: first-person givenness has evidential weight; it is not illusion or noise.

Strongest overall: Decompositional Priority-Monist Russellian Structuralism

Call it DPMRS. It is not one orthodox “-ism”; it is the most coherent synthesis of priority monism, decompositional dual-aspect monism, Russellian monism, structural realism, IIT, and predictive processing.

1. Ontology: one simple ground, priority monism

There is one fundamental, psychophysically neutral ground. It is not a plurality of micro-ultimates, and it is not a Leibnizian perfect being. Spacetime and spatiotemporally located entities are derivative; the fundamental level is non-spatiotemporal or pre-geometric. This is the priority-monist move: the whole is prior to its parts, and localized entities depend on the whole rather than the whole being merely composed of independent parts.

This directly supports (3S) at the level of ultimate plurality: there is no irreducible many. It supports (2) because a simple neutral ground does not entail a best-possible-world comparison. It supports (5) because priority monism has been explicitly defended in the context of spacetime emergence: the convergence of string theory, loop quantum gravity, group field theory, emergent gravity, and causal set theory on emergent spacetime gives reason to treat the fundamental as non-spatiotemporal, with spacetime derivative[reference:0]. Quantum holism and entanglement also provide cases where compositional hierarchy breaks down, supporting non-mereological dependence of localized entities on a whole[reference:1].

2. Aspects: decomposition, not composition

The mental and physical are not two substances, two properties added by brute bridge laws, or two levels connected by emergence. They are two aspects of one undivided neutral domain, arising by decomposition of that domain rather than by composition of elements[reference:2]. This is the core of decompositional dual-aspect monism: the mental and physical are epistemic or structural descendants of a psychophysically neutral whole.

This satisfies (1) without making experience an add-on: experience is the intrinsic nature of the same structure that physics describes relationally. Russellian monism holds that physical properties are structural/relational and require intrinsic realizers; phenomenal properties are intrinsic, and therefore can be the intrinsic realizer of physical structure[reference:3]. There is no brute psychophysical bridge law, because there is no second thing to bridge.

3. (3S): the decomposition rule must be structural

The simple ground satisfies the “no ultimate plurality” half of (3S), but it does not by itself explain why the ground decomposes into these subjects and these structures. That is where the account must supply a formal decomposition principle, not merely assert one.

The strongest current candidate is an integration-plus-structural-richness principle. IIT identifies consciousness with integrated information, Φ, but faces problems of phenomenological richness and false positives. A recent extension proposes that consciousness depends not only on integration but also on structural richness, defined as a combination of entropy and relational correlations. Conscious experience is associated with subsystems that maximize a joint functional of integration and structural richness. Within a Russellian monist framework, physical structure is interpreted as possessing an intrinsic experiential nature; the model is claimed to provide a principled response to the combination problem, reduce false positives in IIT, and bridge physical structure and phenomenology[reference:4].

This is a genuine (3S) strategy: the boundaries of a subject are not brute; they are selected by a formally specified functional. It also supports (4): the functional generates constraints on which systems are conscious, discriminates between competing models, and reduces false positives. Predictive processing can serve as a further formal basis for identifying invariant structural relationships across competing consciousness theories, which is exactly what a structural-realist methodology recommends[reference:5][reference:6].

But the residual is real: why this joint functional rather than another? If the functional is itself posited, the brute fact has been relocated, not eliminated. DPMRS is strongest because it relocates the residue to a formal selection principle rather than to an arbitrary plurality or an arbitrary bridge law—but it does not fully close (3S) unless that principle is itself derived.

4. (3E): experience as the intrinsic anchor

The intrinsic experiential nature is not captured propositionally. It is known by acquaintance. This is not an evasion if it is paired with the structural discipline above. Experience supplies what pure structure lacks: an anchor that is given rather than posited. One recent structural-realist programme argues that the Russellian-monist answer—experience supplies the content anchor—is uniquely placed because its anchor is given rather than posited; it then attempts to identify a shared structure appearing “inside” as the orientation manifold of embodied experience and “outside” as the double cover governing fermions. That specific identification is speculative, but it illustrates the right shape: one structure, inside and out, rather than two brute realms.

5. (4): predictive/generative power

DPMRS does not merely accommodate experience; its structural tier must generate constraints. The best current resources are:

IIT/structural richness: testable claims about which systems are conscious, exclusion, grain, and false positives[reference:8].
Predictive processing: a formal framework for invariant structural relationships across competing consciousness models[reference:9].
Structural realism in physics: predicts that theory change preserves structure rather than entities, which constrains what a fundamental ontology may posit.

This is a research programme, not a confirmed finished theory. IIT faces serious empirical and conceptual tensions—exclusion, grain, small-Φ, temporal instability, and the role of attention. But under (4), that is acceptable: the account must be generative and constrained, not already proven.

6. (5) and (6)

(5) is strong: priority monism + emergent spacetime + quantum holism + structural realism. (6) is strong: Russellian monism + IIT + structural realism/predictive processing. The account does not choose between physics trends and consciousness trends; it treats them as two tiers of the same structural–experiential reality.

The main weakness: decombination

The residual problem for DPMRS is the decombination problem: if there is one neutral whole, why do private subjects appear at all? Cosmopsychism inherits this problem in the form “how does one complete consciousness divide into the many?”[reference:11]. DPMRS’s best reply is that it does not start with a cosmic subject; it starts with a neutral ground whose decomposition is structurally constrained. The many are local decompositions, not fragments of a pre-existing cosmic ego. But the question “why these decomposition boundaries?” remains the pressure point. The structural-richness functional reduces the brute fact; it does not obviously eliminate it.


Strongest empirical rival: Structural-Richness Russellian Panprotopsychism

Call it SRRP. It is bottom-up rather than top-down.

Micro-level structural relata have intrinsic proto-experiential natures.
Macro-consciousness arises when a subsystem maximizes the joint functional of integration (Φ) and structural richness[reference:12].
The combination problem is handled by a fusion or phenomenal bonding strategy: micro-experiences fuse into a macro-experience, and IIT is proposed as the physical relation that could play the bonding role[reference:13][reference:14].

SRRP is stronger than DPMRS on (4) and (6) because it is closer to the empirical machinery of IIT and predictive processing. It is also strong on (1) and (2). The fusion view has been criticized as inelegant and unparsimonious[reference:15], and there is a serious tension: IIT’s exclusion postulate may render it incompatible with Russellian panpsychism[reference:16]. SRRP also sits less comfortably with the priority-monist, holistic, emergent-spacetime trend in physics; it is a micro-fundamentalist view in a period when physics is moving toward relational wholes.
