# Graded rivalry, eye signals and the limits of report

## Bottom line

[Naber, Frässle and Einhäuser (2011)](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0020910) provide more than an abstract argument about unreliable reports. They compare physiological eye signals, binary button presses, graded joystick reports, and physical controls. Their results support **graded global measures of rivalry** and show why a forced binary response can miss intermediate states. But a graded global signal does not by itself establish whether experience is graded at each location, a spatial mixture of locally discrete percepts, or something else. Nor does a faster eye signal prove that an unreported experience was absent or present.

The article's authors argue that rivalry is globally gradual and identify spatially piecemeal rivalry as one possible explanation. That is a useful, more nuanced claim than saying that their experiments prove a universal law that every conscious experience varies continuously. The previous [Frässle 2014 audit](FRASSLE_2014_NO_REPORT_CALIBRATION_AUDIT.md) assessed a later fMRI study from overlapping authors; this earlier work helps explain its proxy choice but is not independent calibration of its passive condition.

**Source scope:** I read the primary PLOS article's introduction, all four results sections, discussion, methods and figure captions, plus the abstract of [Fahle et al. (2011)](https://doi.org/10.3389/fnhum.2011.00120). I did not obtain or analyze individual participant recordings or reproduce published statistics. The constructions in section 4 are invented mathematical illustrations, not experimental measurements.

## 1. Four experiments, distinct questions

| Experiment | Sample and observation | Contribution and limit |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Ten observers; pupil size under several luminance/contrast rivalry conditions and binary reports. | Pupil changes tracked reported percept when stimuli stayed fixed; observer report and pupil were not independent phenomenal ground truths. |
| 2 | Eight observers; pupil responses compared with abrupt simulated transitions. | Pupil changes around abrupt physical transitions were steeper than around rivalry; per-transition slope and unnormalized analyses address particular averaging and scaling objections, not every possible channel difference. |
| 3 | A different group of eight; opposing moving gratings, OKN, button report, passive viewing and abrupt simulation. | OKN varied with reported motion; the authors report shorter eye-inferred dominance durations with active report for selected settings. They note ambiguity between true short dominance and reflex noise. |
| 4 | The same eight as experiment 3; analog joystick report and OKN, with rivalry and a smooth spatial replay. | Intermediate joystick positions were reported; eye and joystick signals covaried, but global measures do not reveal the local structure of every percept. |

In experiments 3 and 4, observers could release buttons or center the joystick if neither percept dominated. That differs from experiment 1's instruction to choose the most dominant percept and from the 2014 experiment's forced dominant button. The studies' stimuli, coding, preprocessing and samples differ; numerical agreement or latency is not automatically interchangeable across them. Experiments 3 and 4 included active and passive trials, but **the authors did not measure passive pupil conditions** in the pupil experiments; the specific active/passive result emphasized in experiment 3 is OKN-based.

For experiment 3, the authors report mean OKN acceleration around transitions of `36.8 ± 3.7 deg/s²` for abrupt simulated transitions versus `21.7 ± 1.6 deg/s²` for rivalry. In experiment 4, smooth replay used a moving spatial border with a 14-degree overlap; corresponding reported accelerations were `31.8 ± 4.6` versus `29.7 ± 4.5 deg/s²`, with `p>0.25`. A nonsignificant difference is not equivalence, and matching these particular summaries does not establish identical phenomenal trajectories. Still, the combination is a useful control: it challenges the idea that all slower rivalry-associated changes must arise from an invariant sluggish eye response to an abrupt change.

## 2. What graded reports add—and what they do not

In experiment 4 the paper reports peak OKN–joystick correlations of approximately **0.70 ± 0.03** for rivalry and **0.81 ± 0.03** for smooth simulation. The paper prints an extraneous `s` beside these correlation values; correlation is dimensionless. The corresponding peak lags, where OKN led joystick, were **0.86 ± 0.09 s** and **0.51 ± 0.10 s**. These are cross-correlation summaries, not percent correct, class-specific sensitivity, or measured times between neural events and experience. The paper describes the `±` values in Figure 5C as mean and standard error across dominance durations; they are not independent-participant confidence intervals. The reported group comparison uses its stated statistical tests; raw data would be needed to assess its dependence and uncertainty independently.

Observers sometimes held the joystick near the middle for a sustained interval, rather than merely passing through it. Their first-person reports are positive evidence for mixed or intermediate dominance in these conditions, not merely noise to be discarded. Yet the joystick is one global, motor-mediated response, and intermediate positions could express spatial mixture, perceived fading, uncertainty, or a mixture of these. Without additional local, time-resolved and target-valid evidence, one cannot read a unique distribution of local experiences from the scalar position. This does not deny the validity of first-person experience; it distinguishes the participant's experience from what a single standardized response can communicate about its detailed spatial contents.

The article also compares latencies in physical replay and finds that eye measures can respond sooner than buttons. But a response leading a button does not establish the exact onset of the experienced content. Under the stated timing analysis, response latencies longer than two seconds were excluded as missed transitions; this limits what the reported latency summaries say about long delays and misses. It should not be silently treated as an exclusion rule for the separate cross-correlation calculation.

## 3. Report effects, filtering and the comparison with 2014

For a range of eye-signal smoothing and sign-change thresholds, the authors compare inferred dominance durations with button-derived durations. Some short eye-inferred periods were more common in rivalry than in simulated trials. They explicitly recognize that a short reflex-defined period might reflect noise rather than a true dominance interval, and that simulated rivalry supplies a known **physical** switching schedule. That schedule does not provide direct ground truth for every subjective state in real rivalry: physiological noise or response dynamics could differ across stimuli or task conditions, and replay is not the same visual input.

With parameters selected to closely match active OKN and button durations, experiment 3 reports shorter OKN-inferred dominance durations for active reporting than passive viewing (`t(7)=3.21, p<0.05`). This is consistent with an influence of reporting context, potentially including attention, on rivalry or its measurement; it does not by itself isolate a pure motor effect or independently validate passive experience labels. The later 2014 fMRI study found a significant active/passive duration difference for its **pupil** stimulus but not for its **OKN** stimulus. These results are not a direct contradiction: stimuli, tasks, samples and analysis differ, and the later null is not proof of no effect. They do caution against declaring one fixed report-effect size or universal measurement channel.

The 2011 paper's sensitivity analysis is a strength: it does not rely on one arbitrary smoothing setting. Its findings also show why a new protocol should freeze preprocessing and present sensitivity analyses, rather than tuning until eye and button durations happen to match. Identified differences in rates or durations should be treated as eye-based operational effects unless a target-specific bridge supports a stronger experiential interpretation.

## 4. Two finite illustrations of what can be hidden

These examples are **not** Naber's stimuli, data, perceptual mechanisms or fitted eye model. They test simple consequences of the specified calculations only.

### The same global curve can have different local organization

Imagine four positions. In one sequence each local numerical dominance score is either 0 or 1, but the positions change one at a time: `0000`, `1000`, `1100`, `1110`, `1111`. Their mean is `0, 1/4, 1/2, 3/4, 1`. In a second sequence all four positions share the same intermediate score at each step: `(0,0,0,0)`, `(1/4,1/4,1/4,1/4)`, and so on up to `(1,1,1,1)`. The mean is identical at every step although the local patterns differ. Any hypothetical readout defined **only** as that mean, or as the same deterministic function of it, gives the same global trace in both cases.

This is not a claim that a fraction is a phenomenal quality or that actual OKN or a joystick computes a linear spatial average. It illustrates why global graduality alone does not distinguish a moving boundary of local dominance from a locally graded score. The real study's spatial replay and graded reports offer evidence relevant to that question, but do not identify local states just by matching a global summary. A location-specific measure or report could distinguish these finite patterns if it genuinely measures the relevant local variable; obtaining and validating such a measure is an additional empirical problem.

### A short excursion may disappear under smoothing

For the sequence `−1,−1,+1,−1,−1,−1`, take a causal three-sample moving average with a `−1` baseline before the sequence. Its output is `−1,−1,−1/3,−1/3,−1/3,−1`: a detector requiring a positive value sees no reversal. An otherwise similar **two-sample** positive excursion produces positive averages. Thus detecting short switches can depend on smoothing and detection rules even when the underlying input really changes. A noisy channel can also produce false switches; neither possibility decides what happened in Naber's participants. Their actual preprocessing, thresholds and parameter sweep are more complex than this toy.

## 5. A focused empirical next step

A prospective, consent-based and ethics-reviewed human study could combine the strengths of both papers:

1. **Define local and global targets.** Use moving rivalry stimuli, continuous global joystick or buttons in one condition, and a separate condition with randomly timed post-window prompts about a *preselected local region*. Permit mixed, uncertain and missing responses; counterbalance region, color, motion and timing. Prompt expectations can themselves alter perception, so call this a prompt-possible condition, not pure passive viewing.
2. **Freeze and validate the readout.** Train lag, smoothing, thresholds and any local decoder on separate development runs or people. Evaluate held-out agreement with reports, report full denominators and uncertainty, and vary filtering only in prespecified sensitivity analyses. A local probe may change attention or mask perception: use matched no-probe controls, measure probe effects, and stop if it invalidates the target.
3. **Compare discriminating predictions.** Predefine a spatial-mixture model and a locally graded model that both predict similar global trajectories but make **different local report or independently validated local-measure predictions**, including their timing and error models. Test whether the observed differences fall outside the rival's uncertainty range. Real local transitions need not be uniform or independent; spatial sampling and temporal dependence limit resolution.
4. **Separate passive transport.** Compare randomized-prompt blocks with true no-prompt blocks using the available physical signals and controls. Similarity of observable signals cannot alone prove that the experiential readout has identical error in both. Do not infer a passive error bound without a justified target-condition anchor or transport premise.

The proposed result would constrain a **particular visual-content model**, not identify a subject from eye movements or explain why a percept has its specific qualitative character. If the local models' predicted observations overlap under defensible error bounds, the correct result remains inconclusive. This extends the existing [random-audit proposal](RIVALRY_RANDOM_AUDIT_EXPERIENCE_BRIDGE.md) from a binary global motion target to a more demanding spatial target; it does not claim that the new protocol was run or that the required experiential calibration already exists.
