## 1. Bayne and Chalmers: what unity requires

**Argument, sections 1–2.** The authors separate unity of experiences directed at one object, unity in a shared spatial framework, unity in belonging to one subject, and *subsumptive unity*: experiences occur as parts of a single encompassing conscious state. They also distinguish phenomenal unity—what it is like to have experiences together—from access unity, joint availability for reasoning or report. A unity thesis formulated merely in terms of same-subject ownership is too thin to answer the substantive question. They suggest a strong, synchronic, subject-indexed subsumptive thesis: every set of a subject's conscious states at a time is subsumed by a single conscious state of that subject. They argue for its plausibility, not a conclusive proof.

**Objections and formal scope, sections 3–4.** Disunity, divided attention and split-brain cases require care about which states are actually conscious, which are simultaneous, whose they are, and what phenomenal unity rather than access requires. The authors' response does not settle these empirical and philosophical disputes. Their finite pairwise-to-general closure argument assumes subsumption is transitive and relies on repeatedly subsuming states **of the same subject**, including the states produced by prior subsumptions. It does not follow merely from a bare pairwise relation whose witness can change from pair to pair. Infinite cases require a further assumption. They also discuss a total-state version and their argued equivalences, including the nontrivial relationship between phenomenal type-entailment and subsumption: duplicate tokens or gestalt dependence make unrestricted substitution unsafe. These are philosophical claims with premises, not theorems about arbitrary networks.

**Explanation, final sections and conclusion.** They examine higher-order and functional/representationalist approaches and argue that particular independently generated states do not automatically yield the requisite encompassing conscious state; possible modifications remain open. A top-down holistic picture is a proposal about how a total state might ground its parts, not an established solution. Most importantly for this project, the authors explicitly leave open how experience is packaged into fields and whether fields can overlap. The fact that an encompassing state is defined as maximal does not by itself prove exclusive subjects.

**Correction to our use.** The hypothetical family `{AB, AC, BC}` without `{ABC}` is not a counterexample to their same-subject finite closure: it says only that *some* field contains each pair, potentially a different field each time. Nor should their subject-indexed premise be invoked to settle which subject owns a given token. See the [partition gate](PHENOMENAL_UNITY_AND_PLURAL_SUBJECT_IDENTIFICATION_GATE.md) and [pair–triple gate](TRIPLE_UNITY_RANDOMIZED_REPORT_PARTITION_GATE.md), which are explicitly conditional.

## 2. Schwitzgebel and Nelson: countability is a substantive commitment

**Scope, sections 1–3.** The authors focus on conscious subjects as bundles of experience individuated by maximal phenomenal unity, rather than assuming that a subject is identical to an organism, agent, person or persisting self. They caution that experiential aspects themselves may not have a unique atomic decomposition (note 4). They argue that complex, multidimensional processes make a universally sharp integer census questionable. This is an argument about possible cases, not a measurement of fractional human subjects.

**Gradual change, sections 4–5.** In zero-to-one scenarios the choices include consciousness throughout, no consciousness throughout, a sharp threshold, or an intermediate/indeterminate transition. In one-to-many cases the analogous options include an unchanged nonzero count, no subjects, a sharp jump, or gradual/intermediate/indeterminate cases. Which options are available depends on substantive ontology; a sharp transition is not logically impossible just because it is surprising. *Determinate intermediacy* is distinct from *indeterminacy*, which is not simply ignorance about a determinate hidden integer. A single real number is not automatically an adequate representation of a multidimensional or indeterminate subject situation. Their thought experiments do not show which option nature chooses.

**Conceivability and overlap, section 6.** The authors consider whether an intermediate case can be imagined as having a single, determinate “what-it-is-like” and respond that this can impose a contradictory demand, or that limitations of imagination do not entail impossibility. This is a philosophical reply, not a proof of such cases. Figure captions distinguish indeterminate/partial consciousness (Fig. 1), determinate experiences with intermediate/indeterminate unity (Fig. 2), intransitive unity (Fig. 3), and two determinately countable overlapping regions (Fig. 4). Those different cases must not be collapsed.

A finite number of sharply defined overlapping sets can be counted, even with enormous overlap. But, they argue, counting every nearly identical maximal field as a separate subject may be intuitively and pragmatically misleading; moreover, counting sharp overlapping fields does not resolve zero-to-one or genuinely indeterminate/intermediate unity. They consider sufficiently rather than all-or-none unified bundles and the possibility of no uniquely best individuation. The manuscript also notes tension between phenomenal vagueness, fundamental unity and a ban on fundamental vagueness: one cannot silently assert all three without an account of their compatibility.

**Project implication.** Plurality is a target, not permission to declare every overlapping maximal field an independent subject. The finite set of counts output by our graph procedure is *epistemic/model uncertainty under sharp assumptions*, not itself metaphysical vagueness or a fractional subject. Conversely, allowing indeterminacy is not a denial of actual experience. Strong illusionism that denies experience is ineligible under the project's criteria, whatever broader uses of “illusionism” occur in the literature.

## 3. Lopez: partial phenomenal unity is not merely shared behavior

**Model, sections 1–3.** Lopez develops a *conjoint phenomenology thesis*: some aspects of simultaneous right- and left-side experiences are experienced together, even where cross-matching fails. The more specific layered proposal distinguishes shared **global** features, such as coarse configurations or scene gist, from **local** features of individual objects, whose right–left conjunction is proposed to break down. “Global” describes content/configuration here, not the whole universe, a universal subject, or necessarily global neural workspace access. Visual tasks motivate the model; other modalities and affect or cognition are tentative extensions.

Lopez acknowledges that local non-unity can itself violate an unrestricted same-subject unity thesis. Her account is explicitly **partial**, not total phenomenal unity. She considers an inaccessible local conjunction as a rival, and separately a duplication alternative: each hemisphere could represent global features without a shared experiential token. She suggests a subcortical global layer might favor the shared interpretation, contingent on establishing the relevant mechanisms; the possibility is not eliminated. Her model allows nontransitivity: global left and right layers may be unified, each with its own local layer, even when the two local layers are not mutually unified.

**Attention argument, section 4.** The pivotal bridge is conditional: if unified attention suffices for phenomenal unity, and relevant split-brain attentional capacities remain unified, some phenomenal unity follows. The proposed unity-making relations are a *single experienced centrality system* and consciously voluntary attentional agency. Neither is automatically established by a general common resource or a behavioral shift. Alertness is characterized as largely preserved; exogenous orienting as strongly divided; top-down orienting and executive control as mixed and condition-dependent. Notes 25, 28 and 30 recognize contrary evidence or individual variation. The mapping of specific attentional capacities onto global and local experiential layers is deliberately left speculative (section 4.4). An experienced centrality system is richer than a numerical ordering of independently observed performances; the author's own comparison of two people's distinct centrality systems illustrates this point.

**Consequences and qualifications, sections 5–6.** If the model is right, complete unity need not be essential, unity can be partial, and unity need not completely depend on cortical integration. Lopez discusses conditional tension with IIT's split-stream prediction, but explicitly considers that IIT's integration axiom as irreducibility might remain compatible with some local disunity. This is not an established falsification of IIT, or proof of a particular number of subjects. Evidence of task performance, arousal, shared processing resources, and conscious phenomenal conjunction are distinct evidential levels. Lopez's article is an interpretive synthesis, not new patient-level experimental data.

## 4. A common comparison without concealing the hard step

For a single specified window, distinguish (i) phenomenal aspects and their token identity, (ii) which sets are actually jointly experienced, (iii) their ownership or perspective, (iv) behavioral access/report, and (v) the physical implementation. These are not interchangeable. Compare three experience-respecting possibilities:

| Candidate | A left-local, global, right-local pattern | What is left open |
|---|---|---|
| Strong same-subject subsumption | If all three are states of one subject, the stronger closure thesis demands an encompassing state. | Whether one subject owns them, and whether an inaccessible local conjunction exists. |
| Partial/layered unity | Global relations can join aspects while local left and right lack a conjunction; unity need not be transitive. | Whether the global experience is genuinely shared, and how subjects are individuated. |
| Separate/duplicated fields | Similar or coordinated global content may occur in distinct fields with distinct tokens. | Whether observed coordination is duplicated, transferred, shared, or otherwise grounded. |

As a **toy** example only, take phenomenally stipulated fields `{L,G}` and `{G,R}`, but not `{L,R}` or `{L,G,R}`. The *same token* `G` is shared, so existential pairwise unity is nontransitive. Replace it by qualitatively similar distinct tokens `G_L` and `G_R` and fields `{L,G_L}` and `{G_R,R}`: there is no shared token. Both stipulations might be compatible with the same coarse external reports if those reports only encode the appearance of `G`, rather than its token identity. This conditional illustration does not show that either arrangement is physically realized or that its fields equal subjects. In particular, treating `{L,G}` and `{G,R}` as separate subjects is an additional individuation rule, not a consequence of the diagram; counting a single global layer as an extra subject is also an additional rule.

These examples are not countermodels to Bayne–Chalmers' full subject-indexed closure unless their subject and subsumption premises are separately imposed. They do demonstrate why a model must state the **quantifier and identity** conditions: `for each pair, some witness` is different from `one subject and its subsuming states witness all pairs`. Nor does a path of overlapping pair fields alone imply a jointly experienced whole. On the other hand, a partial-unity theorist may coherently propose a different, explicitly justified subject concept; no verdict follows merely from the graph.

A follow-up [shared-token versus duplication gate](SHARED_EXPERIENCE_VERSUS_DUPLICATED_TOKENS_GATE.md) makes this comparison precise: certain adaptive report histories can be identical under both models, while a separately calibrated disagreement test can discriminate particular strengthened packages. Neither the calibration nor the necessary intervention has been established. A separate [finite relational constitution model](RELATIONAL_WHOLE_SUBJECT_FACET_CONSTITUTION.md) explicitly distinguishes Bayne–Chalmers same-subject closure from globally nontransitive existential co-occurrence and exposes the extra assumptions required to count subjects.

## 5. Concrete next requirements for the framework

1. **Specify the experiential primitive and decomposition.** State whether local/global are parts, aspects, representational contents or relational descriptions, including what makes two tokens identical rather than qualitatively alike. Explain how this connects to the experiential ground without assuming its answer.
2. **State the subject rule independently of the test.** Distinguish subject-indexed subsumption from existential co-incidence, maximal field count, agency and degrees of unity. Specify whether overlap, partial or indeterminate cases are permitted and whether plurality requires numerically distinct subjects, not just multiple descriptions.
3. **Bridge first-person evidence carefully.** In addition to reports of individual items, predefine phenomenal conjunction, centrality and local versus global content at matching time resolution. Test attention and report limitations, cross-cueing, duplicate representation, fixation and temporal switching. A performance effect is not by itself a phenomenal certificate; independent calibration for potentially inaccessible perspectives remains an open obstacle.
4. **Compare rivals on the same physical and experiential domain.** Prespecify what observations would challenge each model, including whether they target shared token identity or merely shared content, and keep error, model and sampling assumptions separate. Do not call the toy construction or past probability bounds experimental confirmation.
5. **Resolve the remaining foundational obligations.** Explain plural perspective constitution, genuine possibilities as actual within the timeless whole, and structural–experiential correspondence without an external creator, universal subject, temporal actualization mechanism or actual simulation premise. A substrate-independent formulation must identify which transformations preserve the experiential facts; relabeling alone is insufficient. These readings expose constraints; they do not complete the framework or establish C3.
