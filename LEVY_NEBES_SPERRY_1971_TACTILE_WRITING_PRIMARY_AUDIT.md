# Tactile words, hidden writing and feedback: the 1971 primary study

## Result and correction

We located an accessible [archival copy](https://people.uncw.edu/puente/sperry/sperrypapers/70s/162-1971.pdf) of Levy, Nebes and Sperry's 1971 study, *Expressive Language in the Surgically Separated Minor Hemisphere*. Its main text sharpens and **corrects** the previous source summary: L.B. completed two different tactile tasks, which the earlier [indirect-communication audit](SUBJECT_INCIDENCE_INDIRECT_COMMUNICATION_AND_NESTING_AUDIT.md) ran together.

1. In the **letter-arrangement task**, L.B. felt two or three separate letters behind a screen, arranged all six tested sets correctly, and was unable to name any of the six words after arranging them.
2. In the **preformed-word task**, the letters were already arranged into a word. He was asked to name and/or write it with his hidden left hand. The authors say no correct spoken identification occurred before writing; for the particular word `PET`, they report that he could not first name it, then printed it, and immediately afterward named it.

The important distinction is **arrangement versus writing**, not a contradiction in the participant's responses. Schechter herself describes arbitrarily ordered letters before discussing the preformed-word writing table, and one sentence says L.B. had “viewed his own written response”; that wording conflicts with her description of the screen and the original authors' hidden-hand procedure. It should not be used to infer visual feedback. Our earlier audit repeated the task conflation, though it did not assume visual access. The authors attributed the successful later vocalizations to bilateral kinesthetic feedback and report a control in which an examiner moved his passive left hand through similar words and numbers and he could generally verbalize them. That control supports the plausibility of a feedback route; it does not isolate that route's necessity or establish separate simultaneous experiencers. The tasks were sequential and involved a single patient, not the local/global trial in P or C-layered.

## 1. Source, access and reading scope

Jerre Levy, Robert D. Nebes and Roger W. Sperry, [*Cortex* 7(1), 49–58 (1971)](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0010-9452(71)80021-7). A [Sperry publications index](https://people.uncw.edu/puente/sperry/sperrypapers/) lists this PDF as item 162. After the [fixed retrospective plan](LEVY_NEBES_SPERRY_1971_FEEDBACK_PRIMARY_READING_PLAN.md), all three chunks of its extracted text were read, from title through references, including authors' case descriptions, methods, discussion and tables. The publisher's [article page](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0010945271800217) confirms title, authors, publication metadata and summary; its full PDF fetch previously failed.

**Extraction limits matter.** The archival PDF is a scan. The retrieval tool returned imperfect OCR with page-order/column interleaving and a misaligned Table II—for example, the extracted row for `PET` conflicts with the authors' explicit narrative about that trial. The original page images and table layout could not be visually inspected in this environment. We therefore rely on legible prose for the `PET` example, on the prose and simple extracted Table I for the six arrangement items, and on Schechter's independently readable reproduction for her description of Table II; we do **not** reconstruct the complete Table II row by row or claim precise counts from its malformed OCR. Figure images were not inspected. The two textual sources have not been checked against a visually rendered original table. Underlying clinical charts, raw observations and data are unavailable. This is a full extracted-text reading **with these table and image limitations**, not a complete visual audit.

## 2. Who was tested and why?

The authors report that both participants had commissurotomies to treat severe epilepsy. L.B. was **17 at testing**, five years after surgery; the article says the preoperative cause of epilepsy was unknown and no neurological evidence of brain damage was found. A.A. had surgery at **14** and a different history including early injury indications, left-hemisphere EEG abnormalities, a right-hand sensory deficit and postoperative right-hemisphere edema. These are the authors' descriptions, not independent confirmation of anatomy, exact extent of disconnection or residual pathways.

The investigators selected these two patients because they judged their minor hemispheres relatively well preserved for the language question. Thus this is neither a random sample of patients nor an estimate of what occurs across all callosotomies. L.B. and A.A. were not interchangeable. In particular, the authors explicitly state that A.A.'s **left-hand tactile information was not confined to the right hemisphere**, in their interpretation because of compensation after early cortical injury. Consequently they tested A.A. primarily with visually lateralized stimuli rather than applying L.B.'s tactile protocol to him. This reported ipsilateral exception is a direct warning against treating every left-hand signal as a uniquely right-hemisphere input.

The prior [DDC and DDV evidence](SPLIT_BRAIN_PRIVATE_SUBJECT_IDENTITY_EVIDENCE_AUDIT.md) concerns different patients, decades later, in visual tasks. Their results are important but do not amend L.B.'s observed sequence by pooling patients, or settle his physiology. Neither set establishes a clinical-to-spin mapping.

## 3. L.B.: three separate tasks

### A. Arranging separate letters

Two or three plastic letters were put into the hidden left hand. The experimenter had demonstrated the task in free vision; L.B. was instructed to arrange each set into a word. Table I lists six sets; the authors report **six correct arrangements out of six**, with their stated `p < .001`. They then asked him to name each arranged word, and report that he could not say any of them. The report does not establish the sampling model behind that p-value, an independently reproduced test, or his subjective experience of each item. The letter-arrangement success shows a dissociation between manual performance and requested spoken naming in that setting; it does not imply a nonexistent verbal attempt was a negative experience.

### B. Writing a preformed tactile word

Here the plastic letters were **already formed into words**, not scrambled sets to be rearranged. L.B. felt a word and was asked either to name it or write it with a pencil in his hidden left hand. Some trials asked for writing and then naming; others asked for naming and then writing. The authors report no instance of correct vocal designation **before writing**. Their prose specifically identifies the sequence for `PET`: initial failure to say the word, correct hidden printing, then immediate correct speech. This is an informative within-trial temporal sequence, but it is one example, not an estimate of a causal treatment effect. An unrequested response is not a miss. The malformed OCR prevents a reliable quantitative comparison of all table cells here.

The report proposes that active writing produces **bilateral kinesthetic information**, whereas tactile letter recognition involved circular exploration or discrete palpation rather than hand movements tracing letter shapes. That is the authors' interpretation of the informational difference between tasks A and B. It is not demonstrated by the mere fact that both use a hand, or by the elapsed time alone.

### C. Writing the names of objects

With household objects placed in his hidden left hand, L.B. was asked to write their names. The authors say he consistently began with only one or two correct letters, then stopped or added incorrect ones. They describe a pipe trial: he wrote `PI` with slow, firm movements, then changed grip and completed `Pencil`. They interpret such switches as interference from the language-dominant hemisphere, while also proposing limitations in the other hemisphere's linguistic abilities. We have read that description, not visually verified the figure or the actual motor-control assignment.

### The feedback check

The authors say an examiner moved L.B.'s **passive** left hand through similar words and numbers, which he could generally verbalize. This supports the existence of an available route from movement to verbal identification without requiring him to choose the writing himself. But the paper's qualitative description, as extracted, does not give the control's full trial list, denominator, assignment or error rate. It does not show a randomized comparison of the *same preformed-word trials* with writing feedback intact versus selectively blocked while all other capacities were preserved. We cannot compute the route's necessity, exclusivity, sensitivity or subject-specific error bounds from “generally.”

## 4. A.A.: an informative different case

A.A. saw previously introduced stimuli flashed to a visual field, then wrote with his left hand out of sight and verbally identified the stimulus after writing. The article reports that of 39 left-field presentations of printed nouns, he wrote 12 correctly; on 10 of those occasions he then either could not name or misnamed the word just written. It reports 16 correct written responses from 24 right-field noun presentations, all subsequently vocalized correctly. These are author-reported counts for these tasks, not a replication of L.B.'s tactile `PET` sequence. The report also describes verb and picture tests, including **two** correct-written/unrecognized left-field picture cases in 54 presentations; specific interpretations of hemispheric control are the authors'.

A.A.'s results show why one must not assume that a hidden left-hand written answer always supplies usable verbal feedback. His special sensory history and different visual stimuli and tasks prevent a simple patient-to-patient comparison of rates. No detailed anatomical reconstruction or modern statistical reanalysis was performed. The article's use of “minor” and “major” hemisphere reflects its historical and task-specific language; it should not be generalized as a judgment of a patient's value or overall abilities.

## 5. What the feedback data do—and do not—identify

Let the outcome of interest be correct spoken identification of the tactile word in a specified time window. A route hypothesis says that hidden writing produces informative movement, sensory registration and eventually a successful spoken response. The `PET` episode and passive-movement check both support that as a concrete possible explanation. They are more informative than a story invented merely to explain coordinated output.

But comparing speech **before** and **after** writing changes several things at once: elapsed time, an additional prompt, motor planning and movement, sensory feedback and possibly memory, attention or strategy. The patient cannot simultaneously receive and not receive the same prior writing event. A later correct answer is not necessarily caused exclusively by feedback. The passive movement control shows that the route can carry information under a different procedure; it does not show that it carried the relevant information on each preformed-word trial or exclude internal transfer and other signals. Conversely, noting these limitations is not evidence that the authors' proposed route was absent.

The conclusion is an **operational constraint with a plausible mechanism**, not proof of two subjects. Correct writing could reflect perception, cognition or motor processes; the source is not a calibrated trial-level first-person report of exactly what was experienced. Failure to speak can reflect limitations of access, instruction or output rather than absence of perception or experience. Likewise, the paper's proposed right/left mental organization remains an interpretation of this patient's responses, not independently observed subject ownership.

A stronger prospective process test would specify an actual target route and compare, where feasible and safe, writing or matched movement under conditions with meaningfully different feedback, while independently checking sensory delivery, motor ability, timing, attention, cue leakage and task comprehension. Active versus passive movement, visual versus hidden conditions and delay-only controls could test **different** parts of the mechanism; none is automatically a clean intervention that preserves the same experience. Randomization, missingness handling, patient-specific uncertainty and clinical ethics are essential. No such test has been performed or promised here.

## 6. Consequences for Schechter and the P/C-layered comparison

Schechter's reconstructed argument distinguishes candidate thinkers, indirect psychological interaction and a substantive individuation principle. The 1971 result strengthens the evidence base for one of the examples used to **motivate a possible indirect sensory route**. It does not establish that *all* relevant psychological interactions of L.B.'s putative R and L are indirect, nor does it independently prove that R and L are two subjects. Nor is indirectness a universal rule simply because some of our own mental states can interact directly.

For the project's common-sector completions, P retains two local subjects plus a distinct global subject, with **numerically private** local/global experiential tokens and shared content types; C-layered retains one subject with partially disunified local detail and a coarse global conjunction. P asserts `(D,G,N)=(0,1,1)` and C-layered `(0,1,0)` for their stated target episode. The 1971 study reports neither a same-window local-detail and global-coarse conjunction nor distinct subject owners. One left-hand writing response followed by speech is not P's local-versus-global incidence, and treating the global subject as the speaking hemisphere would merely assume the missing mapping.

The experimental support is physical and psychological evidence at a **left/right task interface**, not a measurement of the four-spin L selector, an IIT score or an experiential valence law. Physical overlap does not decide whether phenomenal tokens are shared, separate or aspects of one subject. A justified `N=1` would conditionally challenge the closed C-layered package; a justified population-level `N=0` would challenge P, but a single verbal failure or unobserved reporter is not that negative finding. This source alone establishes neither.

The immediate research gain is therefore not that the debate is solved: the earlier report's conflation has been corrected, and the stronger feedback inference can now be assessed against an actual primary protocol. C3, B4–B5/F2, B9/F3 and B10 remain open. The unrelated PAINgen 21 mean discrepancies still require the actual conversion/export code. No clinical contact, new experiment, or subject census was conducted.
